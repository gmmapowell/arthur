14. Pictland

AD498

Arthur now had his "invincible" army.

Nobody, of course, was ever invincible, but he had patched together all the cavalry resistance units, a number of Saxon units from the various Saxon lands that he was trying to integrate into proper Roman formations, a number of men from the conscription of two years' before who preferred life under arms to returning to the subsistence existence of peasants and a handful of garrison troops who were, for the time being at least, surplus to the requirements of the more peaceful times.

He had also incorporated the remnants of the "Great Army of the North" under its Dux in York. What had amounted to four front-line regions a little over a century ago was now more accurately described as ten under-strength cohorts. Still, it was as big as the rest of his forces put together. For many years, they had remained static on the northern frontier; unable to evict the Saxons but - with the single exception of the Pict invasion of AD478 - easily able to defend their territory north of the Humber from Saxon and Pict alike.

Arthur had appointed Dyfnwal commander of this motley army and sent him - together with his mobile forces - north to drill with the experienced and largely hereditary centurions of the Dux's forces. By the end of his year as consul, this had become a single fighting force and the strongest - although not the largest - army to be seen in Britain since Maximillian had led three of the legions astray in his march to become emperor of Rome.

What this army had that all the others had not was an integration of forces and a true, disciplined, professional nature. Every man is this army wanted to be there, wanted to fight, and was ready to be disciplined and train. The army had experienced legionaries from York, mobile cavalry forces from the south, and shock troops and a navy provided by the Saxons.

And it had a leader - Dyfnwal.

Dyfnwal had served many years with Arthur and had come to understand his multifaceted tactical approach. While he would be the first to admit that he did not have the tactical genius of the master, he had the same intellectual approach to understanding the fighting of wars. His first task was to secure his supplies and supply lines, and to ensure that his troops were able to eat and be ready for battle. His second was to make sure that every man was thoroughly drilled and disciplined, and knew his role. Only then did he start to think about the campaigns and the battles.

And for this campaign, he had Arthur to do all that for him.

"Right, men," said Arthur to the officers and centurions when they gathered in late March before the campaigning season. "I want there to be absolutely no doubt about this. Dyfnwal is in charge and will remain in charge - unless he's killed or incapacitated. My reputation proceeds me - especially since many of you have served alongside me in one capacity or another over the past twenty years. I may be the second coming of Julius Caesar - although I doubt it myself - but you take your orders for Dyfnwal and nobody else. On the other hand, since I'm here, it makes sense for me to offer my advice and perspective on the situation."

The officers laughed. In other words, constitutionally, Arthur would observe the forms; and in the field he would let Dyfnwal give the orders, but the campaign strategy was coming from just one man.

"You know the situation. Everything's a mess. We have peace in the south and east because both Britons and Saxons have decided to work together. Up here, we have issues with both the Picts and the governors that were appointed to deal with them. And all over the west we have old-fashioned Irish invaders. So.

"We need to move quickly and sweep them all away. My old comrade Fergus - yes, the Irish Fergus - has been having some issues with his neighbours over the sea, and I've promised him land between the walls of Hadrian and Antonine if he wants to - uh - come and claim it. He has agreed, and will be sailing up the Clyde. I have sent messengers to our friends there, and they will let him pass as long as we vouch for his good behaviour, which I've done.

"We meanwhile, are going to push up the east coast and take the old fort at Dunedin from the Votadini. We will bring them to pitched battle and crush them, and then we will displace them. Dyfnwal will remain there as the new governor and take responsibility for properly organizing the territory, carrying out a census and deciding on taxation, and I will be looking for about a thousand volunteers who want to stay with him and be the regular standing army and police force up there. Fergus will push up from the west and do the same thing on that side, displacing all the petty governors who think themselves above the law. Fergus will then be Dyfnwal's deputy, each given a ten-year pro-consular imperium.

"Any questions? Good."

And he walked out. The grand strategy was in place; Dyfnwal and his officers could work through the details. Arthur would have little to do until the time came to bring the Votadini to battle.

The Votadini had been sent to the border by Vortigern in an attempt to shore it up. Supplied from the south through the supply chains based at York, the arrangement had worked well until the Great Rebellion had caused the central government to collapse and the funnelling of supplies could no longer be arranged.

The Votadini had then followed the example of the Saxons and "gone native" - looking after themselves rather than after the common weal of Britain. Arthur had not fought them when he had previously come north, because at the time their interests were aligned - the Votadini had been roundly beaten by the Picts as they broke through their territory on their way south to ravage York.

Arthur had been tempted to try and do a deal with the Votadini as he had with the Saxons - to forgive and forget. But he felt he had already forgiven too many people too many times since becoming Consul and sooner or later he needed to make an example of someone. He had to fight them first; and make them concede. Then maybe it would be possible to do a deal with a weakened enemy.

But that deal did not include letting them keep their power here. Arthur knew who he wanted in charge of this critical area, and that was Dyfnwal and Fergus. When he left, they were the men he would leave behind.

The army rode north in true Roman style. They did not need to build marching camps south of Hadrian's wall, for everywhere they went had been Roman - nominally at least - for centuries and proper forts were available all along the Great North Road.

In these days, of course, the forts were not used to accomodating an army of this size. But that didn't mean they weren't still capable of it - and in general they found themselves in camps that were designed for even bigger armies. And, more often than not, they found themselves re-fortifying the camp before settling in for the night. But on the whole, it was easier than marching into the unknown.

Which they did after crossing Hadrian's wall.

The traditional frontier of Britain to the north, it had been originally envisaged by Hadrianus as a temporary frontier to hold back the Picts until they "realized" the benefits of civilization. A couple of times the British and Romans had pushed up passed this line - and Antoninus had even built a more advanced fortification, the Antonine wall - but each time they had been pushed back here. The distances to the north were simply too vast.

Vortigern - among others - had attempted to defuse the problem by introducing a buffer zone occupied by "friendly" barbarians, given the right of self-government and general independence north of the wall, in exchange for being in a state of constant war with the barbarians to the north.

The most recent of these were the Votadini, who had established themselves at a place colloquially known as "The Rock" - often written as Dunedin. A natural fortress, it was generally considered impossible to capture, but Arthur knew better.

It was a perfect defensive position against barbarians, and, with the appropriate care and attention it was possible to defend an area of many hundreds of square miles from the fortification. And the Votadini - with just the one exception - had managed to hold it for all that time.

Now Arthur purposed to take it from them and install Dyfnwal instead.

They dug in two miles to the south of the fort. They built a full size, properly Roman winter fort: not just a marching camp or a battle camp. To any who knew the signs, this meant they planned to stay as long as it took. Arthur hoped that someone on The Rock knew the signs.

"That's one big hill," remarked Dyfnwal as he, Arthur and the majority of the command staff rode out to look at it. "I wouldn't like to have to storm that."

"It must be over a hundred feet up to the top," agreed Arthur cheerfully. "Even our catapults are going to have a problem getting missiles up there with any force."

"So we're stuck with a siege, hence the big camp," said Dyfnwal.

"Well, I wouldn't like to rule it out," conceded Arthur. "But where there's a will, there's a way. And I have the will to take this place."

"Treachery?" asked Quintus.

"I certainly wouldn't turn anyone down who came to me with the right offer," agreed Arthur. "If you have any contacts inside the fort, let them know I would be prepared to consider a deal. But there are definitely limits: the Votadini are not going to be in charge here by this time next year; and that's not open to negotiation."

"Stealth?" suggested Dyfnwal. "It seems as if the hill slopes off more gently to the east and there are other lesser hills there we could use to stage a small party."

"It's possible," admitted Arthur warily. "But I assume that they have fortified that hill too, against that possibility. You'd probably just lose all the men you sent - and admit you didn't know how to take the place."

"So what is your plan?" asked Quintus, once again in his life irritated by Arthur's apparent nonchalance in the face of the seemingly impossible.

"My usual one," he replied with a smirk. "I intend to fight them down here in the plain on my own terms and defeat them."

"How?" the others asked, almost in unison.

"Make it seem like their best option."

The digging began in earnest the next day.

"It's only four miles," said Arthur. "And there's plenty of timber. A circumvallation like this would take a real army just four days. Caesar claims he managed to get this kind of job done overnight, but even though he had more men, I still don't see how he managed it.

"You understand the plan? The key is to allow the line to bulge here. This is the logical place for them to come down and attack us, and it needs to look good. But this bulge will drive them apart and give us the line to attack."

"But why will they come down?" asked Quintus.

"Fear," replied Arthur. "They have planned and prepared for an attack by barbarians and haven't laid in the kinds of resources that they need to deal with us. And we outnumber them. Meanwhile, we have declared that we are here to stay and they will see their only option to come down and fight their way out before we complete the circumvallation.

"Which is why this part - where we want them to attack - must still be incomplete when they come down."

At dawn, two days later, the Votadini struck.

In theory, they were a Roman-trained force. In practice, they were a violent rabble who understood the benefits of organization but not the benefits of daily, dull drill.

They came down the hill in good order. Arthur's bugler immediately sounded the call to drop tools and arm. Within minutes, Arthur's units had formed up in the two remaining gaps in the southern arm of the wall - gaps that were exactly the size of the hundred-man front Arthur wanted to deploy. In front of them, many of the trenches and hurdles had already been dug into no-man's land. Arthur's forces had not had time to "rehearse" this battle, as they so often had in the past, but they knew what they were doing and had practised the appropriate manoeuvres a thousand times over the past year.

Dyfnwal was commanding the forces to the left; Quintus to the right. Arthur held the cavalry and the reserves behind the main lines but in front of the fort in the centre of the field.

The Votadini worked their way around most of the obstacles and came on at a rush. Dyfnwal's men met the rush and their line wobbled but held. The Roman killing machine went to work: open-stab-close-shove, open-stab-close-shove.

On the right, Quintus' line gave way from the moment the contact was made. The first wobble became a wave and almost allowed some of the descending attackers to break through. But Quintus called for order and retreat and the line moved back a few paces. Then a few more.

Quintus called out the commands and tried to steady the line but nothing he tried worked. Slowly but surely, they were being pushed back down the hill and losing the battle.

Arthur saw what was happening but did not immediately react. The Votadini were still pushing through the gap in the defences and they were still covering their flanks. But he watched carefully for the right moment.

Dyfnwal's line was holding and there was noise and clamour all along the front. The Votadini shield wall was up against the Britons' and the two lines were shoving against each other and stabbing when they could. But battles between Roman-trained armies invariably came down to a wrestling match: the defence of the wall of shields was more than a match for the thrusting of swords or the occasional stabbing thrust of a spear coming down from above. From time to time a strike would go home and sometimes a man would be forced to retreat.

Quintus' line continued to retreat as Arthur watched. The attackers had pushed through the gap in the encircling wall now, and had continued in formation, leaving their flank unprotected. As they pushed on, a steady gap opened up behind them ... two paces, three, now four. Arthur watched carefully, then, deciding the moment had come, set his horse off on a trot and signalled his personal cavalry to follow him. The others should know what to do when the time came.

Arthur rode straight for the gap in the wall. That was the key to this whole battle. From there, he could encircle both groups of enemy soldiers and, once there, nothing they could do would dislodge him.

As they reached the distance of fifty paces from the flank of the ongoing battle, Arthur signalled the charge and spurred his horse into a gallop. The noise of the pounding hooves transmitted itself through the floor to the soldiers, but there was no time to react. The troops on the outer flanks turned, and tried to lock shields, but they were not Arthur's target: he rode around behind them and attacked the Votadini in the rear. Here too, the defenders tried to turn and lock shields, but they were too slow; the move too unexpected.

All Arthur's reinforcement companies came up as fast as they could. Within minutes, the battle on the right was over as the Votadini lines crumpled under attack from all sides. More, as reinforcements arrived, Arthur was able to lead his original storm company to attack the units pressuring Dyfnwal. While he was unable to repeat the element of surprise, the early offer of quarter - as opposed to the alternative, almost certain death - brought the battle to a quick close. Once more, Arthur was victor on the field. The rest of this campaign would be mopping up. Arthur was happy to delegate that to Dyfnwal and Fergus.

Cunedda came to the tribunal somewhat apprehensive.

He had been the leader of the forces facing Quintus, and had to face the double ignominy of having nearly beaten one of Arthur's generals, while at the same time recognizing that Arthur's plan had always been to allow him to advance precisely so that he could encircle him.

Arthur was seated there, in all the dignity of an emperor, although he had not claimed the formal title. He recognized that more than once, in the ancient Roman Republican tradition he had been hailed as "Imperator" - victor without peer - on the battlefield, but had never accepted the true validity of the role of Emperor, let alone "Emperor of Britain".

But as he stood before him at the tribunal, Cunedda felt the awe and the majesty that required words like "Emperor" and "King" to describe the people that radiated them in this way.

"Cunedda," began Arthur. "You fought valiantly yesterday, and could possibly have won had your troops been alert and disciplined. That they were not might be held at your account, or it might be held at that of your senior officer. Either way, I expect that you will learn from this salutary experience."

Arthur looked at him intently.

"By now, I'm sure that you have heard of my plans and dispositions for the north. Dyfnwal will be in overall control, and Fergus the Scot from Ireland has come to take the battle further north and make continual war against the Picts. I am sure that in due course the Scots will push the Picts out: they are just too vicious not to.

"You, I hear, are a good man, and many of your forces are good men, too. But they are no longer welcome in the north. But I ask whether you would accept the promotion to Dux and take on the task of defending the central western highlands?

"I would have you take with you such men as can be trusted and are still young and fit. Those that you do not trust - or are not suited to the discipline of military life can remain here and I will discharge them from the army and they can become civilians again under the rule of Dyfnwal.

"What do you say?"

Cunedda looked at him, new hope being born in his eyes. "I would like that very much. Will there be war?"

"I hope not," replied Arthur. "At least, not for you. I am hiring you as an administrator and a protector; not as a general. Where you are going there are no enemies at the moment, although some may come your way as we evict them from further south, in Demetia. I would have you always ready to defend your territory against any that would cause harm to the people there."

"I accept," said Cunedda, and became Dux on the field of battle outside Dunedin.
