Kent

AD442

Vortigern's decision to cede Kent to the Saxons might have been pragmatic. It might have saved the rest of Britain from having to fight, but it was not popular. It was, understandably, not popular with the citizens of Kent, who did not want to be under the control of marauding barbarians. Neither was it popular with that faction of the Senate that had wanted the Saxons out of Britain: now they had control of a much larger part of it; and more than that, officially sanctioned control, whatever the Senate might say.

And the Saxon territory was uncomfortably close. The centre of British power lay in London, on the north side of the Thames River. Across that river lay the grand structure of London Bridge, with its stone arches, reaching across to the south side. From there, a few miles downstream, or a few more inland, brought you to the borders of the now-Saxon "Kingdom of Kent".

Perhaps more suprisingly, Vortigern's son, "little overlord" Vortimer, was also unhappy with his father's actions. As a child, he had believed that everything his father had done had been right and just. As a youth, he had chosen to do military service with the army in the north, and had learned just how feared - and fearsome - their Saxon allies were, both north of the border - and at home.

While he abstractly accepted his father's position that the Saxons were needed for the defence of Britain - and that, even if they were no longer needed could not be evicted at this point - he was concerned that there official "Foederati" status was now being overlooked and turning into more of a "domini" status. There was a big difference between billeting and feeding an army of Saxon allies in a few places and accepting their overlordship across much of the southeast of Britain. Where would it end?

He raised some of these points with his father, who remained unimpressed. The naivete of youth, he thought - and said. A row followed which involved many things being said that could not be unsaid and young Vortimer left home and went and found common cause with a nascent rebel army in Kent.

The battles raged across Kent for most of the next decade.

While Hengest felt that he was now the King of Kent, most of his forces were still based on the island of Thanet, off the coast. Until he could redeploy his forces, he was unable to enforce anything more than nominal authority over his nominal kingdom.

While a great warrior, and a not-indifferent leader of men, Hengest lacked the wide-area strategic awareness that came naturally to educated Romans. He largely assumed that having been granted the land by Vortigern - and having won a decisive battle at Guollopum - there would be little or no resistance to his moving troops off Thanet Island and onto the mainland.

He counted without Vortimer.

Vortimer was determined that no Saxons would take over Kent and gathered together a scratch force from across the south east of Britain. He gathered these forces and marched to oppose a small band of Hengest's men who were planning on establishing themselves at Canterbury, athwart the main road from Rutupiae to London and thus able to control access to most of the region.

The forces met at Crayford, and fought long and hard in battle lines across the river. A running battle that lasted three days, as neither side could fully cross the river to the other side and engage in full combat, it ended when the Saxons left the ford at night and, crossing the Darenth, circled behind the British. Caught between the British and the river on the final day of the battle, Vortimer's brother Categirn died leading the right wing. In the centre of the line, Horsa, the leader of the Saxon mission to Canterbury died trying to break the British line.

He died, but not in vain. A few minutes later the Saxons broke through and drove the British screaming and scrambling across the river. For the next two days, they were chased back to London where they took refuge inside the city walls.

Hengest bitterly regretted the loss of his brother and for two years failed to follow up this victory with any further action; but then once again sent men to occupy Canterbury. On this occasion they were able to enter the city unopposed and held it for the next two years.

Once again, Vortimer acted to evict them. Gathering more troops from both the south east and across the west country, he marshalled and disciplined his army almost five cohorts strong, and led it into battle in Kent. He laid siege to Canterbury and eventually made the commander there admit defeat and once again retreated to Thanet Island.

A couple more years passed and Hengest, returning to his roots, raided across Kent, all the while claiming that he was "acting legally" in collecting the taxes and tribute that was owed to him, largely avoided Vortimer's forces which were neither full-time, professional soldiers nor able to be everywhere at once.

Indecisive engagements were fought on a number of occasions and the Britons took some advantage from their superiority in cavalry; but in general the advantage went to the Saxons who, from their base of safety on Thanet Island sailed across to the mainland when they chose, landed in unexpected locations, raided inland, only to escape before the British could mobilize.

By 452, Vortimer had had enough.

He called a meeting of his advisors in the thick snows of February and met at Rochester.

"It's true that they come and go where they want, like the wind," he said. "But they have no horses, so we can go faster."

The others acknowledged the truth of this, but did not see how it benefitted them. The number of cavalry they had could not beat the quanity and quality of Saxon raiders, and they could not mobilize the infantry in that amount of time, even if they could deploy the cavalry.

"Unless we can delay them for a while," said Vortimer.

"How?"

"How do they come?" asked Vortimer, either rhetorically or redundantly.

"By ship."

"And how do they leave?"

"By ship."

Vortimer let the pause develop for a few seconds while they all shared in the same realization he had had over the winter.

"But only if their ships are still there," said Ambrosius Jr, one of Vortimer's "loyal rebels".

"Exactly. If they return to find their ships burned to the waterline, what will they do? What can they do?"

"Turn and fight like the cornered rats they are."

"And we will be there with some cavalry to harass them, and meanwhile our infantry will be mobilizing and coming across the countryside. They should be there in three or four days. With some horsemen to run messages, we should be able to bring them to battle somewhere."

They all agreed this was an excellent, workable plan.

"But how will we be able to find their ships, let alone destroy them?" Ambrosius, ever practical, asked.

"This year must be the year of total commitment," stated Vortimer. "We must all apply ourselves utterly to the task. If some crops go ungrown, so be it. If we can win this battle, we will be able to ask for help from others in the island. And if we lose, we would not have use for the food anyway.

"We must round up and dedicate all our cavalry soldiers all year. The first task is to station lookouts all along the coast. As soon as any lookout sees the Saxons coming, they must immediately ride to alert the other lookouts, gather the cavalry and mobilize the infantry. We can use the same beacons that have been used in the north. Then, when everyone is alerted, the cavalry can converge from all directions at the landing site.

"The cavalry will be responsible for destroying the fleet by any means possible, and, if necessary, holding the enemy at bay. We must then somehow shepherd the Saxons onto our infantry for the final engagement."

The Saxons managed to get three or four raids out and home again before the British were able to put their plan into full effect. But in late June, the Saxons attempted to take advantage of the long days and short nights to land on the south coast and raid deep inland. Their ships were spotted when still offshore in the early morning light, and the signal beacons were lit.

Still assuming that the purpose of these beacons was early warning and defence, and not a rallying cry for the defenders, the Saxons came ashore as they had planned and began to raid. By midday, behind the bluffs just inland from the coast, over a hundred horsemen had arrived and decided that they were sufficient for the task. The Saxons had conveniently beached their ships - all the better to offload their warriors and then load them and their plunder back again when the tide started to rise that evening - and left just forty men to guard them against any local threats.

Vortimer was not present in person for this battle; he was mustering and mobilizing units of the infantry and trying to figure out where to bring the Saxons to battle. But Ambrosius Jr had turned up just as the final decision was being made as to whether to attack now or to wait. His voice was the one that pushed the men across the line in their decision to attack.

The key, of course, was to destroy the ships; and the easiest way to do that was to burn them. But in order to do that, they would need to get to them, and that involved dealing with the men without allowing them to escape.

Ambrosius quickly formed up his forces. Ensuring there was an adequate supply of fire and timber, he deputed twenty men to be his "burners" - four for each of the five ships, four brands per man - who were responsible for setting fire to the ships and making sure they burnt.

Twenty more were designated as fighting as cavalry. From horseback, they would be responsible for trying to encircle the enemy, harass them, and deal with any that tried to escape.

The remaining sixty were to form four ranks of fifteen. It wasn't a long line, but he figured that the Saxons - with only forty men - would struggle to match it, particularly as trying to defend the ships would limit their range of maneouvre and they had to deal with their lack of cavalry.

The battle was a complete success. Within two hours, all the Saxons were dead and the ships were burnt to the waterline. Ambrosius' forces had suffered ten dead themselves, whom they mourned and cremated, but it was a price worth paying for isolating five hundred Saxons on the mainland without supplies, support or a route home.

Vortimer brought them to battle six days later, hungry, tired and ragged from constant harassment from the British cavalry.

They had, reasonably enough, tried to work their way back across Kent to where they could signal for reinforcements or rescue from Hengest on Thanet Island. But Ambrosius' cavalry had continually forced them to follow the coast road until they come close to Rutupiae.

And there Vortimer struck. Assembling all the infantry he could raise in Kent, he drove into the Saxon forces outside Rutupiae, and the cavalry crashed into the shield wall time and again, reducing the Saxons to exhausted wrecks. The fighting was close and bitter, and Vortimer was fatally wounded before, finally, as dusk was approaching, the Saxons broke and ran - and were caught between the cavalry and the sea, and were slaughtered and drowned in an outpouring of rage that had building in the British since the defeat of Ambrosius Sr at Guollopum over fifteen years before. None of the Saxons lived to tell the tale.

As he lay dying, Vortimer begged his men to bury him on that coastline, facing out to sea, as a reminder to all men of the vigilance that had created this victory and the eternal vigilance and mutual defence that would be needed to keep Britain forever free.

They sent his body back to London to his father, who cremated him with all military honours. In death, all is forgiven.
