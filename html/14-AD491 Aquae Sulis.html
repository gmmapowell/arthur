7. Aquae Sulis

AD491

The winter had been bleak.

Since the grand battle at Linnius, Arthur had been engaged in a continual, low-key struggle for control of the east of the island. Always, his plan was to drive home to the Saxons the weakness of their strategic position, even if they held a numerical and man-for-man advantage.

But his long-term objective remained to draw the Saxons out into a large-scale, pitched battle. Without a decisive victory, they would continue to grow and establish their control over the lands that they occupied.

Meanwhile, he continued to have concerns about the welfare of the country as a whole. Ever since the province had become independent of the Roman Empire, it seemed that it had struggled to maintain the sense of decency and law and order in which Arthur - steeped in the writings of the classic philosophers such as Socrates, Plato, Cicero and Seneca - believed to be essential to civilized society. At war with barbarians in the field, Arthur felt himself at war with barbarism at home.

Surprisingly, his biggest ally in this "shadow war" against the inevitable slide into barbarism seemed to be the Christian Church. Still in its infancy, the latest trend of groups of devout monks setting up in "monasteries" and keeping alive the skills of reading and writing seemed to Arthur to offer hope against what felt like the crushing out of the light of hope to all that he held dear.

It saddened him that they seemed more interested in copying and preserving the lives of their "saints" than they did in keeping alive the veritable works of philosophy, history and ethics that he most cherished from his childhood education.

Interestingly, one of the main monasteries was growing up at Glastonbury under the watchful eye of his old tutor, Merula. Inasmuch as the Senate still met, it met there, and the monastery there benefitted from its patronage and doubtless from Merula's advice and influence.

Having been there to visit Merula, Arthur had agreed to indulge Merula's enthuasiasm for the project by visiting the monastery on the nearby hill and seeing the scribes at work.

"They tell me, you know," Merula confided with twinkling eyes to Arthur, "that when the Lord Jesus was buried and rose again, that the man who helped bury him fled to this spot. Seems strange does it not?"

"Was this spot even part of the Empire at the time?" asked Arthur, whose grip on Roman history was rock solid, but his grip on the fanciful tales of the Christians less so.

"No, that it was not. But if you were fleeing from the power of Rome, it seems an odd way to come, does it not? I can only imagine that he was fleeing by sea and was taken by a storm beyond the Pillars of Hercules and then ultimately up the Severn channel to pitch up here."

"I think the story more likely an invention. Many of these places - not just here, but I've heard in Gaul as well - seem to thrive on telling these stories of connections to famous people in their legends to draw in visitors and raise donations."

"You may be right, young Arthur. Although I feel you should leave the world-weary cynicism to me and concentrate on fighting your battles in the east."

"Will I ever be young in your eyes, Merula?"

"Well, in my eyes, yes. Since you will ever be my dear Artorius' young son. But I suppose that when I am gone, many others will judge you to be old. But remember, in days of old, at this time you would have been whitening up your toga and making a run for praetor, not lording it over half of Britain as Dux Bellorum."

"Oh, how I wish that were so. I feel us slipping inevitably into a mere mimicry of these barbarians we so dispose. Can we win the war without losing our civilization, Merula? Can we?"

"Much has already been lost. What do you think to salvage?"

Arthur thought. There really was only one answer to that. Faced with the magnitude of the task, he shrugged.

"I doubt I have time but, to be frank, all of it."

Merula smiled. "You were ever ambitious. I remember you as a lad, trying to read all those books ..." he tailed off. "You may be able to do it, but if not, know that what you do ... is enough. It may not hold the dark days at bay, but beyond the dark days ... I see brighter days ahead. And what you do now will inspire men through those days and will bring the light again. This I know."

Arthur gripped the old man's forearm in a military salute. "I take great comfort from that, Merula. If I am fortunate, I have another thirty years to do what I can to guard against the dark days."

"Fortune has nothing to do with it, young man. I swear by a strict regimen of healthy eating and regular bathing. And don't cut yourself short at thirty more years. Fifty is well within reach if you look after yourself."

Arthur had been distressed by many of the reports he had heard when he was in Glastonbury. Everywhere it seemed that the economy was collapsing and people were becoming more tribal and insular. What had started as a tactical policy under Voritgern of appointing regional prefects with wide-ranging discretionary powers was rapidly becoming a patchwork where anything went if you could enforce it. In a land where the rule of law was becoming more of a concept that an established fact, might was becoming right.

Arthur rode with his band of loyal retainers up to Aquae Sulis. He had heard that the duumvir here - it was a while now since city governors had been actually appointed in pairs and changed every year - had become more authoritarian in his "rule" over the town. Arthur was determined to stamp out such abuses and, if might was right, then Arthur felt he had the greatest might in the land and could hopefully enforce the rights of individuals against the abuses of overlords.

He raised his standard as he approached the city walls; let them know who was coming. Once across the river and inside the city walls, he demanded an immediate audience with the duumvir.

The man came in and greeted him. "Gaius Artorius. It has been a while."

"Indeed it has." This man had been his captain once, long ago, before he had taken on that role himself as a very young man. This man had gone on to greater things and now was the Lord - de facto even if there were no such thing de jure - of one of the major western towns of Britain, the spa town of Aquae Sulis on the River Afon.

"To what do I owe the pleasure?"

"I wouldn't rush to judgment, my old comrade," countered Arthur. "If I did, you would have six inches of steel sticking out of your back right now."

Arthur's tone had remained conversational, but such was the tenor of threat in the words that the man involuntarily took a step backwards and raised his hands in self-defence.

"What? What are you saying? What lies have people been telling about me?"

"Lies? Are they lies? Let us see."

"You know me, Artorius. Straight as an arrow and honest as the day is long."

"The days are very short, right now, <duumvir>, and people change. You were straight and honest, and once the law of Rome was the law here.

"Bring me Aemilia Tertia."

<Duumvir> blinked and went a shade paler. "How ..." he began, then corrected himself. "Who ... who is she?"

"Oh, come on, <duumvir>," countered Arthur. "Surely you can't pretend that you don't even know who she is. If the case is notorious enough to reach my ears, you really can't imagine that I'd believe that you hadn't heard about it. So that's not really a good start, is it?"

<Duumvir> gulped twice and looked around him. Then he signalled his servant and said to him, "Send for Aemilia Tertia ... and her husband."

"Oh, no need for that," said Arthur cheerfully. "I went to the effort of bringing her husband with me here. And really, you should have known that he had left your jurisdiction to take his case to the Senate in Glastonbury."

After a few minutes a short, dark-haired woman came in. She looked terrified and, when she saw Arthur and her husband with the duumvir, she burst into tears and fell to her knees, grovelling before Arthur.

"Please lord, don't hurt him. I will do whatever you say. As I promised already. You don't need to threaten me any more."

Arthur was appalled and pulled back from this wreck of a woman. Her husband had told him, but in this land it did not seem possible that a Roman citizen woman could be so treated. His face began to purple with rage.

He turned away and faced Quintus. "Arrest this man and take him for trial at Glastonbury. The man and his wife must go too as witnesses. The proper procedure must be strictly adhered to, but be in no doubt that I want the best prosecutor you can find. Let him choose his advocate, from here if he wants, but be aware that I will be carrying out a detailed investigation here and that will probably end up with many of his accomplices being arrested."

Over the following week, Arthur did what he had said and probed deeply into the administration of the area. Aquae Sulis was the principal town of most of the Afon valley, stretching north to the border with Gloucester, and south down to Glastonbury. As he dug, he found any number of injustices had been perpetrated in the name of the law and arrested over a dozen men on various charges, sending them all to stand trial in Glastonbury.

"They'll probably all avoid punishment," said Quintus, awed again by the power of the mind inside his officer's head. "Who will want to punish men like these in times like these?"

"And that is the problem," conceded Arthur. "The times are like they are, and until something changes we cannot expect things to get any better.

"What really worries me is the extent of the corruption. If it's as bad as this here, in a provincial backwater that hasn't even really come into contact with the Saxons, what is it going to be like elsewhere? In the north? In the autonomous regions? I've never really thought to look; I've been too busy trying to make sure people have been sending me the taxes we need to wonder how."

This was the aspect of his enquiry that had most worried him. The kinds of abuse of power that occurred when too much power was given over to individuals without the appropriate constraints drew notice, but were really just the pimples under the skin of a much worse disease. And that disease was that the country was basically bankrupt. The never-ending war was draining all the life from the economy and could not be supported for another dozen years.

"We need a reprieve, Quintus. In short, we need to win this war."

Quintus nodded. This was Arthur's refrain, his oft repeated homily on the state of the world and what needed to change. The only problem was, as far as Quintus knew, there was no prospect of that happening. But until it did, all Arthur's dreams were just that: dreams.

Arthur's bigger task on this occasion was to try and improve the running of the local economy. Technically, of course, it wasn't his responsibility, but then neither was arresting the people whose responsibility it was - and who were ducking that responsibility in order to enrich themselves or otherwise improve their own lives.

The problem was that the economy was going backwards. In times gone by, this town was a stage on a major trade route that led down into Dumnonia and funnelled goods across the dangerous sea crossings to Armorica, Hispania and even as far as Rome. That trade had all but ceased: a few daring merchants still came to Dumnonia for things they literally could not obtain elsewhere - tin and lead mainly - but the demand for any kind of luxury goods had disappeared along with the capital required to fund the risky adventures of sending ships all the way to Britain. With the easy routes to Gaul cut off by the Saxon occupation on this side and the Franks holding the ports on the far side, Britain was becoming ever more an island, with just a limited trade direct to Armorica, with that acting as an entrepot to the rest of the Empire, such as it still was.

Arthur heard that way out to the east, there were still riches to be had and the Emperor of the East still held sway over an indescribably large domain. But most of the west - the Gauls, Hispania and Africa - had been overrun by barbarians who at the very least had disrupted trade.

And without that trade, many of the imports upon which the economy of Britain depended also did not come. Britain had mainly been a staple producer of raw materials - corn, wool, meat, tin, lead, timber - shipped to factories in Italy or the Gauls. Inasmuch as there were local industries, they were mainly supplying arms to the Army of the Rhine - which had ceased to exist. All manner of things - from vehicles to ploughs to building materials - had been imported, and cut off from that, many of the areas of the British economy which were still functioning could not function at full capacity, forcing more men back to doing work that could otherwise have been done more efficiently.

And ever more men and resources were being pulled out to pay taxes to fund armies that went around destroying fields that could otherwise be used for growing more food. And then, to top it off, administrators like these were skimming off the top for their own personal profit and stopping the produce ending up in the right places.

"Let's go, Quintus. We've got an economy to destroy."
