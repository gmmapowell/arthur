10. Mons Badonicus

AD496

The rider was spotted coming up the hill.

Arthur had set up his temporary base at South Cadbury Hill. While he knew that Lucullus would betray what plans he knew, he also knew that Lucullus didn't know everything. And what he didn't know, he couldn't betray.

For example, Lucullus did not know that Arthur was going to station himself here. He had believed - reasonably enough - that Arthur would stay at his usual base at Badonicus until the legion that was in training there was ready to march and fight in whatever campaign occurred.

And although it must have been obvious to him, he also did not know the depth of planning Arthur had done, and the additional post riders - and post stations - he had set up.

One of the troubles with allowing the enemy to take and destroy your territory is that key infrastructure will be taken and destroyed - and that includes your messenger post. Arthur's solution to this was to build an entire backup system "off the main roads" - literally. He had had a team of dedicated riders spend months working up a network of hidden stations and hidden routes that would be largely ignored by the invading army and, as much as possible, off their main routes.

The messenger arrived at the top of the hill, slid off his horse and saluted Arthur.

"Greetings. All the reports are now in. All three columns have broken out from behind their borders and are converging on the Thames, burning everything as they go. We expect them to form up on the south side of the river at Reading three days from now."

For a professional, mobile force like Arthur's, three days was enough time to cross the country and start back. But for a force entirely composed of - disorganized - infantry, burning and looting as it went, three more days was not unreasonable.

The forces coming down from the north had already been in train over a week, and the plan would have been clear even if it had not already been in Arthur's mind. From Reading, they would probably strike west towards Aquae Sulis - another three days if they stuck to it. But Arthur's plan remained to drag them further south, to Badonicus, the battlefield he had prepared.

Already he had units in the field contesting the Saxons' right of passage through the countryside. There was a limit to what he could do, since he had to seem to oppose them in all directions, but his commanders all knew the same plan: as long as they keep coming south and west, let them come. Any bands foolish enough to become significantly separated from the main body of marchers were quickly encircled and killed. Every unit taken out now was one left to be faced at the battle.

All around him was the din of ongoing legionary training. The basic three months' training was over, but the II Legion (Artoria), which he had moved from Isca Dumnorium to Cadbury, was continuing its daily drill practices. There was being trained, and then there was being ready. Arthur wanted his legions more than ready when the day came. In his mind, he had already planned the battle and every day he ensured that his men knew how they would be expected to fight it. For that day was less than a week away now.

"Charge!"

The trumpets sounded and the horsemen rode in three columns to where the wall of shields was rapidly forming. Over the past two days, the cavalry had managed to drive the Saxons further south than they had wanted to go, off the good Roman road and into the countryside. They hadn't done it all with cavalry of course - they had fortified two of the river crossings and forced them south of the hill line.

They were now not far from Venta Belgarum, and from there the road ran straight west across the downlands to Badonicus. With intent, that road had not been blocked east of Badonicus and the cavalry would be able to draw back and harry those who left the road.

But for now, there was still the hard work of sheep-driving to be done. The wall had fully formed and they knew what that meant. The horses would happily charge through any gaps that might be left in the wall, but faced with a solid wall of steel they would shy away at the last minute.

Quintus knew that and didn't try to make his horse go straight. As he neared the wall, he pulled to the left and thrust his lance down over the wall. As he did so, a Saxon batted at it with his axe and knocked it away. Quintus kept turning and kept hold of his lance. Then he was streaking away from the wall as the next man rode in.

Quintus knew that Arthur was right: these cavalry against infantry skirmishes were indecisive. One or two men on either side might end up dead or injured, but the vast body of men would just roll on. Only a major infantry action would see an end to this vast army.

Quintus signalled for his men to regroup at the top of the rise. They allowed the horses to catch their breath and watched as the Saxons slowly moved away. They allowed them some time - after all, it was this slow south-westwards drift they were trying to encourage.

As the Saxon army started to climb the far side of the undulating valley and pick its way over some rocky ground, Quintus signalled his men again and they started to trot down the hill. Again, the Saxons formed up and presented a solid wall of shields. Again the charge and the ineffective use of lances and discharging of spears. Then the retreat and once more quiet.

"Last one for today, lads," said Quintus as he watched the Saxons move on. "Then we'll let them make a big of progress."

And they charged down the hill again and up the other side.

The Saxons were very sloppy about the marching camps they built.

Since time immemorial, the Romans had had a clear policy on building marching camps and it was so engrained in the troops by the end of basic training that once they arrived at the site and the surveyors had marked out the corners, each man could carry out all his assigned tasks without even having to ask for orders or directions. Within an hour, a solid, fortified camp was built that would resist even direct assault.

The Saxons seemed to just decide where to put their camps based on where they were when they became bored with marching for the day. They had no surveyors to point out the key features, such as where water would be raised and latrines sunk. And the outer fortification was little more than a token ditch with the baggage stacked up against it as an obstacle.

Fighting at night was tricky: it was very hard to see what you were doing, to begin with. Telling who was friend and who foe was even more tricky.

But the sloppiness of the Saxon preparations gave the Britons an advantage. Entirely mounted, their need for organization was less than the defenders, and the sloppiness of the camp meant that the defenders could not really count on it to assist in the defence.

It was deep into the night when Quintus attacked, coming out of the dark like demons on horseback. Men threw torches into the camp, trying to light tents and other flammable items. They crashed into the camp with lances and spears out, and tangled with the carts that formed the outer wall.

Saxons sprang to the defence, but it was spotty and disorganized. They were unable to form a strong shield wall, because of the darkness, the obstacles underfoot and the presence of enemy horsemen.

Quintus charged into the camp and thrust his lance into the face of a defender, dimly lit by the burning of torches around him. He pulled back and thrust into another man's face. Then he retreated as the enemy organized and swung axes at his horse's legs.

Like a wave washing against the shore, Quintus and his men charged and retreated for two hours before dawn broke, finally showing the damaged camp and injured men. Then Quintus retreated to safety, back to the north-east, blocking the obvious line of retreat.

Not that Norvel had any intention of retreating.

Norvel was angry with Lucullus.

"You did not tell me that we would be harried in this way. You said nothing of attacking at dark and burning my camps," he complained.

Lucullus stared at him, outwardly haughty, but internally in shock that Norvel could have been so ignorant and now, having been beaten in this way, that he should be so uncouth as to take it out on him. Good commanders took setbacks in their stride and learnt from them. Great commanders anticipated the enemy's moves before they happened.

"We should build proper, defensive camps every night," observed Lucullus. "I did try to tell you that."

"But we have a great superiority in numbers," countered Norvel. "How dare they attack?"

"Because they have superiority in mobility, and in tactics," replied Lucullus. "But that is a minor annoyance; a fly in the ointment. They are doing this just to slow us down and deter us from continuing. We must be resolute."

"If they want to deter us from continuing, why do they keep moving behind us, rather than blocking the road in front of us?"

Lucullus thought about that. It had not occurred to him up until this point, but for the past couple of days, the enemy had indeed seemed to take up positions behind the Saxon army.

"They are probably scared of being in front. They know they cannot stand in your way; you will just run over them. But at the same time, if they follow you and dog your footsteps, you will be slowed down and will not be able to move as quickly west as you would like. And eventually you will be ground down and run out of supplies and have to retreat."

Supplies were another issue. Norvel had assumed that they would just take want they wanted from the peasants, who would be unable to oppose them. But foraging had been hard because of the continual presence of Arthur's cavalry - it had not been possible to continually detach small units to forage from each farm along the front, and the whole army was too large to be able to survive on the small rations that could be had from the farms that were in their direct line of march.

Once again, Lucullus tried to suggest dividing the army into columns; each column could then protect itself against the cavalry, but divided they would be able to cover much more territory and forage from more farms.

"No, I tell you, no," said Norvel, intuitively distrusting the advice. Apart from anything else, once separated, his units would be prey for Arthur's forces which, he sensed, were out there somewhere waiting for him. Moreover, once separated, he would find it very difficult to keep the columns together and they would end up all over the place. And finally, he just didn't trust Lucullus - and thus his suggestions.

They crept on slowly, meandering through the countryside, being shepherded on towards the prepared battlefield.

Another argument broke out when they came upon the road.

"It will be blocked like the other," insisted Norvel.

"True, that may be true. But until it is, we can take it," argued Lucullus.

Ormod stalked over to enter the discussion.

"It may be that the road is blocked, but I said it then and I say it again. We should roll over any blockage in the road. We can take them."

"But they fortify so well. It would cost lives."

"We have the lives to give. What we do not have is time or food. We need to capture towns, take food and kill Britons. Only then will we be able to drive these peasants into the sea and make Arthur grovel at our boots."

The argument continued for half an hour before finally the obvious prevailed and they took the road.

Mons Badonicus rose to the south of the road like a shining beacon.

Lucullus already knew where it was and knew what was there: the I Legion (Artoria), along with two divisions of cavalry. All well dug in. A trap waiting and baited for the Saxons with enough food to feed this army for a year.

"You can't starve them out," pointed out Lucullus. "If the whole point is to get their food because they have more than us, then the arithmetic simply doesn't work."

"They are too well entrenched to hope to assault them," contributed Ormod. "We would have to attack uphill with trenches and stakes and cavalry to deal with. I don't think it's possible."

"What choice do we have?" asked Norvel, aware of the difficulties but unable to think of any other way of proceeding.

He suddenly turned to face Lucullus. "Do they have water up there?"

"Yes," he replied, but then doubted himself. Did they truly have wells, or were they reliant on rain water to fill cisterns? "But I think they depend on it to rain."

Norvel thought about this for a while. Here was one downside to adding five thousand men to an encampment designed for four hundred. A man could go without food for days - or even weeks - at a time if need be. But water was a daily essential. Take a man's water away for two or three days and he was a dead man. How long, he wondered, how long?

"Call the shaman," he ordered. The magic man came forward and was consulted. Would it rain in the next week? He did his dance and spilt fluids on the floor. No, it would not. For the next week, there would be no rain.

Norvel made his decision. They would dig in around the base of this hill, weaken them with thirst, and then storm the camp on the seventh day.

As plans went, it wasn't a bad one.

They fortified the base of the hill, surrounding it with two ditches and two palisades: one facing inwards, one facing outwards. They sank wells - a gratifyingly long way down, even here at the base of the hill - and ensured their own water supply. They sent out scouts in parties of a thousand to reconnoitre what was going on and to try and forage for whatever food remained in the neighbourhood.

All their actions were watched both from the top of the hill and from neighbouring hills. Every day, several times a day, messengers rode off to the north and others came back. Try as they might they could not catch any of the horsemen.

Three days passed, then four, and the siege continued. Then from the top of the hill a small delegation came down to meet with the Saxon leaders.

"We are dying of thirst," they complained. "What terms will you give us for surrender?"
