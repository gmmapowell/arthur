London

AD425

History has a habit of repeating itself.

Rome had been a Republic for centuries before Pompey and Caesar's titanic struggle turned an essentially civilian society with distributed powers into a state of personal loyalty. And when the very fabric of that society was threatened, Caesar's heir, Octavian, concentrated all the power into a single pair of hands - his own - even while keeping faith with the "forms" of the Republic.

Britain had made it about fifteen years of being an independent Republic before much the same forces led to much the same result.

There was little doubt that two men stood head and shoulders above the rest in terms of both auctorias - personal clout and straightforward military competence. Ambrosius Aurelianus, the first senior Consul, was one; Quintus Domitius Vitalinus was the other. Vitalinus was younger than Aurelianus, but since his election as senior praetor in 418 had taken the British world by storm. As praetor for Chester, he had led campaign after campaign against the Irish invaders in Ordovica, winning more battles than he lost. At the end of his year, he returned in triumph to London, where he was applauded and decorated. Official triumphs were not given in Britain - but then they hadn't really been given in Rome for the past four centuries either - but the "ovation" had been retained, and Vitalinus won one, as had Aurelianus eight years earlier.

Two years of vigorous campaigning in the Senate followed, leading up to the job he coveted: Consul of Britain. Once again, he chose to fight the Irish in Ordovica, this time taking a Consular army to the task. How much the British army had been reduced over the years was evidenced by the inability of even this force under a commander brimming with energy and enthusiasm to permanently dislodge the Irish from their mountain redoubts.

Retired once again to the front bench of Consulars in the Senate, he continued to fight for more action, more taxes, more recruitment, more training, more logistical support. He had one vocal supporter, Aurelianus, but many a detractor. It was too easy to paint a caricature of the glory commander, interested in nothing but winning battles and gathering power to himself. On the other hand, the majority of Lords in the Senate, coming from secure south-western holdings that hadn't seen an attack in the past four hundred years since Vespasian had brought the II legion's siege train through the district, were perfectly happy to cede an inconsequential part of a rugged terrain which nobody really wanted anyway to these foreigners. "Give them time, and they'll settle down and become good neighbors," as Titus Fulvius senior used to say. "It's not worth the time and money to evict them. Where are you going to get the troops anyway? Off our land. No. It is not happening. Not on my watch."

And so he floundered for many years. Yet, they were never years of real peace. Every year brought some other attack from some direction. The Irish continued to raid across the sea and bring reinforcements to their fledgling kingdom amongst the Ordovices; the Saxons raided the coast, often inflicting significant damage, killing, looting and plundering; and the Picts crossed the wall in sufficient strength to lay waste to much of the north.

For every Aurelianus or Vitalinus that obtained the consular power, there was a completely inept nobody who was awarded the same job. In 425, Lucius Fabius Maximus and Gnaeus Marcellus managed to both make it to the top at the same time. That might have ended well, except that the Picts chose that year to attack in force, and by sea, bypassing all the defensive structures that had been put in place by Theodosius almost a half-century before.

Attacking at will down the east coast, they bypassed several layers of defences and destroyed others. The signal and warning stations down the east coast were destroyed, and the Picts landed in force in the lands of the Iceni, working their way quickly to the Icknield Way; from here Verulanium was a short hop.

Fabius led the main mobile force to meet this threat, and went down badly, losing his own life - no great loss - along with five hundred others in a military reverse the country could not handle. Marcellus was sent to his aid with the backup force and what he could gather together of the remnants of Fabius' force. And went down so badly that he committed suicide on the field as his forces scattered.

In panic, the Senate met and conferred an extraordinary command on Vitalinus, making him effectively dictator. He gathered what troops he could and, in a lightning march, beat the invaders to Verulanium and whipped them soundly. They retreated, but only as far as the coast, where they started to dig in.

Vitalinus returned to London and summoned the Senate. "We have waited and waited and have not acted. You allowed incompetents like Fabius and Marcellus to lead armies and they have lost them. We no longer have the manpower in reserve to evict the Picts from their base on the coast without opening the whole of the west to the Irish. Ideas anyone?" He flung his arm out in a rhetorical gesture indicating that he imagined nobody would have any ideas at all.

It is said that the bad blood between the two great leaders - the rift that eventually sparked into open warfare - came from the moment that Aurelianus stood up. Probably, though, the causes were deeper; a more fundamental difference in their worldviews.

"We have enough men right now to hold them," argued Aurelianus quietly. "You have shown that in charge of this scratch force you can effectively defend the republic. Bottle them up on the coastline of the Iceni and they will probably soon tire of it and go home. Give them some gold and they will probably go sooner.

"At the same time, I can go down to the West Country and start a conscription of all males between 17 and 25. Within three months I could have an army ready that would be sufficient to drive the Picts into the sea if they haven't already seen the sense of leaving."

Men in the benches howled outrage. A conscription of all young men in the fields just to achieve in three months what could easily be had by waiting a year or two? And the very thought of paying them to leave - wouldn't that just make them come again and again for free money?

Vitalinus smiled at the outrage. In one way, he agreed with Aurelianus; had considered suggesting the same thing himself, but had seen the political suicide in entailed. Only a fool would align himself against the vested interests of the rich landlords.

"There is another way; a way that does not involve paying Picts to come here and loot and pillage; nor does it involve the sacrifice of this year's harvest.

"Across the sea are many sailors who would be happy to take a crack at these Picts. I'm almost surprised they haven't done it already, to be honest. To natural sailors, the Picts are sitting ducks there on the coast."

His words sank in slowly. Under the honeyed words and the seductive thought of not having to conscript workers was the sharp sword of betrayal: Vitalinus could only be talking about doing a deal with the Saxons. How could he? They were the enemy.

Moments of silence gave way to more howls of outrage. "Traitor! Betrayal! How could you?" rang out around the meeting hall. Vitalinus soaked it all up and waited for it to burn out and then strode confidently down the hall towards the imposing double doors.

"Your choice, my Lords. I say that this is the right way, the expeditious way, to proceed. Yes, we could achieve the same thing on our own, with our own resources, but at much greater expense. We already have all the tools, all the structures. We invite in 300 trained sailors, we allocate them ports on say - Thanet Island - we billet them under the normal provisioning laws for military on the move. The cost would be a fraction of what it would take to deprive our economy of a similar number of men, let alone train and equip them. And, moreover, we would be getting expert sailors. It is a bargain, my lords, and you are fools not to see it."

"It is a bargain, I agree," said Aurelianus, standing again as was his right as a Consular. "It is a bargain with the gods of the underworld. Once here, they will always be here; we will never dislodge them. They will come and their friends will come and they will swallow us up. What have we being doing all these years if not trying to displace the Irish from Ordovica? Trying to deny them a bridgehead on our island. And now you want to offer the Saxons - who you admit are better fighters - just that bridgehead. No, this is not a good deal. Any of the other options would be better."

Heads nodded around the chamber. The Saxons had been raiding for years and were a big threat; inviting them in was asking for trouble and to be overrun. But were the other options better? Were they even really options? The cost ...

"Well," said Vitalinus. "You instructed me to see to the defence of the state. I say that this is the only way in which I can carry out that charge. Accede to my wishes, or relieve me of my responsibility and look to your own laurels. The door is here, I can walk out if that is your wish."

Put like that, there was no choice. Even Aurelianus had said that his plan depended on the energy and genius of Vitalinus to bottle the Picts up for three months. There was little for it but to give in.

"Not that I like it," confided Fulvius to Aurelianus. "I think your plan was better, for all the cost. I fear we will come to rue this day. If nothing else, I think that man means to be king over us."

"King? What is this king? He wishes to be more than King over us, he means to be King over the Saxons as well."

"An over-king. A good name. Vortigern it is." And the nickname stuck.

Within weeks, Vortigern had done his deals and Hengest had been settled on Thanet Island with his "cousin" - a phrase that could mean anything with these Germans from brother to my wife's best friend - Horsa along with three ships full of men. Two weeks later they attacked the Picts from the sea while Vortigern led what native troops he had into battle from the landward side and utterly crushed the resistance. With nowhere to run, the Picts were annihilated or taken captive and turned into slaves to work the tin mines in Dumnonia.

"Good work, well done, Hengest. Now, as to the future ..."

Within days, more deals were struck, and over the coming months another ten crews arrived, to be stationed up and down the east coast. The "Great Raid" of 425 had demonstrated that the defences of the north simply weren't good enough to handle a determined seaborne attack. To be sure, the raid had ultimately failed, and the remnant of the raiders eliminated, but there were stil plenty of places where it was possible to come ashore.

Up and down the east coast, Vortigern built forts and ports and billeted a crew or two of his new allies along with their ships on the local residents. Uncouth and violent, they were not popular; nor was paying for them popular with locals or those further afield. But while the fear that the losses of Fabius and Marcellus had sustained was fresh in the mind, the money kept coming. The money was there, no doubt about it, but convincing the Senate year in and year out to devote it to taxes for defence was no easy matter. Even if that defence came at a discount when paying barbarians to do it rather than citizens.

And they answered to Vortigern, personally.

"You know the formula, Quintus Domitius," intoned Titus Fulvius pedantically at a meeting of the Senate held that December. "You are empowered to use all means necessary to defend the republic. We agree your success has been stunning. But it is, without question, a success. And now you must resign your extraordinary powers and hold elections for a new set of magistrates and return Britain to its proper government."

"No," replied Vortigern, without even bothering to stand.

"On what grounds? The republic has been defended. Even without an explicit term limit, your authority ends when the republic is safe. And the republic is safe. We can all see that."

"I disagree. The republic is not safe."

"How so? The Picts are annihilated or enslaved. How can they be a threat? The army is restored, if not to full strength, at least to a fighting force, and your Saxons defend our coastline."

"Until every Irishman has been evicted from our western hills, and until the threat of the Picts is finally ended, I cannot agree that we are safe. Even then, we need to build up our strength against further encroachment of barbarians from the continent."

"But you invited them here. You cannot call them a threat! That would be nonsense."

"Clearly our allies cannot be a threat. I speak of the others, the Angles, the Jutes, the Frisians, that we have not invited here. Still, they wish to flee the depradations of the Huns who they all fear, and presumably some day these Huns, too, will want to come here and take our green and pleasant land. Which of these threats do you not see? Are you blind?"

"Quintus Domitius," admonished Merula, leader of the house. "You will obey the forms of politeness."

Vortigern acknowledged the rebuke with a slight nod of his head, then looked back at Fulvius, expectantly.

"They may be a threat. But the Picts have been a threat for a thousand years but not even the skill of Hadrianus or Agricola could defeat them. The normal forms of government can contain these long-term threats over the long term. I demand that you resign and call elections immediately."

"Again, I say no. You may view the threat to the republic as long term, but I view it as real, existential and immanent. Depose me, and you will live to regret it."

Ultimately, Vortigern won the argument. If not by force of rhetoric, by force. When the Senate next met, two days later, it did so with Hengest and fifty of his Saxon warriors in attendance as Vortigern's personal bodyguard. When Vortigern moved for a division on the subject of the end of the state of emergency, any Senator who looked outside and considered for a moment how he would make it home alive, decided that there was, at the very least, a state of personal emergency that was very real and very immanent. And that the only thing to do was to pass to the right of the tribunal and accede to Vortigern becoming "Emperor Britannico", much as Octavian had five centuries earlier.

As Emperor, he moved quickly to do those things that he could not have done under the state of emergency: he invited two Frisians - Octha and Ebissa - and their retainers - some 500 in all - and gave them land north of Hadrian's wall, along with licence to do anything they wanted north of the border, promising them full logistical support from the bases at the wall as well as from Theodosius' buffer kingdoms. It was not a large force, but well dug in on the eastern coastline, supplied by their countrymen as well as the British, and able to raid both on land and at sea at will, it became a major thorn in the flesh of the Picts.

When buying allies for the defence of the republic, Vortigern paid with cash and land from the general fund. But for his next project, he needed Hengest's help with no long-term benefit.

"What I would like to do," he told Hengest through the interpreter Ceretic, "is to attack the Irish in Ireland. Attack is the best defence and all that. I don't imagine that it will be possible to make things so bad that our Irish will up and go home, but at least it would be good to stop any more coming over."

"You want to give bases on west coast?" asked Ceretic.

"No, no, you can work from here, or from your bases across the channel. You can settle in Ireland if you want, but I cannot support you there as I did in the North. I want you to raid Ireland, kill the Irish and make them stay home and look to their own defence. While you are doing that, I will try and evict the ones who are already here."

"What you offer me?" asked Hengest through Ceretic.

"All the plunder you can take."

Hengest laughed as Ceretic translated. "But what you offer me? Plunder I take, I take. What you give me more."

"I have no more," admitted Vortigern. "I can only give what I have."

"You have wife?" asked Hengest.

"No, my wife died four years ago," responded Vortigern.

"I have daughter, beautiful daughter. She now your wife. For this I do you say."

Vortigern considered, carefully keeping the disgust from his face. Marry a barbarian? Was it possible? What would the Senate say. Yet, it was a small price to pay. He could put her in one of his villas with some Saxon slaves and nobody would ever know the difference.

"Agreed," he replied. "I will marry your daughter."
