15. London

AD500

A lot of the work of the next decade was for administrators.

Arthur - well, Britain really, but Arthur was the guiding force in the Senate in those days - needed to get the economy back on its feet and get tax revenue flowing. It was all very well crushing rebellious forces in the south west, midlands and the north, but when it came down to it, what the country really needed was to get back to work.

After returning from the north, Arthur had convened the Senate and asked them to consider two interconnected motions. The first was to order a large-scale census of the island: who lived where, what equipment there was, and what land was lying fallow. The other was to authorize him to redistribute people and things around the country to maximize their utility and to begin a programme of improvements to the country's infrastructure. The roads and bridges had not been propertly maintained in almost a century, and the work that had been done from time to time had been done sloppily and in a hurry, generally by soldiers who wanted to use the road.

The Senate passed the motion for the census relatively easily. They recognized that without a fair and complete census, it was going to be impossible to figure out the correct taxes for the country and for as long as they could not tax effectively, the whole of the costs of government were going to be a burden on those rich enough to be known to be able to contribute - to wit, the members of the Senate. Of course, they didn't like the costs that Arthur's motion carried with it - the number of inspectors, the overlapping areas to ensure everything was covered and the men could not be bribed, the thoroughness of the investigations and the random inspections to make sure that none of the inspectors were enriching themselves. "Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?", as Arthur said, costs money.

Nothing and nobody could move the Senate to pass the second motion. Now that the Senate met in London, Merula - who didn't want to leave his estates near Glastonbury - no longer attended, and without his sage advice, Arthur felt frustrated and impotent.

He railed and raged, argued with individual senators and canvassed more generally for support. But the root of the problem was trust and personal interest. The powers Arthur was asking for - no matter how much he protested he would use them for the good of the people at large - were dictatorial in nature and could stand the current order on its head. Moreover, since he could arbitrarily move people or equipment from one place to another, he could easily impoverish his enemies to the benefit of his friends. By the end of the three weeks of debate, Arthur had to admit he felt sorely tempted. And felt he knew who his enemies were: basically the whole Senate.

Slowly the census moved ahead and the numbers came in. As Arthur had expected, Dyfnwal did a superlative job in the north - an area that hadn't been properly covered by a census ever, as far as Arthur could tell. The man was a born administrator and was well able to delegate the job of fighting to Fergus - a born warrior.

Again and again Arthur used the excellent job that Dyfnwal had done as a model for the rest of the country, and more than once sent to him to ask him to loan him some of the census-takers to distribute to other parts of the country. Cunedda did well in the central western highlands as well; another area where it was traditionally difficult to do a census. He made himself - and Arthur - more than a few enemies among those who wanted to conceal their wealth from the "thieves" in London, but his census was complete within the year.

Among land actually held by the British, the south-west gave the greatest difficulties. There, the vested interests opposed the actions of the census-takers over and over again, and more than once Arthur had to send detachments from his army to assist them in collecting the relevant information.

Surprisingly, the Saxons were very willing to collaborate on the census, and were curious about the whole process. They worked rapidly to obtain all the relevant information and assisted the inspectors in every way possible.

In the far west, where the Irish still held territory against the will of Arthur and the British Senate, there was no census. Soon, they would have to be dealt with, but first, Britain needed a breather from war to rebuild.

After two years, with all the information in, Arthur went back to the Senate to obtain approval for his long and complicated plan of "redistribution" that would get the economy back on its feet and make the most of their land, people and materials. If he had been authorized in the first place, this could have been happening as the census was ongoing and the benefits might be starting to be felt soon.

Arthur delegated to Agricola, one of the census chief inspectors - styled "Tribune".

"They are fed up with me," explained Arthur. "They assume that because I am victorious in battle, I must want to overpower them at policy and in wealth too. Nothing could be further from the truth. I would love for them to take the responsibility for making this country great again. But they just wish to carp, criticize and oppose what needs to be done. I hope that with you, they will listen and consider the merits of the facts that we have gathered; and, once they have listened, that they will then be prepared to act - or authorize you to act.

"I will be there, of course, but I intend to say nothing. I apologize if you end up feeling you have been fed to the sharks, but I truly believe this is the best way of making what we know to be necessary to happen."

Agricola smiled at him and simply replied, "All I ask is that when this is over you let me take out my rage and frustration on someone who deserves it."

"How about the Irish in Demetia? We still need to deal with them if we are to reunite this island under its proper government."

Agricola smiled. There was a campaign to sink your teeth into!

It did not go well.

"What do you mean you want to transplant my peasants and their ploughs to Saxon-held lands? That's madness. First you forgive the enemy, then you arm the enemy and now you want to reward the enemy."

"I would remind you," said <somebody>, acting Leader of the House in Merula's absence, "that while they were our enemy, before that, they were our allies, and now they are our fellow citizens. Some of their representatives are here in this hall."

"Just waiting for the opportunity to rise up and kill us all again, no doubt! Well, I refuse. My people are needed to grow my crops, and I will not part with them."

"That is not the case," countered Agricola, outwardly calm but inwardly seething. He understood know what Arthur was talking about but had no idea of how the old man had put up with it for so many years. He was a man of action, and to be stymied by idiots like these ... it was demeaning.

"The census clearly shows that much of the western lands are overpopulated; many fled there at various stages during the troubles and there is simply not the land there to support them being productive. So we have too many people working land at subsistence levels. Meanwhile, we have land in the east - some owned by lords of Saxon descent, it is true - which has remained fallow for many years due to the depravations of the wars. It makes sense to move the people to where the land is, for we surely cannot do the reverse."

In the end, Arthur managed to achieve about a tenth of the improvements he was hoping for. It would not be enough, he knew it.
