4. Portchester

AD480

Nothing is ever easy.

By the time Arthur had returned to Linnius, basking in the laurels of victory, the Saxons had overrun the outer defences and the siege was in full swing. Taking advantage of the element of surprise, Arthur had swung down the river and attacked the Saxons, driving them back onto their side of the river and relieving the siege. Over the following year, some amount of normality had been restored: the units in front of Linnius were holding the line again and the legions were back in their barracks near the wall.

As autumn turned to winter in the year of our Lord 479, Arthur and his men finally returned to Mons Badonicus, tired and weary, but content with what they had achieved over the course of eighteen months.

They returned to a different environment.

Cerdic, one of Arthur's alleged allies, had decided to branch out on his own, and agree a treaty of peace with the Saxons. In a small way, it made sense: without Arthur's forces in place to go on the offensive, Cerdic had been under daily attack without the ability to effectively resist. Furthermore, four more crews had come ashore to the east, reinforcing Aelle's band considerably. Seemingly out of options, Cerdic had capitulated rather than endure until Arthur might return. Or might not.

Within days of returning, Arthur called a meeting of the council.

"There is no way we can tolerate this. It is worse than the Picts attacking. At least we know they are enemies; this is supposed to be an ally."

"What did you expect him to do?" Titus Fulvius asked. "You were off, playing the boy hero and getting all the glory, and he was having his people killed on a daily basis with no defence. What could he do?"

"He had the citizen guard. What are they for if not to defend the citizenry against attack? Why do I have to do everything?" he fumed, unreasonably. "You may view me as having wandered off seeking glory, but I tell you, we have dealt with the Picts for a generation. If we can deal with the Saxons, we could restore Roman Britain in our lifetime."

A pipe dream; they all knew it. But did Arthur know it, and cynically exploit it, or did he really think it was possible. Did he not see the country fragmenting around him? But he probably did: that was why he was so irate about what Cerdic had done. If Cerdic were to get away with it, then the habit might spread. Before you knew it, the entity that Arthur saw as "Britain" and that now was realistically two - Britain and Angle-land - could become fractured into a host of petty domains ruled by self-appointed tyrants like the ones Theodosius had installed north of the border. If Arthur really dreamed of "Britain", then he would have to act. But attack a fellow Briton? That way madness lay.

"There are no good options, Arthur. Think carefully before you act," injected Mardoc. "If you fight him, you could break up the republic sooner than by letting it splinter by itself. Remember the true enemy."

"I remember the true enemy, Mardoc. I don't need to remind you who killed my parents. But does Cerdic remember? I cannot attack the Saxons without driving through Cerdic's lands, and I cannot go through his lands without his support. And I cannot have his support while he maintains neutrality. No, Mardoc, what he has done cannot be tolerated. We must act, and we must act promptly. By spring, we must in position to attack the Saxons, and if that means we must first destroy Cerdic, so be it.

"But let us use our time wisely. I will ride west to meet with the Senate. They will pass the resolution that we need declaring Cerdic outlaw for selling parts of the republic. We will pass that far and wide as both cover for our actions and to dissuade others from considering the same. I will think and I will take counsel and in the spring we will have and plan, and then we will act. Until then, Cerdic has four months to consider his position and change his mind. Quintus!"

"Yes, sire."

"You were once comrades with Cerdic were you not? Did you not serve together years ago?"

"I did, sire. I count him still my friend and would be sorry to war against him, but if that is what you command, sire, so will I do."

"I may or I may not. But for now, Quintus, I want you to ride east. Ride east and see Cerdic. He may decide that his best policy is to realign himself with us, now that we are returned. He may see the error of his ways, or he may even wish to say that it was just a stalling manoeuvre to give us time to return from the north. Do you see what I am saying, Quintus?"

"Yes, sire."

"Well, get going. If you can convince him of the error of his ways, let me know before the Ides of January. It is then that I will convene the Senate to indict on charges of treason. Be clear to him: I mean full treason, hostilius; not just malefaction. He will be outcast and enemy of the state, to be hunted down personally. I want no doubt about this message: I will completely believe him if he says before the Ides that he was just buying us time; I will completely destroy him if he does not."

"Yes, sire."

Arthur mulled the wine Merula had given him.

While he did indeed intend to call the Senate into session, Arthur's primary motive for coming west was to seek counsel with Merula. He not only had a lifetime's experience of diplomacy and military leadership, he was well-versed in all the classics and had a thought process that Arthur preferred to think of as "deep" but could possibly be more accurately described as "twisted". Arthur had no desire to declare Cerdic hostis; much less to waste precious lives fighting a civil war. As Britons, they should clearly be on the same side. Why could Cerdic not see that?

Because see it he could not. A message had arrived the previous day from Quintus saying that it was not a question of form of words, or of fear, but simply he saw no reason for him to not be "governor" of his district, at peace with both Aelle to the east and Arthur to the west. And if he could manage that, then maybe it would offer hope for the rest of the country as to how to live in peace. Because peace is what serves the peasants, not the continual destruction of their crops and buildings.

An opinion about which Arthur had expressed his opinion. Vituperatively.

"You are yet young and impetuous," chided Merula. "I don't think you see the big picture."

Arthur was too angry at this point for one of Merula's philosophical discourses and told him so. He had work to do.

"Yes, but what if you do the wrong work?"

"You think Cerdic is right? We should declare peace for as long as it suits Aelle and then let him roll over us when he's stronger? Isn't that exactly what Vortigern did that got us into this mess in the first place? Would Caesar have done that? Octavian?"

"You could say that Octavian did do it. He allowed Antonius to hang himself, if you recall. Patience, my son, patience. Ask yourself: is Cerdic really the enemy?"

"Yes."

"Sorry, I didn't mean 'just say yes without thinking about it'; I meant, 'ask yourself' as in 'think about this deeply for a while'. You never know, you might have missed something."

Once again, Arthur was struck by how hard he found it to think rationally when his blood was boiling and his emotions were running riot. How could Merula sit there so calmly playing with Britain like it was of no import?

He struggled to think, but nothing would come. Obviously somebody who stood in opposition to his plans was the enemy. That was basically the definition of the word. That was why Arthur wanted him declared hostis: a public enemy.

"What is the real goal?" Merula prompted, gently.

"To kill Saxons and restore Britain to proper Roman rule."

"Is that one goal, or two?"

Arthur thought. Surely they were inseparable. But in one sense, they were two goals. But how could he restore Roman rule without killing the Saxons.

"Possibly two. But inextricably linked."

"And if you could have just one, which would it be?"

Instinctively Arthur knew the right answer but mulled over the wrong one for a long while. Much of his desire to kill Saxons did not arise from the desire for justice or proper government but from simple revenge: the desire to kill the men who had killed his parents. How much did that obstruct him in actually doing his duty: his first duty - to protect the innocent and ensure justice for all. Was Cerdic right about that?

"It would have to be restoring Roman rule," he finally admitted.

"In that case, can you leverage this situation to make that happen?"

Arthur blinked. The thought was immense; bigger by far than his ability to process it. Could he use this rebellion to trick Aelle in some way? If so, what? And could he do it without Cerdic's cooperation, which seemed impossible to obtain?

Arthur thought until his head hurt, but nothing he could do could shift the mental blocks.

"No, Socrates, you will have to show me."

"I am sure you can solve it by yourself, my pupil. But let me ask you a few questions."

As it had ever since Merula became his parent cum guardian cum tutor, the game of Socratic dialogue never failed to relax Arthur: the questions, cunningly worded, drove the listener down an inexorable chain of logic by their precise nature. And yet, at the end, you could never really deny that you had "thought of it yourself". You certainly hadn't been told the answer.

"So, how many men would you be able to deploy against Cerdic?"

"Well, basically the four regiments at Badonicus."

"You wouldn't recruit more from the west country?"

"No, I don't think so."

"I don't think we'd really need them. You see, Cerdic doesn't have that many troops at his disposal, just the supply unit at Clausentum."

"I see," said Merula.

"So do I," echoed Arthur, letting out a low sigh. "I hadn't thought of that."

He knew, of course, that Cerdic had few or no troops. It had been a concern all the time he had been in the north and was, to a large extent, a proximate cause of the current crisis: Cerdic would probably not have turned on him had he been able to defend himself properly without Arthur's support. It had also been a consideration when deciding to issue the writ of hostilius; you don't go to war with somebody who will easily beat you.

But what he hadn't considered was that, in saying no to Arthur's olive branch to recant his defection, Cerdic must have known that Arthur was serious and would attack; and, as Arthur had just thought, you don't go to war with somebody who will easily beat you.

So Cerdic must believe that there was a way that Arthur would not easily beat him. He could, of course, issue a conscription of all his young men, but if he were going to do that, he could have done it two years ago and defended himself against Aelle. So, the only remaining possibility was that Cerdic was going to ask Aelle for help in fighting Arthur. Hostis indeed.

But more importantly, it meant that Saxon troops would cross the Arun - in numbers - to assist their new ally. And would come and fight Arthur on ground of his choosing. Well, not exactly, but he would choose the ground, and they would fight, and they would die. Why? Because he was better.

A low buzz sounded across the chamber.

The Ides had come, and Arthur had summoned the Senate. They all knew what Arthur was going to demand, and fully expected to issue the hostilius decree.

What they hadn't expected was Arthur to make this demand for a conscription.

"We've been through this so many times. We don't want to fight civil wars at the best of times, and we certainly don't want to impoverish the entire state to do so. What Cerdic has proposed is entirely reasonable. Live and let live. If he is prepared to act as a buffer zone against the Saxons, let's take that offer."

Ready and willing for this line of argument, Arthur had no difficulty keeping calm. "If he were, there might be some merit in it. But he is not a neutral buffer zone against the Saxons. He has chosen to side with the Saxons. We cannot allow them to just keep taking land piecemeal. Sooner or later this council hall will be Saxon too. What will you do then? Retreat to Dumnonia? And when they push you into the sea?

"Or is your plan to follow your erstwhile exemplars and flee to Armorica? If so, going now while it is still not in the hands of the Goths might be a good idea.

"On the other hand, if you want your children to grow up knowing what it is like to live under Roman law, you need to act now. In a few years it will be too late. They will take and hold, take and hold. That is their way. The more land they have, the more places they can land reinforcements. We can't stop them coming, and if we can't get to their bases we can't stop them landing. So the only thing we can do is stop them at the borders. The Arun is the border."

"So is the Dubglas around Linnius, but you seem prepared to sacrifice that."

A point on which Arthur knew he was weak and would have to cede ground. That this situation had arisen out of his attempts to keep Linnius did not alter the fact that the situation there was still precarious and the troops he had and was trying to raise could make a big difference there.

"I cannot be everywhere at once, and we simply don't have the troop numbers to do everything."

"Once again, you come back to demanding more troops. It seems that you will not be happy until the whole country is under arms."

"I will not be happy until all the Saxons have been evicted from these shores and we are once again masters of our own destiny," he countered. "If that requires us to temporarily conscript every man in this country, I think it's a fair price."

"And who would feed this army of yours? This is not a century ago where troops raised in Gaul could be fed by corn raised in Britain! There is no other land to supply the army you wish to raise. We would starve."

"It is true that we would starve if every man in Britain was in the army for many years. But many men are engaged in other labours, that send food or metals overseas; or build houses and villas. And we would not starve if only some of the men were taken from the fields that grow food for those that live here."

"How many men then do you say you need?"

"A legion would be good, but I could make do with two cohorts."

"A thousand men? Impossible."

"How many men are gathered here? A hundred? Are you really saying that each of you is so impoverished you could not find ten men of military age to send off to fight to preserve the republic?"

The Senators looked around; at the floor; anywhere. None of them could accurately claim any such thing: the majority of them could have found that many just from the domestic staff that they knew by name.

"I thought not. Two cohorts it is. The men to arrive for training at Mons Badonicus no later than the Kalends of February. Each to bring - or separately to be provided with - sufficient rations for five months, to include training and the campaign. The men will be returned by the end of June, assuming that they are still alive and that there is still a republic of Britain at that time. Clear?"

There were murmurs of assent; they didn't like being talked to like this, but at the same time they recognized their own weakness in the face of the man who had the army. As always, the Senate had to face the facts: for as long as they allowed these military men, whether native, foreign or Saxon, to be in charge of the army, they were in charge of the country.

Merula visited Arthur again before he left.

"You have your plan worked out, I presume."

"Yes," replied Arthur. "Portchester, I think. How many men do you think Aelle will bring with him?"

"Now that he will hear you will have a thousand, you mean?"

"Plus the eight hundred already in my command."

"He would be mad to bring less than 3,000 then, wouldn't he?"

"Do you think he has that many?"

"Don't you?"

"Thirty ships' worth," mused Arthur. "They have been coming for years now. It's possible."

"How many men would he need to hold Cerdic's territory if he did take it? You have to presume he has too many men for his current holdings."

"Because he is spread very thin. Most of the work is done by British peasants."

"And I know that rankles with you, Arthur. But all they want is a quiet life. And to not die of starvation, of course. Which is a big incentive when it comes to getting up in the morning and ploughing a field."

"If we could wipe them out ..."

"But if he wiped you out ..."

"Then all would be lost. All Britain will one day become Saxony or Angle-land. It could happen anyway, whatever we do. We can only try and do our best. Try and stave off the inevitable for as long as we can manage."

"Cavalry can't win against infantry, you know."

"I do, indeed. As our illustrious ancestors discovered back in the days of the kings when they were surrounded by charioteers but could not afford to grow horses themselves. But cavalry only cannot win unaided against organized infantry."

"Surely you are not suggesting that Aelle will be as disorganized as the Picts?"

"Not when he forms his battle line, no. But when he's driven our infantry to rout, there's a good possibility that his organization might crumple a little. Especially if our camp were, say, over-stocked."

"So you do have a plan worked out."

"Oh, yes, very much so. As I say, Portchester."

The raw infantry formed a ragged front.

What Arthur had not elucidated was the ground near Portchester that he had decided was suitable for this battle. The barely-trained infantry that he had conscripted were six ranks deep and thus about two hundred across some hundred paces in front of their camp. In the camp another fifty men were "guarding" the baggage. The camp was close to the top of the hill and it sloped gently down all the way to the sea. The main road - and thus the way that Cerdic and Aelle's forces must come was another four hundred paces down that gentle slope.

The infantry was not quite as raw as it at first appeared. Along the front couple of lines, Arthur had added some steel drawn from his own mounted troops - who could all fight as infantry - and some other "volunteers" he had borrowed from around the country. After all, this was the big battle; this was where the action was going to be this year; and Arthur was fairly certain that Aelle would be doing the same - stripping other areas to boost his numbers here.

Moreover, the conscripts did not know they were as raw as they were. Arthur had judged them fit, and they assumed that meant "fully trained" and it wasn't in Arthur's interests to let them know any differently. His own men - with Bryn the senior officer in charge of this infantry rabble - knew the plan and would put it into effect: first holding the line steady and then causing them to flee at the right moment. Arthur just hoped they could do it with a minimum of casualties. As with any plan, there was always risk ...

Quintus had the skirmishing job, as he often did, since he relished that work. For two days he had been harrassing Aelle's route of march, but since he was coming along a solid Roman road he was always able to form up in line of battle and present a wall of shields to Quintus' men. The two sides exchanged missiles but little more, and little harm came from an exchange of missiles. But it lent credibility to the idea that Arthur was trying to resist.

The messengers that Quintus had sent first thing in the morning indicated that the Saxons had broken camp - not a proper Roman military camp like the one that topped the hill, but sufficient to stop Quintus from overrunning them at night - and would be here by noon. Bryn had marshalled the men and, with reasonable grace, managed to bring them out here in an emulation of battle-readiness. It would look the part until the line gave.

Aelle and his men appeared on the road below, and continued marching until they were opposite the men on the hill. Then they turned and took a few paces up the hill, and milled around, organizing themselves for the attack. Unlike disciplined Roman troops, Saxons seemed to always need to negotiate their formation, which could take hours. Since he'd decided he didn't want to spend hours with his troops getting ever more nervous, Arthur had instructed Bryn that this was the moment to hurry them along a little.

"Scorpions. Fire!"

There were no range markers here. They had known exactly where the Saxons would arrive and within a few paces where they would turn and form up. In fact, a discerning general would have noticed the apparent lack of any battlefield preparation - except for the camp of course.

Arthur had also chosen to deploy just four scorpions. It would be enough, he figured, for what he had in mind, and they would probably all be destroyed during the battle.

The bolts crashed down into the milling ranks of men, slicing straight through three men and wounding two others. More men went crashing down as the dead and wounded were knocked from their feet. The scorpion operators started cranking the giant crossbows again.

Shouts of invective and fury came from the Saxons.

It would be too much to hope that they would completely lose it and just charge up the hill as individuals; but the artillery fire achieved its objective and before the scorpions had reloaded the shield wall had formed and three thousand men, ten deep and three hundred across started moving up the hill.

Against a three-hundred-man front, Bryn had no choice but to change his own deployment and move his reserves out and widen his own lines, now dangerously thin: just four men deep to counter the Saxon's ten-man depth.

I hope Arthur knows what his doing, Bryn thought. But at least we have the advantage of the hill.

The Saxons came up the hill relentlessly but steadily. Twice more the scorpions fired and killed, but the lines formed up effortlessly and flowed around the casualties.

With ten paces to go, Aelle let out a guttural shout and the Saxons started running. Bryn responded with "Take the Hit!" and hoped that there were enough professional soldiers in the front rank to do just that.

The two shield walls crashed into each other with enormous force: fourteen men pushing with all their might in a giant melee, stabbing over, under or through the walls of shields pressed up against each other.

Here and there along the line, Saxons in the second and third ranks wielded giant axes, threatening death to those in the British front lines. On both sides, men with spears in the rear lines poked and prodded, aiming for eyes and other unprotected areas of the men in the front couple of ranks.

The pressure was inexorable. Even with the advatage of the slope, the British were slowly pushed back up the hill. And the line started to wobble.

"On my word, two steps back," commanded Bryn. "Now."

With remarkable consistency, the men stepped back two paces and relieved the pressure. In the moment before the Saxons followed, they straightened up and reformed the wall in a straight line.

Again the crash of shields as the Saxons charged to close the gap, and again the pressure slowly forced the British uphill.

And so it went for half an hour or more as casualties slowly bled the numbers on both sides. But they were higher on the British side - and more significant.

"On my word, two steps back," ordered Bryn again, "then disengage and reform at the camp. Go!"

This was a maneouvre they had practised many times but which, unbeknownst to these raw recruits, was not a major part of the Roman military manual. If called to account, Rutilius Rufus would probably have been able to remember that such a maneouvre did exist - for exigencies only - but would not have been able to recall when he or another commander had actually practised it during drill.

As the men took two steps back, the hardened warriors of Arthur's unit reformed a one-man-deep wall and slowly retreated one step at a time. Meanwhile, the rest of the men turned and ran for the four drawbridges - two more that was usual for a Roman camp of this type - at the front of the camp. Once the men were safely inside, Arthur's men retreated too, although being careful to do so in such a way as they protected each other. The artillery had long since been abandoned and now all the men were in the camp.

Before they could pull in the drawbridges and close the camp gates, the Saxons were inside. A running battle developed in the camp and most of the men kept running out of the back of the camp. Before long, the field belonged to the Saxons, as did the treasures of Arthur's camp, which they began to plunder.

The noise of hoofbeats coming up the hill alerted them to their danger. About five hundred of the Saxons had made it into the camp; the rest were still outside, but no longer in battle order. Arthur and his other three regiments of cavalry were descending on them from all points of the compass and there was nowhere to go.

As rapidly as they could, they started to turn and form their shield wall. But many of the men had dropped shields and spears, taken off armour and were relaxing. The men who fought in the front ranks were the ones inside the camp, and while they weren't trapped it would be minutes before they could cross back over the drawbridges and take their place in the army. On top of which, they were doubly distracted by having abandoned their weaponry and picked up plunder.

Arthur's men crashed into the sea of warriors, lances at the ready. It was important to keep them off-balance; stop them ever forming a shield wall, because, once formed, it was impossible for the cavalry to break it down.

The Saxons started to come back out of the camp, wielding spears and hefting shields. As they did, a new sound distracted them: the British infantry, that they assumed had run away, was now behind them, back in the camp. What was going on?

A trap, Aelle realized. It was a trap and he was caught. Fifteen hundred Saxon dead later, he acknowledged the inevitable and surrendered. Cerdic was handed over for execution as a traitor and Aelle agreed to the continuation of the border at the Arun. He and his men handed over their weapons and retreated back down the road from whence they had come. A mistake to leave their lands.

The bonfire burned merrily, but the stench was awful.

The British had spent the afternoon and evening clearing the battlefield, gathering the "valuables" - such as food, weapons, armor and other equipment - into the camp, and piling the bodies up down the hill in pyres for cremation. It was hard work and not for the faint of heart, since it was often hard to remove a helmet without removing the head at the same time.

Outside the camp, the commanders gathered and looked at the burning bonfire. As usual, they had worked harder than any of the others, setting an example and just simply being everywhere at once, making sure that the job was done. Now they basked in the glow of the bonfire whose message was that it had all been worth it: they were the victors.

"You ran away," Quintus said to Bryn. "I will never forget this."

"I did not run away," countered Bryn. "I ordered the retreat and then held the line and covered for the recruits. I was brave."

"Oh, you bravely ran away, is that what you're saying?"

"Tell him, Arthur. I did not run away."

"I wasn't there. I was too busy leading a charge. But if you were obeying orders, you should have bravely turned your tail and fled. We were counting on it."

Bryn shrugged. If they were determined to have it that way, what could he do?
