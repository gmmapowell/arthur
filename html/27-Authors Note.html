Author's Note

I was first inspired to write about Arthur when I read the notes to Robert Graves' "Count Belisarius". He makes the point that our relative confusion about the events of Arthur’s time come down to a lack of good source material, especially compared to that of the time of Justinian and Belisarius.

It’s hard to know if this is because nobody wrote that history, or if it simply did not survive the sacking of Britain in the sixth century. Obviously, at this point it is not possible to recover the original, true story of Arthur. However, by working only from the earliest sources (before Geoffrey of Monmouth) and by constructing a story in parallel with that of Belisarius and what we know the Romans were capable of, I have attempted to reconstruct something that could have happened, unlike the traditional tales which cast Arthur very much as a mediaeval knight descended from the Anglo-Saxon tradition.

Which obviously he was not: there can be very little doubt that Arthur was a Roman. If he existed at all, and fought for the Britons against the Saxons, then he was a fifth-century Briton and that century started with Britain still a part of the “whole” Roman Empire, a status it lost when the Emperor Honorius washed his hands of defending it around 410. If we assume that Arthur was born sometime in the middle of the century – I have assumed 452 – then his parents could easily (per Gildas' description of Ambrosius' family) have been "wearers of the purple" who died battling the Saxons. The only plausible interpretation of "wearers of the purple" would be at least the rank of Roman Knights, which would have plausibly carried with it the command of auxiliary regiments of infantry or cavalry. While these would probably have disbanded over the course of time, it is not implausible that between this history, Arthur’s upbringing, and his possible access to Roman Literature (I have him at least having access to all the works extant today, but probably many others, especially those known to have existed in antiquity but lost today), Arthur could have been "the second coming of Caesar to Britain", one of the rare men who combined an instinctive understanding of war with the arts of democracy and a longing for peace.

In so doing he could have moved from a small-scale operator as a warlord (initially commanding 50-100 like-minded warriors) to a full-scale army general, leading the British forces at the battle of Mons Badonicus.

There is no need or reason to assume that he took at any time the title of "King" or "Emperor". It is reasonable to assume that there was a strong central government, and there may have been an emperor, and it may even have been Arthur - but I have chosen to assume a more Republican form of government, which feels closer to the context described in the sources before the rise of Vortigern.

In the peace that followed the battle at Badonicus, both the need for an army and the prohibitive cost of keeping one no doubt would have contributed to its demise. In this time, Arthur would doubtless have stepped back from public life. The fact that he died at the battle of Camlann suggests that he was still involved in some role.

While trying to remain as faithful to the "true" story as possible, I have tried to acknowledge the other traditions and point at incidents that, had they happened and been remembered, could have been later misunderstood in such a way as to give rise to the tradition. On the other hand, I think it is much more likely that later authors added to the tale exactly what they wanted to add to the tale without any thoughts for its accuracy. These traditions include, of course, the historically inaccurate "Monty Python and the Holy Grail" whose "Brave Sir Robin" inspired the Bryn story in Chapter 8.
