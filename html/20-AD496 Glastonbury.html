12. Glastonbury

AD496

Arthur entered the Senate chamber to rapturous applause.

He had achieved the impossible: he had drawn the Saxons into open battle, and, once there, managed to roundly defeat them. There were still Saxons in the east, of course, but any lingering threat that they might come west and again ravage the heart of the country had gone. Arthur had saved Briton for the British. And here he was!

In the aftermath of the battle, word had flown that Arthur was victorious and the Saxons defeated; indeed, destroyed, wiped out. The Senate had gathered and, in Arthur's absence, issued a series of decrees awarding him the highest honors. The very men who had opposed all his actions now gloried in the results of those actions - safety and security at last!

And Arthur had kept his promise to bring the Saxons to battle within a year; the army could now be decommissioned and the conscripts returned to their farms.

Arthur acknowledged the applause, moved to his customary place and sat down.

The consul began the proceedings and then asked Arthur to speak "on the state of the Republic and the Saxon threat."

Arthur rose and moved to the centre of the mosaic, poised, yet thoughtful. "I think," he began, "that it would be happier to take those in the reverse order."

He paused and looked around the chamber.

"As you already know, the Saxon army is no longer a threat to us in the West. Over twenty thousand Saxons were killed at the siege of Badonicus and another two thousand who survived were disarmed, swore perpertual allegiance to the Senate of Britain and were sent home.

"The number of useful Saxon fighters can now be measured in the few thousands. They could possibly outnumber our full-time professionals, but they, too, have none of those. They would need to strip their lands in order to put together an army that could again threaten our borders.

"But by the same token, we can still not evict them from this island. It may be - in fact, I would expect - that many of the families who have lost fathers and eldest sons will consider returning to the continent where they may feel they have better opportunities than they do here, but we cannot make them move, nor can we dislodge them from their current strongholds."

There was a noticeable intake of breath as he said this. For most here, the "Saxon threat" and "the Saxons being here" were interchangeable sentences. Not so for Arthur, who distinguished very clearly between their presence, and indeed, their military strength, about which he was neutral, and their risk to the independence, prosperity and values of Britain.

"I say that, at this point, we are at a crossroads. We have guaranteed ourselves peace for a generation - we have destroyed a generation of Saxon warriors, and they will need to wait for their forces to replenish and their young men to grow up. In twenty years, they will once again be able to pose a threat to us, should they wish.

"So, will they pose a threat?" He asked with the appropriate rhetorical flourish. Among the audience, there were those - like Merula - who still appreciated the technique of Greek and Roman oratory and rhetoric and they sat back and relaxed to enjoy what was, they knew, going to be a fantastic speech. The scribes, responsible for copying down the notes of the meeting, sensed the same thing and increased their intensity to catch as much of what was said as verbatim as possible. This was a speech that would need to be recorded for the archives - up there with Cato's famous filibusters.

"That is, curiously enough, for us to decide today. You see, people create a threat when they feel threatened. Let us review our interactions with the Saxons.

"For fifty years, we felt threatened by them and their raiding ships. Then Vortigern did a deal with them and the raids ended - in exchange for allowing them to settle here and paying them to defend us against the Picts and Irish. And for twenty years - as partners - neither felt threatened and there was peace.

"Then what happened, my colleagues? You should know, because even if you were not members at the time, you are now members of this body and should know what decisions it has made over the years.

"We refused to cooperate and engage with them. We rejected the alliances we had signed and reneged on the pledges we had made. We refused to pay the Saxons, and we refused to cooperate them. Yet we did nothing to clean up the situation and allowed them to hold territory against our sovereign will.

"With what results, O Senators? What happened? Well, the Saxons rebelled and took what we would not give them. As we all - or at least those of us that were here - must have known they would. And for twenty years they ran riot is this land of ours until - slowly but surely - they were tamed by the efforts of the great Ambrosius Aurelianus. Tamed, but at a cost.

"You applaud me today, Britons, for adding the next chapter in this story. But it is not the final chapter. There never is a final chapter, but this story is very much incomplete. This island holds Britons, and it holds Saxons. It is big enough for all the people but it is not big enough for two ways of life. Either ours must prevail - or theirs will."

Again the dramatic pause, the rhetorical flourish. The questioning pose, as if a thought had hit him like one of Jupiter's lightning bolts from heaven.

"But, my friends, what is our way of life? What makes it ours and not theirs? And have we given it up in order to achieve this victory for which you applaud me?

"I stand here, holder of a title I still consider unconstitutional - I hold it because it is what is needed in order to get the job done, not because I approve of it. And today, as I said I would, I wish to lay it down and announce my desire to stand for election to that very constitutional post of Consul.

"But before I do, let me reiterate that I hold the unconsitutional title of Dux Bellorum right now for very sound reasons. Simply put, because there was no constitutional route which would have preserved this republic.

"Think about that for a moment. There was no constitutional way of preserving the constitution. This tells you something - or at least it should.

"What makes us different from the Saxons is that we are not families, bands and tribes; we are a constitutionally mandated society under a set of laws. All of us! Each and every one of us is under the laws that we as a body choose to pass, and all of those are supposed to be in line with our constitution, which says what kind of people we are."

He paused again for a while to let that sink in. Looked at individual faces to see how they were taking his lecture in basic Roman governance. Knowing all this, but as background knowledge, not something they used to inform their daily decisions; thinking that there was always someone else who would do the dirty work for them.

"We are not a warlike people. We have been at war for so long that it often seems like war is a key part of who we are, but just a century or so ago, our grandparents knew a period of peace. A period of peace unprecedented in history. Neither Greeks nor Egyptians not Babylonians nor Republican Romans had known a period of peace as long as the two hundred years of security ushered in by Hadrianus."

His voice swelled, reached to the rooftops.

"A golden age, my friends. A golden age of the world. And yet, it slipped through our fingertips. Rome was destroyed. Not in my lifetime; not in the lifetimes of most of us, although one or two might remember it," this last with a glance and a smile and Merula, who claimed to remember where he was - and what he was reading - when the news came that Rome had been sacked. His claim that he was reading about the fall of Troy in The Iliad made the claim somewhat dubious, but Arthur was not one to doubt the word of Merula.

"We have the opportunity here and now to try and re-build that golden age. Not across the whole of Europe, but we can do it here, in this Albion of ours. We can stand out as a beacon of hope to the rest of Europe in this night of domination of Barbarians. We can remain an island of civilization in the midst of a sea of Chaos.

"Or we can choose to give up our values and what we believe in. We can descend into the pit of barbarism ourselves and, regardless of what happens to our bodies, sacrifice our souls to the barbaric values and methods of the Saxon invader.

"It is our choice and today is the day we choose."

And he walked back to his seat and sat down. For a few moments all was silent in the chamber as the majority of those there were temporarily transported by his oratory from their daily concerns to a broader view, a wider view of what it meant to be Roman; not just the lifestyle; not just the privileges; but the responsibilities and duties that went with it. A duty to truth; and virtue; and to be the light of civilization for all mankind.

Then the applause broke out. For today at least, the hero could do no wrong.

But would that last? When the applause died down, Merula called the assembly to order and dismissed them. And then invited Arthur and a few others to dine at his house. They accepted. But then, who could refuse?

The dinner was pleasant, with like-minded individuals sharing an intimate conversation.

Many of those present had been with Arthur at the battle of Mons Badonicus, and the first two courses passed with a discussion of the battle and their roles in it. Those who were not there were impressed with the courage and skill of those who were, but to Arthur's mind this was all the more symptomatic of the problems facing Britain. Without the stirring oratory of the Senate meeting, he raised the same issues he had brought up then.

"We cannot afford to continue viewing the Saxons as the enemy; they simply are not. They came here willingly, under contract to defend us. They are warlike and have a simple worldview that goes with that: kill or be killed. If we work with them, we can all benefit, but if we insist on fighting them, we will all lose. And, eithout the kind of all-out commitment that we have just made every generation, it will ultimately be us that lose."

"Why?" asked <somebody>.

"Because, you simpleton," interjected Merula, "while Arthur has just won a great victory, he has just killed the fathers of some sixty thousand little Saxon brats who will learn at their mother's knee who killed their darling father and swear revenge. And in twenty years' time when they have grown up they will carry out that threat.

"And Arthur may or may not be alive; they may or may not kill him. But they will do great destruction across this land. And if we beat them, then what? They will again have children who will swear vengeance on the heads of those who killed their fathers.

"And we will lose not because they are better than us, or more warlike than us, or more prepared than us, but for the very simple reason that they have less to lose than us.

"Think about what Arthur did: he tricked them into coming here with the promise of wealth and the opportunity to destroy us by taking out a couple of our key cities. And now the tables are turned, Arthur says we cannot destroy them. How is this possible? Because they do not have key cities; they do not have large forts that are critical to their defence; they do not have infrastructure that can be degraded. They are not a civilized people; if civilization falls apart, what do they care? They can have their little lords of nowhere-in-particular and whereever-I-am-right-now and maybe they meet once a year in some moot or whatever and thrash out problems, or possibly the King of Thanet Island goes to war with the King of Rutupiae and sort it out on the battlefield. As long as they have enough food to eat and enough steel to make swords, they are happy.

"Books? Who needs them? Cities? What benefits do they really bring? Baths? What for? Law courts? Easier to use a sword. You get the picture?"

Arthur laughed. "Merula, you do take this so very personally. Not to be rude, but I think I have guaranteed civilization for your lifetime, at least."

Merula smiled at Arthur, but the bitter edge remained. "You may be right, young man, but it is cold comfort to know that for all that I have had in my life, all those I leave behind - my children, grandchildren, friends, comrades, good men and women all - will be left in the night of barbarism. I have fought against it my whole life and it pains me to see it slip from the grasp of those left behind."

"Then let us choose otherwise," replied Arthur. "I mentioned a choice today, and while the idea that we have to choose today is maybe a rhetorical flourish, the choice is real but is a choice for all of us to make together.

"If civilization is to survive, then we have to not beat the Saxons as men, but as an idea. Their idea, as Merula says, is 'each man for himself'. Each man wants to become a Lord, and each Lord wants power over his rivals.

"That is not our way. We have rivals, but we are all rivals in the glory of Rome. My greatness is not based on whether you fear me, or whether I can destroy you, but whether or not you believe that I can contribute to the greatness of what Rome stands for.

"How does that win against the Saxon? It cannot win by force of arms; that is their way and regardless of the military results, they will ultimately win. We have to overwhelm them with our culture. We need to win over the hearts and souls with what Rome stands for and has to offer.

"To paraphrase Merula: we have to get them to want books, cities, baths and lawyers. Only then will we win."

The others looked at him and at Merula, once again struck by the notion that in a land of midgets, these two men were intellectual giants. Where many fumbled to even know right from wrong, and they themselves struggled to know how to achieve their aims, these two had it all figured out two hundred years into the future. It was almost as if they had been to the future and were now just coming back to tell them what was inevitably going to happen. Almost like fortune tellers with their crystal spheres.

But over and over again, they both mentioned a choice. While making it clear that only one selection would be considered valid by their offspring.

"What does that look like?" asked <somebody> again, speaking for all the others in the dining room.

"We must engage with the Saxons. Not take our army east, but our culture," stated Merula authoritatively.

"But it is already there. The majority of people living east of the Arun are already British."

"The people are," replied Arthur. "That is true. But the culture is not. They are ruled by Saxons and the Saxons do not share our culture and where it is not supported it cannot thrive. London is a beacon of civilization - because we still hold it. Linnius is civilized - because we still hold it.

"But Camulodunum is a ruin. Claudius' fair city is back to what it was after Boudicca burned it to the ground: a mess. Canterbury is still a town with some buildings but they are cattle sheds, not law courts. We need to change that."

"And," added Merula, "there is also the difficult part, which Arthur is keeping back from you. We need to convince the Saxons that they want it to change.

"Oh, and we have twenty years."

"Is that even possible?" asked <somebody>, breathlessly. "I wouldn't even know where to begin."

"Oh, that's easy," said Merula. "Vote for one Gaius Artorius who I hear will be running for Consul next month. And then support every aspect of his legislative programme energetically and enthusiastically; argue for it and campaign for it. Oh, and convince everyone you can that the money they spend over the next twenty years is worth every penny if they want to see anything they own passed on to their grandchildren. In another century Britain will be Saxon if we fail."

The next month went by in a whirlwind.

Arthur had been used to ruling by dictat and working with like-minded individuals who were sufficiently familiar with him to know that, even if he ordered something that seemed unexpected to them, he was going to be right and that they should "just get on with it."

Not so the privileged nobles and electors of Western Britain.

They were grateful to him for saving them, and pleased that the expensive conscript army could be disbanded. But now he wanted to start spending more money on an expensive and ridiculous project to "civilize" the Saxons. As if that made any sense: they were barbarians.

Patiently, over and over again, he covered the same ground. Winning a battle was one thing; winning a war was more complicated; but winning the peace required a different skillset. And Arthur was starting to wonder if it was one that he had.

His surrogates, Merula chief among them, also campaigned energetically among the electors. There was little doubt that Arthur would be elected - his reputation after Badonicus was just too stellar - but the question now became one of whether he could manage to get his aggressive programme of legislation through the Senate.

He could, of course, have resorted to the old trick of hiding his agenda until after the election and then trying to force it through a reticent Senate; but he reasoned - with the essential concurrence of Merula - that to do so would generate enormous resistance from a Senate that thought the difficult months and years were behind them. Much better to admit up front that this was a crisis election where a choice needed to be made - the tone Arthur had taken from his first speech - and then argue in every subsequent debate that he had received a mandate from the electors for his entire agenda. Those that truly supported him would back this message, and those that merely supported the victor of Badonicus would go along with it. And hopefully that would cobble together a majority most of the time.

Finally election day came.

Arthur was returned as senior consul - no surprise. But, to his dismay but also no real surprise, Titus Fulvius was appointed as his colleague. A brake and an obstacle on any radical plans he might try and force through the senate. On the upside, the senior praetor - the one, who, officially, should be the commander-in-chief of the various armed forces - was his preferred co-consul, Dyfnwal.

For the past twenty years of course, Arthur had been unconstitutionally filling this role; and he had taken over from Ambrosius who had merely filled it de facto by dint of getting on and doing it without any legal appointment whatsoever. And before that, there had been the ten-year hiatus of government ... but in theory, this was an appointment that was made on an annual basis.

Arthur's main campaign theme had been a return to "normality" and "constitutional government", so one of his first measures was to announce that Dyfnwal would, in fact, fulfill this role and would be responsible for trying to reign in and harmonize the action of the different armed forces around the country.

Over the past thirty years - in reality, ever since the government in Rome officially abandoned them in AD410 - Britain had been in "survival" mode, doing whatever it took to keep the Saxons (and others) at bay regardless of the long-term consequences. It was these that Arthur now sought to rein in.

The problem in Britain remained intractable: in order to have peace and stability, there needed to be a strong central government that was able to pass - and enforce - laws on all the citizens of the republic. But in a fragmented republic where there were small, independent, bands of horse soldiers ready to go to war at any time and who were - following the example of Ambrosius and Arthur - entrenched in easily-defensible hillforts, enforcing the law became a battle of wills - or worse.

What differentiated Arthur from most of the warlords was his genuine desire for a return to constitutional government, rather than attempting to use his position for personal gain.

Of course, there were those that would argue having been Dux Bellorum for all those years and now, having leapt from that unconstitutional role into the highest constitutional one, it was easy for him to lecture others on the virtue of being "under the law".

Arthur took all those attacks in his stride, and tried to work as openly as possible, consulting with others and working hard to make sure that all his actions were constitutional, fair and open. But at every turn, he faced obstructionism. The vested interests in Britain were just too strong for the interests of Britain to overcome them.

On the Ides of January, Arthur called the Senate into session for the third time since becoming consul to promulgate a series of measures that he hoped would crystallize his approach to unifying the country once again. His agenda for one year as consul, but a blueprint he hoped would be followed for the next generation; one that offered a slim hope of peace, law and security on this island.

Once the meeting was called to order, Arthur arose from the consul's chair and addressed the gathered senators.

"It is pleasant indeed to address you in a time of peace as a man of peace," he opened. "I hope that this is something that my children may have the opportunity to do, and their children.

"And it for their sakes - my children, your children, their grandchildren - that I have called you here today. I have said it ever since we returned victorious from the battle of Mons Badonicus: we stand at a crossroads.

"To the one hand, lies utter destruction. We can relax our vigilance and allow our enemies - traditional, recent or as yet unknown - destroy us by allowing our defences to slip and taking this current peace for granted.

"On the other hand lies fragmentation and decay. We can choose as our defenders wolves; we can feed them and encourage them; and then one day, as did the Saxons, they will reveal their true colours and devour us. We have already done this once by appointing Saxons as our guardians; we must not make that mistake again.

"But in between these paths lies the one straight way. It is, in the words of the Hebrew Jesus, as quoted in the Christian texts, a narrow way, which avoids the destruction to every hand.

"I can point this way. Indeed, today, I will point this way and I will point it clearly and forcefully. And over the course of this year, I will try and place your feet upon that way. But then at the end of this year I will move from this chair to one amongst the consulars - constitutionally, for a change. And then it will be up to whomever you choose to elect as the magistrates for next year - and the year after - and the year after that - whether we stay on that straight and narrow way, or if we fall off that path to the one hand or the other into the jaws of one or another dragon.

"Because listen, and listen well. With the vast range of threats that surround us, there can be no question that success is a very narrow window. It will take the same level of effort - although of a different nature - as we expended last year to defeat the Saxons. And it will take it year after year.

"I know that this is not what you want to hear. You want to hear that everything has been resolved and that I have guaranteed us peace and we can all rest on our laurels and enjoy the fruits of our labours.

"But that is not the case."

Arthur paused and watched for their reaction. Nobody likes to be told that their dreams of peace and security are just that: dreams. And much as all these men loved their children - and in some cases grandchildren - it was hard to understand that sacrifice now was the only way to guarantee their continued existence, let alone a fair and just world for them to live in.

"So let me start by outlining the threats as I see them. There are four.

"Firstly, there are the foreigners. The original threat is still there, if different. The Picts, like the Saxons, have been defeated for this generation but are threatening to rise again. With swift action, we can capitalize on that victory to establish a new order in which we can defuse that threat for all time. I will return to this later in my programme of action.

"The Irish have been defanged by the work of Vortigern, Germanus and the monk Patricius. They are now - nominally at least - part of the family of Christian countries in Europe and slowly becoming civilized. If we treat fairly with them, they will be our allies for centuries to come.

"However, to our west, across the Sefern river, many of the old Irish have colonized parts of our lands. We must evict them by one means or another. Sadly, I think the only means that will work - since they would no longer be welcome back in their former lands in Ireland - is to evict them or destroy them by force.

"Across the sea to Europe are nothing but barbarians. Against them we must continue to defend our eastern coastline. At the moment, we either need to depend on the Saxons to do that work for us, or we must admit that we cannot do it, because we have no navy and no ports - and we hold none of the eastern coastline.

"Across the sea to the south are the lands of Armorica - or Britannilla - where many of our former people live, holding the Frankish barbarians at bay. They are our natural allies, and we should try and work together to hold the barbarians back. This may or may not be possible. Here in Britain we have the advantage of the sea to hold the barbarians at bay - they must be able to land and stage in large numbers in order to threaten us; whereas in Armorica they can stage all along a border many miles long.

"Secondly, there are the Saxons. Like the Picts and Irish, they present no current and immediate threat due to the victory at Badonicus. But long-term they present much more of a threat than all the foreigners put together. Why? Because they are here, in our land, have a long border with us, are very warlike and are a major force in this island. Without immediate and positive action, they will eventually overrun us. Be in no doubt about this: they are not going anywhere, and if we do not civilize them, they will overrun us.

"Much of my programme will deal with this truly existential threat; I hope that I will convince you that this programme must be fully funded and carried through with application and dedication.

"Thirdly, and more insidiously, there are our own rogue governors. Like the Saxons, they pose a long-term existential threat, in that they are here among us, have borders with us, cannot be easily controlled because they are a force in the land.

"These men see themselves as above the law. And I say that no man in Britain is above the law! The law must apply to all men. Above all else, we must ensure that, in future, the government of this island is a government for all men, according to their station, which is honest, equal, blind and enforced.

"I have plans to deal with them. Of all the parts of my programme, this is the area where I am least happy and would most welcome better solutions, but I - and others - have looked hard at this problem and can see no better way of proceeding.

"Finally, I come to the most insidious threat of all. The threat inside of us: all of us. The external threats can be met and mastered; or countered and neutralized. But the threats inside of us can only be dealt with by a change of heart and a change of spirit.

"For change is needed, my fellow senators! A change as radical as the difference between barbarism and civilization itself. For many years, we have faced threat after threat to our continued existence - and done nothing.

"Oh, we have fought. I, specifically, have fought. I look around this chamber and I see others who have fought. But for what, I ask you, have we fought?"

The question was asked rhetorically; Arthur did not expect or want any answers to be called out. But he did want each and every man there to take the time to think about the answer he would give if questioned. To think about whether he had fought, whether he had sacrificed, whether he cared about his country most - or his personal wealth and standing.

"I say that we have fought to preserve what is ours! Not to preserve the Roman civilization in the West! Not to preserve a way of life that has lasted over a thousand years! Not to preserve the great works of learning and scholarship that have been written and curated. Not even for the lives of other men, less fortunate than ourselves.

"No, we have fought by and large in order to preserve our wealth and our position and our right to rule. Look at when we have fought and when we have not fought. We have fought when our wealth is threatened. We have abstained from fighting when our wealth is secure. But our values? They have been under constant threat for over a century. And in that time we have actively undermined them whenever it was more profitable to go the other way.

"And I say the time to stop that is now. Today is the day we start to put values ahead of profit."

Arthur paused again. He looked around and saw some resistance, some intolerance, but mainly he saw faces that were looking back at him with expectation: they saw him as a man who had been through so much, survived it and now had lessons to teach rather than learn.

Now he had to launch into his agenda. If it were just a programme of legislation, or if he reasonably thought he could carry it through in "his year", he would have been filled with confidence about getting it done.

But this was a multi-year project. He wasn't even sure that it would have fully taken root inside the next generation: and that was the timescale he knew, knew deep in his bones, was the one he was working towards.

"So today, my fellow Senators, I have a programme to propose. It is a long-term programme that may or may not be complete in my lifetime or the lifetime of many of those present today. But it is, at heart, a simple programme which addresses all of these concerns in one simple trick.

"The trick is this: we establish our principles - the principles of good Roman governance - and we gather together all those who support and will fight for those principles. Regardless of who they are - or from whatsoever nation they hail - if they swear to uphold the constitution of the Republic, we will call them 'Romans' and 'Britons' and consider them brothers.

"All others we call enemy. Again, regardless of birth or nation."

He paused once more. He had promised a programme and in large part this didn't sound like one. Indeed, he had not propounded the details. But all the details were implied for those who cared to pay attention. But as he looked around he saw the penny had not yet dropped for most of those present.

"So, to get to specifics. Against my better instincts, I first propose we offer a complete amnesty to all those governors holding troops, camps, hills or land 'in their own right' who will surrender those places and those forces to the proper government of the republic - to wit, this body. I will be clear with you all: without the vast majority of these men coming over to our side, and straight away - within weeks - there is no prospect of any of this agenda working and there is no prospect of our long-term survival. They can and will eat us alive before we even have to face the existential threats.

"I would dearly like to see them punished for their crimes, and for any that will not give themselves up, I wish this body today to pass the sentence of crucifixion on them and all their retainers for the crime of resisting. I want to be clear about this, my friends: there is a huge carrot and a huge stick here. To those who will resign their strength, accede to our authority and agree to follow the law in the future, we will frankly forgive them all their crimes - which are many.

"To those who are foolish enough to refuse, I will take all the might of my army and utterly, utterly destroy them. Just for refusing. There will be no subsequent trial and conviction. A special treason court, constituted here, will pass the sentence, it will be taken to the location where they are resisting and carried out forthwith; with the one exception of any individuals inside that camp which aid the forces of the republic in getting inside will be spared.

"This is my first proposal, and word of this should be carried to all those 'petty generals' by the end of this week. They then have one month - until the Ides of February - to obey this body. The treason court, if needed, will be invested the following day and will pass sentences that day."

He looked around. The majority of faces were reflecting the awe of the impressive sweep and thought that had gone into this proposal: it was a brilliant divide-and-conquer strategem on two separate levels. First, he planned to use the generals who did give in against the ones who didn't; and second, by threatening crucifixion for every man, woman and child who could be considered a collaborator with those resisting, he created a positive army of potential traitors in any camps that did decide to hold out.

But here and there among the senators was a tinge of one fear or another: some had relatives that were the 'petty generals' being targeted; some could think through the consequences of this kind of legislation and see how it could be turned to a more ruthless usage by a suitably unscrupulous individual.

Arthur cleared his throat; moved slightly. Time for a change of tone.

"Then we will have unity among the armed Britons in this island. For too long our forces have had their strength dissipated by fighting each other, or their force has been wasted oppressing the common people of this island. By bringing these men down from their hills and into the main body of the army, we will gain strength and gain wealth.

"But we will still have to pay these men. I know that this will not be popular, but we need to levy the appropriate taxes to pay for this army. On the upside, my friends, we do not need to conscript an army - they will all be volunteers! But we must pay for this army, and we must pay on time and in full.

"Why? Because it is the right thing to do. Oh, and many in this army have a tradition of taking what they want from the nearest peasants who can't resist them. Speaking for myself, I simply don't want to encourage that, but as I look around, I see men who, if the army were to be located near their estates would be highly distressed to find it just 'taking' what it needs.

"And I plan to move this army around. This is not the old army of the North, stuck out of the way; or the Saxon army of the East, only affecting people 'over that way'. This army is going to be running backwards and forwards between Dumnonia, Demetia and up north to fight the Picts.

"In short, my friends, this is an army that is going to go past your estates, your villas, your friends at least once every year. And if you vote to not pay for it, I'm sure that somebody here will let them know why they aren't being paid. If you take my meaning."

Most of them did; the nasty smile that Arthur left on his face as he finished talking didn't leave much room for doubt. He had read that Sulla had been a master of the forceful facial expression, and that many senators in the late Republic had been left quaking in their boots by nothing more than a look and a reprimand from the original Caesar.

"So what is this army going to do apart from run around the country intimidating honest taxpayers? Well, reducing our enemies of course.

"The details are boring and otiose to relate in detail. But by the time this decade ends, all of the Western highlands will be ours, and all of the North up to the Antonine wall will be a secure dwelling place. All these lands will report to this Senate and accept its authority. If you want the details, come to the military planning meetings that will be taking place over the next month.

"Finally, I turn east, and consider the Saxons.

"This is more tricky. We have beaten them in battle, but cannot evict them. So we must integrate them and civilize them. Within twenty years, they must be Roman. Otherwise they will continue to present the biggest threat we face.

"I have a simple solution, but it will not be easy or cheap. It consists of three parts.

"Firstly, we must show them that we are not afraid of them. I propose that from next year, we move the government back to London, where it rightly belongs. This location is more convenient for many of us, it is true, but the real reason we are here is for fear of the Saxons. By returning to London, we may inconvenience ourselves, but we demonstrate that we are the government of the whole island.

"Secondly, we must take civilization to them. Camulodunum was destroyed in the rebellion and has never been rebuilt. Canterbury has become their de facto capital, but it is a barbaric place. We must show them how Romans function: law courts, businesses, forums. We must show them how we live: baths, theatres, sports.

"And finally, we must educate their children. It will be the children who hate us, for killing their fathers. It will be the children who destroy us - because they will grow up hating us.

"We must stop them hating us. And the only way to do that is to show them - every single day - that we destroyed their fathers for a reason. And that reason is civilization."

He sat down and waited for the reaction. Merula applauded, then others. As the grandeur of the vision dawned on them, all of them joined in and cheered him to the rooftops.
