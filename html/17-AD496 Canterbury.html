9. Canterbury

AD496

Lucullus was regarded warily by the Saxons.

He had arrived the previous day with a handful of warriors in tow and two tame Saxons that he had brought as interpreters. Their loyalty was questionable on both sides, but they had been held prisoner by the British for long enough to speak the local Gallic-derived language. They did not speak any Latin.

Norvel looked at them intently, trying to see if he could tell divine their intentions. Since Hengest's death, he had been the leader of the Kentish Saxons and was styling himself "King of Kent". With Aelle's forces protecting much of his border to the west, and with ever more reinforcements arriving from the continent, he felt strong and powerful, ready to flex his muscles and see where the chips fell.

That this man should come at this time was strange, very strange. What he claimed to offer was stranger still. The British feared the Saxons, but they also looked down on them. Why was this man willing to sell out their cause? Cerdic had agreed to peace with them and been destroyed for it. Did this man think he would be treated differently?

Lucullus stood erect and poised under their stares. He knew his reputation - the haughty one - but it didn't bother him. And in this situation it was probably more of an asset than a hindrance. These Saxons expected a particular demeanour from Romans, and Lucullus was just the man to give it to them. That he might die for his misplaced arrogance here was a possibility he accepted with equilibrium. He might also come out of it well. There was land to be had, good land, if the Saxons were to prevail and see him as their friend.

"You would betray your master in this?" Norvel asked and one of Lucullus' tame Saxons translated. "Does not that make you lower than the scum of the earth?" His tone, even before translation, conveyed the contempt that the Saxons had for weak men that betrayed the trust of their Lords.

Lucullus saw the emotion for what it was and batted it to one side as he would a flea. No man, let alone a barbarian, had contempt for a Licinius Lucullus. Who were these people?

"Arthur betrays his country and the good of his people. Is he my master then? I say not. I say that I represent the true will of the British people and that is to live in peace."

"And yet you encourage me to make war on your people."

"I say that the only way in which we can have long-term peace between Briton and Saxon is to destroy those forces that would perpetuate the war. One battle now, before Arthur has mobilized his new forces, and we can have peace in our lifetime. If you wait another half-year, you will invite Arthur to bring all his forces east - infantry, not mere cavalry - and allow them to grind you down. You will not be able to resist and you will be driven out of this land and back into the sea."

"So you say. Where is the proof?"

The word of a Licinius Lucullus was not to be doubted. The impudence of this man knew no bounds. He raised himself even straighter, even taller; his face took on a haughtier mien.

"Arthur has conscripted three legions. They are training even as we speak at bases in the west of England where you cannot reach them but I say that they are there. In six months they can be here. _Three legions_. You know what that means? Fifteen thousand men. Maybe not the equal of your men, but fifteen thousand men campaigning through Kent - with Roman tactics and supporting cavalry. Could you oppose that?

"You ask for proof that I am telling you the truth. Ask yourself this: can you risk the possibility that I am telling you the truth, and yet you do not act on it?"

Norvel remained quiet even after the translation had finished. He walked around the party, gauging them. It seemed this man was telling the truth, but ... something was off; wrong. What was it?

"I say you lie. Maybe there are three legions; maybe there are not. But you coming here ... you do not tell me the truth. You tell me something else; something is rotten in your speech.

"You will tell me the whole truth or you will die."

Lucullus looked blankly at Norvel. In fact, what he had told him was the truth: he had heard it from Arthur himself. Arthur had claimed that he wanted Norvel to come out and attack him, but Lucullus had not been able to credit that, and believed that Arthur had just told him that so that Norvel would stay put when he heard rumours of these new forces.

That he risked death in coming here was not news. But given that he felt that he had told him the truth, this seemed a difficult threat. He evaluated what else he could say.

"Arthur claims that he would benefit if you were to attack him. He thinks to harry you along your march with cavalry and then draw you to battle somewhere in the west."

Norvel listened to the translation with interest. Why had Lucullus not said this earlier? Presumably because it was implausible and Lucullus did not want Norvel to doubt his earnestness. For implausible it certainly was: if Norvel were to attack in strength, he would rapidly overrun and destroy whichever part of Britain he were to invade. Arthur could try and harry him, to be sure, but cavalry against a dozen men was one thing; against five or ten thousand was a different matter altogether. Behind a solid wall of shields he would be protected from a few pesky cavalry. They had demonstrated it many times.

"Is that really what he thinks? Or are you just sent here to make me think he thinks that, and then to stay here skulking while he collects his forces?"

Lucullus held on to his temper with great difficulty. This was like dealing with a child; worse, like dealing with an idiot child. How did he hope to beat Arthur? Had Lucullus picked the wrong side.

"I said he claims that. As I say, I believe that is a feint precisely to make you stay here, each guarding your territory individually and thus allowing him to conquer each of you separately - divided as you are from each other by London and Linnius. Note how assiduously he has worked and fought to keep those thorns in your flesh. Together you are too strong for him, and he knows it. His only hope is to keep you from combining."

Norvel nodded, accepting that the wisdom of this, and also that it was consistent with everything else Lucullus had said up until now.

"But one other thing worries me," he said, walking forward and tapping Lucullus on the chest. "You. Why you? Why now? What is in it for you?"

Lucullus felt threatened by the approach and the gesture and found it hard to concentrate on the translation, bizarrely coming from behind him. He felt himself start to slip away, become distant from reality.

Why me? He asked himself. Because I know that Arthur will lose. Because I know that I am the better man. Because I know that Arthur would not have sent me as a mere messenger. He knows that ultimately it must be him against me and that I will win. And that to do that and to preserve some British influence in the final settlement of this island I must come here and lead the Saxons so I can be the overlord of the island when they have defeated Arthur and his legions of amateurs. But how can I say that? How can I tell him that without insulting him and having him hack my head off here and now before he sees the wisdom of it?

"Arthur fears you," he ventured. "He has lost the support of the Senate and this is his one last desperate ploy to try and win back support in the council. I cannot continue to support a man who has lost, who knows he will lose and is prepared to destroy everything for the benefit of his own ego.

"If you fight Arthur, many of the British will support him just because they see it as Briton against Saxon and they are Britons. If I fight with you, many will see it as Briton against Briton and they will say that this dispute between leaders is not for them. They will sit out on the sidelines and wait for the battle to be over and get on with their lives. And when they do, they will be Britons living in a Saxon land."

"And what is it that you want out of all this?" Norvel demanded, finally starting to see what was happening inside Lucullus' head.

"I would expect to be Lord of the Western Saxons and their subjects," he replied as if he had stated this a dozen times already - or as if were obvious.

And as his man translated it, Norvel saw through the whole plan, and realized that it really should have been obvious.

He threw back his head and laughed.

From Canterbury messengers went out across Kent; they went to the South Saxons; they took ship and went across to Anglia, where the Iceni had been; and they went further north also, and visited with their brethren north of Linnius.

Everywhere the message was the same: Arthur, King of the Britons, was preparing a major force readying for a major expedition. He would attempt to attack all three nations separately, and reduce each of them in turn. Norvel requested that all the leaders gather at a suitable meeting point - he suggested Thanet Island, with its central location, secure boundaries, and easy access by boat - in order to discuss how they could work together to oppose this threat.

Lucullus and his translators came to the meeting. Unlike a Roman meeting, speedily and efficiently arranged, and organized with the precision of centuries of practice, the Saxon gathering was chaotic and inefficient.

No specific date was set for the meeting. Norvel had set off two days after sending out his messengers. Lucullus couldn't be sure, but he imagined that he and Norvel had arrived on Thanet before some of the leaders had even received their invitations. The other leaders would come when they were ready.

There was no agenda or meeting protocol. As leaders arrived, they conferred - and drank - until all the news had been exchanged. If a plan was developing, Lucullus - who was present, although hampered in his understanding by the difficulties the translators faced in keeping up with the disorganized, riotous conversation where often three people were talking (well, shouting) at the same time - could not discern it. Used as he was to the organized, Roman way of doing things, he could not even understand the relative ranks of these individuals. The subtleties - in particular, the contests of both drinking prowess and physical ability - passed him by; to him they just seemed to be indications of barbarism and an inability to stick to the task at hand.

About a month passed - January was slipping away by the Roman reckoning - by the time all the leaders had arrived on Thanet Island. By then, unrecognized by Lucullus, a rough organization had formed between the leaders and a plan was starting to develop, in part because - as Arthur had recognized the previous year - only one plan really could develop.

Ormod of Anglia emerged from the following week as the undisputed leader of the combined Anglo-Saxon forces. The son of Osric, who had been commander at Venta Icenorum at the time of the massacre, Ormod had bested the other leaders in drinking, stave fighting and describing the best plan to defeat the British. Another week passed in fleshing out the details of their plans, and agreeing the point at which they would gather their forces (Silchester). Each of the tribes was responsible for individually breaking out of their territory, and marching up or down the nearest convenient Roman road, and converging on Silchester by the second full moon from now. They would then blaze a trail to the west, largely following the Roman road to Aquae Sulis, killing, burning, looting, raping. If Arthur's forces were prepared to meet them in battle, they would do battle, but if not, they were a force of nature let loose behind the enemy's lines. It was a complete plan to the Saxons.

To Lucullus, used to more detailed planning for a single Roman raid into enemy territory, it was a joke.

But numbers told. And between them, the Anglo-Saxons had committed twenty thousand vicious warriors to the task. Arthur could not even match the numbers; he certainly could not match the ferocity. And his troops would be green, even if they had been trained by the time the battles took place.

And whatever Arthur said, he couldn't really want to be fighting on the defensive against these kinds of numbers, able to divide his forces and destroy each of them individually.
