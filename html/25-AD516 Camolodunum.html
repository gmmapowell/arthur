17. Camolodunum

AD516

Medrault was a fool.

Arthur cursed his name over and over as he rode at the head of his force of cavalry enforcers up the Icknield Way as fast as he could.

Arthur, of course, had opposed the appointment of Medrault as Governor of Camolodunum from the start, but once again had been voted down. The only difference was that this time it could be serious.

Camolodunum was in "Saxon" territory, and while over the past twenty years, that had come to mean less than it had before, the Saxons still viewed their heritage with pride and did not want to be talked down to by a stuffy Roman.

But the Senate had wanted a very Roman Roman in charge; one who would look down on his inferiors - and hold them in their place. Fools, the lot of them, cursed Arthur again. And now, once again, I have to clean up their messes.

Because of course, the inevitable had happened. The Saxons had taken umbrage at their treatment by Medrault, and he had been forced to flee the capital at Camolodunum and retreat to London. Arthur had been called out of his genial retirement and was now coming from the west, where he had been wintering with Merula on his estates near Glastonbury. Hopefully Medrault would be waiting for him at Verulanium and then they would proceed - with a suitable armed force which Arthur hoped and prayed would not be needed - to a meeting with the Saxon leaders. Hopefully, everything could be sorted out.

"What were you thinking?" Arthur demanded.

"They were insisting on having their disgusting Modraniht festival in the civic hall. Well, I just couldn't allow that. We are a Christian Republic."

"They have a different religion to us, Medrault. Don't you know that? Romans were pagans once; not even so very long ago. What harm would come from them celebrating their rites?"

"You have been very clear that they need to integrate. There are perfectly acceptable Christian celebrations at that time of year: the birth of Christ, and the feast of St. Stephen."

"And over time, I'm sure our very eminent bishops will make the point to them that their Mothers' Night celebration is really just another way of looking at the stoning and martyrdom of St. Stephen. But until they do manage that miracle, it seems that allowing the Saxons to celebrate in their traditional way would be the most likely way to bring peace and harmony; don't you agree?"

It was clear that Medrault did not agree. Even though his chosen approach - to refuse to let the Saxon leaders into the civic hall, and finally barring all the entrances until they broke them down and stormed the hall, chasing Medrault and his followers out into blind retreat - had clearly not led to peace or harmony.

"So what do we do now?" Medrault asked pathetically; almost whining, thought Arthur.

"What choices do we have?" retorted Arthur. "We have to ride back to Camolodunum and see what we can salvage from the situation. Hopefully, they held their festival, feel that they got the better of you and I can just send you to the Senate for trial and execution and the whole thing will just blow over."

"Hopefully? Execution?" squealed Medrault.

Arthur looked at him in blank confusion. And then it dawned on him that Medrault still did not realize that he was in the wrong. That obviously he had broken the law and would need to be tried, and, when the situation was so sensitive, justice would not only need to be done, but would need to be seen to be done.

In that context, executing the ringleader was the very least that could be expected to happen. Arthur's fear - that the whole of the east coast might erupt and Camolodunum might have been burnt down by the time he arrived - seemed a lot worse. The execution of a petty, self-absorbed bigot like Medrault seemed a small price to pay for peace in this island.

Camolodunum was still standing when they arrived.

While this fact in itself was pleasing to Arthur, the fact that the road to it was blockaded by a Saxon force, well dug-in in almost Roman style, sent the clear message that they expected trouble. Arthur considered the various options at his disposal and sent his troops away. He would have risked fighting marauding Saxons; he would have ridden up with his small force as an insurance policy if everything had seemed calm; but in this context, he wanted to lower tensions and take the heat out of the situation.

He sent Medrault away with the troops; seeing him might be the trigger that set the Saxons off. And rode on alone.

He was greeted by <somebody else we should have met before> and they exchanged greetings. Arthur dismounted and <the saxon> walked out from behind his defensive wall. Arthur hobbled his horse to a nearby fencepost and walked up to <saxon>.

As always, Arthur spoke in the Saxon language.

"Well met, my friend. How can I apologize for the boorishness of Medrault?"

"You could hand him over to me and let my men pull him limb from limb," replied <saxon>.

"Yes, I suppose that would be a possibility. But that's not really how we do things here, as we've discussed."

"It is when you want it to be and it isn't when you don't want it to be," countered <saxon>. "You let this creep barricade the hall against me."

"And he was wrong to do so. I have told him so. I am going to send him to London and the full Senate will try him for treason. He will then be executed - legally."

"So you say. But I know your Senate. They are his friends and they still think of us as enemies. If you want us to be your enemies, so be it. We will fight you again, and who do you think will win this time?"

"If we fight, none of us will win, my friend. You know that. Chaos and darkness will descend as they did before we agreed the truce after Badonicus."

"Ah yes, your great victory, O Arthur. The one for which you will always be remembered. The one you can never cease to rub into the wounds of us Saxons. But I say to you, Arthur, that we have grown strong and you have grown weak. You have looked away from us and fought the others: the Picts, the Irish, your rebels. And you have done so at great cost to yourselves and then you broke up your army, leaving parts of it in the hands of fools like Medrault. I say that we are stronger than you."

"And I say that we are stronger together, <saxon>. Consider this: we are together on this island, free of all others. United as one people, we can live in peace, without war, and with all our children growing up together. I want that for my children. Don't you want that for yours?"

"Can we live in peace, Arthur? Can we live in peace with cretins like Medrault allowed to be governors? Why is an Angle not good enough to be appointed governor of Camolodunum? Or a Saxon? Why must it be a toffee-nosed Roman? Because we are not good enough.

"We may not be good enough for you, Arthur. But we are strong enough. Don't pretend you didn't come with an army today just because you sent them away when you came to parley. We saw it. Your men and your horses. But we are ready for you today, Arthur. We have learnt from our previous mistakes and we know what it takes to beat your horses.

"Enough of this talk. Let us decide on the battlefield who will be Governor of Camolodunum."

"No, my friend. Friends do not fight. Allies do not fight. Fighting is for enemies and I am not - and do not want to be - your enemy."

"But Medrault is my enemy and him I will fight. Are you a coward? Will you not fight me?"

"I will fight you if you give me no other choice. But I would rather be friends."

"Then I give you no choice, Arthur." And, so saying, he lifted his ram's horn to his lips and blew a mighty, ringing tone that sounded around the valley, echoing back from the far hills.

But, as Arthur listened, he realized it was not an echo but the sound of other horns answering. He - separated from his little force - was surrounded by Saxons.

As <saxon> laughed, Arthur ran to his horse, jumped on it and rode back to his small cavalry unit. By the time he arrived, they were already engaged fighting a force of three thousand Saxons, surrounding the two hundred light cavalry troopers. They had the advantage of mobility, but the Saxons had the advantage of the prepared terrain, the numbers, and the organization.

Arthur had ridden into a trap.

It was the only battle he lost. After Medrault died, and Arthur was pulled from his horse and killed, the rest of the men surrendered. But no mercy was shown them.

The Saxons were barbarians on the rampage once again.
