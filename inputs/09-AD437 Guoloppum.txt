&date AD437

Needless to say, Vortigern's wedding was not popular with the other nobles.

He seemed happy enough, and did not in the end send her away to one of his villas as he had planned, but lived with her as man and wife, having three more children.  To the Romans, however, it seemed strange and barbaric and whispering tales went the rounds: that she had cast a love-spell on him; or that the barbarians had strange bed-practices that made them irrestible.  But in reality, it was just one more in a long list of complaints that the Senators had about their new dictator, although most of them were related to his love of the barbarians in one way or another.

One of the areas where there was relativity little enthusiasm among the Romans of Britain was for the theological disputes in the Christian church.  Britain was, of course, nominally Christian and had been since Constantine had told them to be.  Many of the Senators - not to message those of lesser rank - observed the necessary rites and paperwork, and then moved on with their lives.  Certainly the number of craftsmen called upon for mosaics of Orpheus in the underworld or other Olympian themes had not significantly decreased over the previous century.  And the theological disputes between Orthodox, Catholic, Arian and Pelagian passed them by in their entirety.

So why bring it up?  Because the events of the following decard can only really be understood through the prism of Christian fundamentalism.  Who can say if the actions that were taken would have been for the best anyway?  But certainly what drove them was the desire for pure Christianity.

---

Pelagius was a heretic.  There could be no doubt about this, because the Church's supreme thing with the Pope in charge had decided so.  I won't bore you with the exact details of his life-extinguishing heresy, but basically he said that you have to get off your backside and go out there and do stuff if you want a good life after your dead; nothing comes from free.

Apparently, this is not the case, and the "Augustine doctrine" is that God, being all-knowing, decides what life you will lead before you are born, and then you live it, and regardless of what you do, God decides what happens to you based on who you were and whether you accepted his teaching fully enough.  Or something.  Does it really matter?  Well, it did to the Bishop of Auxerre, who came to Britain in AD429 with approval from the collection of Bishops in Gaul - and the Pope, no less - to root out those subscribers to the heresy and put them to death.

You might well think that all the bishops and their paymasters across Italy and Gaul would have better things to do with their time and money - such as stopping the incursions of the Goths and Franks - but apparently not.

Anyway, on his arrival in Britain, he was invited to London and attended a meeting of the Senate, where he outlined his plans for the Inquisition.  After a long debate, it was decided that he should proceed to the western parts of the country, where it seemed that he would find most of the men of this persuasion.  He and Vortigern set off together and became close over the weeks that followed, although in reality Vortigern, who wanted to do nothing to cause further friction in a society that was already struggling with the actions he had taken, did everything in his power to frustrate Germanus' inquisitorial work.

Germanus was staying at the post station on the road near the fort of Caer Gai when the messenger came through, announcing an attack by the Irish coming down from their mountain forts in Ordovica.  At the time, Vortigern had returned to London to deal with the administration, but had left Germanus his "personal" bodyguard of half a century to make sure that his orders were carried out.

Germanus quickly and efficiently determined everything the man knew.  Probably 500 Irish were marauding in strength down the road, probably mainly looking for supplies and loot.  Because of the issues in recent years with other battlefronts, the Irish in the rural west  had been ignored, and now they were running amok.

Germanus had not always been a priest:  years ago, he had been pursuing a political career, and, as did most of his contemporaries, had done military service and led armies.  Twice he had commanded legions fighting against the Goths in Gaul before giving it all up to become a Bishop.

In other words, he knew what to do.  He had the nucleus of a fighting force: the fifty men Vortigern had lent him.  And he knew where to find others, because he had been visiting farms, villages and towns looking for heretics.  And he had, right at hand, an almost ideal fortress: just on the top of the hill was the fort of Caer Gai, which, if not perfectly preserved, at least made for a defensible position.

He briefly outlined his plan to the messenger and then saw him on his way with his new force.  He was riding to Chester, and would come back with as many soldiers as he could find.  Two days, probably, or maybe three before they arrived.

Germanus sent riders out throughout the region, alerting farmers and townsfolk to the danger coming down from the west, and asking for all able men to bring what weapons they had and assemble at the fort, to hold the road.

Over the next day, men straggled in from across the countryside and the fort was repaired and improved.  By the following morning, when the Irish came boisterously into the clearing below the hill, gleaming swords and spear points greeted them from the hillside.  While attacking up the hill seemed like suicide, leaving such a fort behind them was asking to be caught in a trap on their retreat.

The potential stalemate was broken at noon when the reinforcements came up the road from Chester under the command of the _primus pilus_ centurion Gaius Catellius and promptly formed up for battle.  While useful, the two centuries of Roman troops that could be spared were not really sufficient for the job given the terrain.  After a brief encounter, the Romans retreated uphill and were admitted to the fort under covering javelin fire from the ramparts.

Which led straight back to a stalemate.  The Irish could not get in, but the Romans could not effectively deal with the superior numbers of Irish given the territory.

Neither side was well-supplied, and while the Romans were trapped _in_ the castle, unable to gather more supplies without running the gauntlet of the Irish warriors outside, the Irish were likewise trapped by their inability to detach any significant numbers to go raiding without losing their numerical advantage.  Some men did go off, in small groups: some came back with food, while others did not come back at all, killed by small bands of Britons who had not arrived in time to enter the fort before the siege began.

Eventually the stalemate broke after three days with the Irish retreating back up the road towards Ordovica.  Germanus and the centurion from Chester had discussed their options and possible battle sites up the road, and set off about six hours after them.  Marching at their full speed, they were able to go into camp that night two miles behind at a narrowing in the road, which prevented full deployments and encircling tactics.  In a series of running battles over the next two weeks, Germanus won a series of decisive victories, driving the Irish back into their strongholds.

Vortigern, on his return, he was both relieved and pleased at the outcome.  He had wanted to battle with the Irish for many years now and this had been a gift sent from God - and in the person of a Bishop.  Recognizing the situation for what it was, Vortigern immediately appointed Catellius as regional governor and gave him the fort at Caer Gai as his permanent base.  He further allowed him to keep all the troops that had been requisitioned from Chester as well as those from his bodyguard.  Vortigern's plans were starting to take shape.

---

"It is a shame about these Irish, you know."

Vortigern more leaned to the opinion that they were a nuisance, but since the campaign against the Irish, he had come to have a deeper understanding of this man Germanus.  To Vortigern, all priests seemed like leeches: sucking the money out of the economy for no benefit in security or investment.  But this man was deeper - maybe they all were - and seemed to genuinely care about men's souls in this life as well as the next.

"In what way, Germanus?"

"They have never had the opportunity to see the light of God."

Again, Vortigern thought that the thing it would be more useful for them to see would be the point of his sword sticking out of their back, but he tried to see it from the bishop's perspective.

"Consider, this.  Why do they come here and attack us and raid?"

"Because they want our wealth."

"And why is that?"

"Because they are poor."

"Indeed, my friend, but are not all who are without the word of God poor in Spirit?  Is not this what our blessed master tells us in his beatitudes?  That those poor in the Spirit will be blessed by the coming of his word into being filled with the Holy Ghost?"

Again, Vortigern felt that this somewhat missed the point.  Most Irish he knew would prefer to be filled with food and wine, preferably British, and preferably without paying for it.  And would then like to go and get involved in a good rousing fight.

"I'm not sure that would be wise, Eminence.  I think any priest that went to Ireland without a sizable bodyguard would be running the risk of dying a gruesome death before he had the opportunity to explain about the resurrection."

"We must have faith, Vortigern.  On that mountain top, I saw a vision.  God came to me and said, 'These will be my people.  Follow them and minister unto them.'  I have been thinking about this since; while I did follow them I did not minister unto them but slaughtered them.  But I think God was speaking to me in a parable."

Kill me now, thought Vortigern, who hated the way the priests turned everything inside out into tortuous explanations that made no sense to anybody.  And left you wondering if their logic was logic or just a justification of an idea that had just come into their head.

"When I go back to Gaul and confer with the Council of Bishops.  There may be a man who would be prepared to take on this task and go to Ireland to save their souls."

"I pray for that, Eminence," said Vortigern, hoping the sarcasm passed Germanus by.

---

In the years that followed, Vortigern, Hengest and Horsa stepped up their campaigns against the Irish.  The Picts were licking their wounds and those Saxons that came to Britain generally ended up being recruited by Hengest, or sent to work for Octha north of the wall, rather than attacking the coastline.

This gave the British some much-needed respite and an opportunity to build their own forces for defence and even attack.

An opportunity they wasted; instead, preferring to see in the calm an example of divine favour and to complain about the amount of money Vortigern was wasting on billeting "his" Saxons off the coast of Kent and on the relatively small - and all volunteer; no conscription - force he was building at Chester and Caer Gai to final evict the Irish from Ordovica.

In 431, Palladius arrived in England and was greeted warmly as he made his way across the country.  This was the man who was going to Ireland to convert the heathen to Christianity; and, more importantly, it was hoped that he would stop their incursions into the mainland.

As a man to send to convert the Irish he had some positives and some negatives.  Among the positives was that he was not a man to be easily intimidated.  On the other hand, almost every community he visited was more enthusiastic about his arrival than they were about the man after his departure.

In the middle of June he sailed for Ireland and began to preach.  Exactly what happened to him there is not recorded, but by late autumn he had returned to Britain a broken man.  The ship that brought him back reported that his preaching had not gone down well with the "heathen" Irish, and, it is to be presumed, they did not appreciate being called "heathen" or any other perjoratives.

None deterred, the following year Germanus sent another priest, Patricius, with a couple of acolytes to try again.  Although the Irish king, Loegaire, is said to have been less than thrilled at the arrival of "another of these pesky priests who preach the dead-not-dead god on a tree", after a few months Patricius had started to make converts among the nobles, impressed by the tales of power and riches - not to mention life everlasting - that Patricius had held out as benefits of the new teaching.

At the same time, Hengest launched a major raid on the Irish coast and captured land on the east coast, enough to build a bridgehead.  Loegaire, unaware that the Saxons had come with Vortigern's blessing, sent messengers post-haste to Vortigern to propose an alliance.  Since both rulers were threatened by the spread of the barbarians, Loegaire proposed working together to oppose them.  Since this ruled out the possibility of further Irish attacks on the west coast of Britain, Vortigern agreed with alacrity, and sealed the deal with an agreement that Loegaire would convert to Christianity in exchange for a suitable spiritual guide - Vortigern's daughter - as his wife.

---

With the possibility of reinforcements from Ireland fading, Vortigern moved to try and mop up the remaining Irish in the west.  There were two areas that were of concern.  On the right bank of the Severn, deep down by the Irish coast, many Irish settlers were dug in.  Vortigern, considering his options, decided that repeating the "accident" that had happened with Germanus and Catellius would be the cheapest and most expedient option, went north and conferred with Cunedda, a grandson of Paternus Clemens who was settled north of Hadrian's Wall.  A younger son, he had not inherited the command in the north that he felt should rightly come to him as the most talented.  He had, by all accounts, been causing trouble and inciting rebellion and treason among the Picts and northern British for a number of years now, and Vortigern felt that it was time to put that talent to use.

After a couple of months of difficult negotiations - smoothed over by a relatively small amount of cash that he was able to raise without actually getting an official directive from the Senate - he managed to convince all the parties involved that Cunedda should take a thousand mixed troops (British, Picts and some hybrids) to Cetgueli in the far west, where he could make all the trouble he wanted with the local Irish settlers.  In the usual fashion, he settled him down in the area with the usual "billeting" provisions that the locals had to supply him with all the necessities of life.  Since the alternative was to be continually raided by the Irish, this seemed like a good deal to the British in the far west of the island, generally cut off from most Roman support.

On his way back from the north, Vortigern stopped at the fort of Wroxeter and encountered a surprisingly similar situation.  Here, the Cornovii had been responsible for raising and deploying a military rapid-reaction force for many years.  But it had become less reliable over the years and had seemed to spend more time ensuring that its needs were met than that Britain - in particular the west coast facing Ireland - was protected.

As Vortigern arrived at the fort, he was inundated with complaints about a string of recent incidents that had occurred with regard to the actions of Lucius Cornovius and his family.  Since Vortigern happened, at that moment, to be accompanied by the thousand warriors he was relocating to Cetgueli, he was in a very good position to summon Lucius Cornovius and demand an explanation.  Given the powers vested in him - as Emperor of Britain, backed by a small army - he had no problem in finding Cornovius guilty and sentencing him to banishment.

But being both cunning and aware that in these difficult times difficult men needed to be horded and used, Vortigern did not banish him from Britain altogether, but rather send him to Dumnonia with all his forces to build a state there and to dislodge the Irish who had invaded and settled in that part of the country.

In the winter of AD435, Voritgern looked around him and was relatively content with what he saw.  In the north, the Picts had been pushed back and were isolated by the presence of reinforced regional governors.  The Irish had been neutralized by religion and a timely marriage.  In the west, the Irish were in retreat in all the places where they had started landing and making settlements.  And in the east, the shore defences had been reinforced by having Saxons at key locations up and down the east coast.

What could go wrong?

---

"We demand that you disband them and send them home."

In London, in March 436, the Senate met for one of its obligatory meetings on the Ides.  There was no set agenda; the topic was simply "the good of the Republic".  Vortigern had given a swelling speech on how brilliantly his moves over the past five years had worked out, and was completely blindsided by the response.

The Senate was made up of misers.  Rich misers, to be sure: misers who could, probably, each afford the entire upkeep of the handful of Saxons that he had billeted down the east coast.  But now that things seemed secure, did they want to continue paying for that security?  They did not.

"Do you not understand?  Yes, we are paying them to protect us, but more than that, we are paying them to _not_ attack us.  What do you think will happen if we send them away?  They will be back here next year, with their friends and allies, but not as defenders of the Republic but as our enemies.  And they will win.  With what could we oppose them?

"Come to that, how can we oppose them _now_?  What resources, fellow senators, do you propose to deploy to evict foreign troops that are there _because you will not pay to raise our own?_

"You blame me for getting us into this mess by hiring Hengest to do our dirty work.  True, I did that.  But that did not get us into _this mess_.  We are actually in a very good position right now.  If you want the Saxons gone, you may, by all means start preparing now.  A general conscription will get us into a good place to field three legions next year, which would be enough to evict the Saxons, and then we could keep dismiss half of them - assuming they all lived - and keep fifteen cohorts on the east coast.  Or maybe we could do it without fighting and just pay the Saxons off to go home.  But you cannot just wish them away.  The world has changed, fellow senators."

"You are suggesting that we should pay for our defence not once but twice?  Are you further suggesting that we should pay for both sides of a civil war?  Are you insane?"

Vortigern took a deep breath, then another.

"No, I am not suggesting anything of the sort.  I am pointing out your options.  I respectfully _suggest_ that we carry on as we are.  We pay the Saxons for the defence they give us.  We continue to support and provision the other appointments and dispositions that I have made.  And I suggest that we try and avoid fighting amongst ourselves, against the Irish, or against the Saxons.  In fact, I propose that we _only_ fight against any invaders - be they Picts or barbarians from the continent."

"We refuse to pay.  I will see a division.  All those refusing to pay pass to the right, those who wish to continue to pay to have barbarians ruining our country pass to the left."

The Senators stood and, by a vast majority, passed to the right of the division stand.  Vortigern fumed, but there was little he could do.

---

"I do not understand."

To be honest, Vortigern could not say he was surprised.  The decision of the Senate - that they were happy for the Saxons to stay, but weren't prepared to pay them - did not really make any sense.  But it was a compromise; a decision that the Senate had taken that saved them their money - their real concern - but did not want the cost and risk of actually fighting to evict them.  And that was their only real choice if they wanted them to leave;  even the Senators were not so stupid as to think that the Saxons would leave their homes here to go back to the much rougher life on the continent because of anything as trivial as a decree from the Senate.

"They thank you for your service, and they want you to leave, but they are too cowardly to try.  They will not pay you to leave, even assuming that you would agree to leave for any sum of gold.  So they simply assume that if you do not get paid, you will leave of your own account."

"The fools think that?"

"They do indeed.  I have told them, I have reasoned with them, I have begged them.  But they will not listen.  They will not pay."

"I could make them pay.  Literally.  What or who stands between me and the wealth of the country?"

"Nothing, my friend.  They are fools to not see it, that is all I can say."

---

Ambrosius was worried.

He knew that if the British Senate continued their policy of refusing to pay the Saxons, they would eventually break out and take what they needed.  Vortigern had been cunning in where he had located his Saxon bases: on islands, in fens and along the coastline.  All the places were chosen for their strategic significance first, of course.  But after that, Vortigern had tried to isolate their ability to interact with the British as much as possible, to reduce the friction that having barbarians in their midst would undoubtedly cause.

The only option he could see was to prepare against that day.  While he did not have the authority of the Senate to issue a conscription or otherwise to raise troops, he rode around his heartland in the rich west downlands near of the Severn valley and talked to local leaders and freemen.  And he started to build a defence association against that day.  In towns and villages and in rural communities he built up a small army of infantry.

Not all the Senators were fools; the Knights even less so.  Many could see the dangers of not paying for the Saxons, but apart from anything else, none really felt that they could afford to do so "on their own" even if they could have subverted the Senatorial decree that said the Saxons were not to be paid.

---

In May of 437, Hengest broke out from Thanet Island.  With more troops that he "officially" had, either from the continent or other stations, he crossed Kent and marched towards the west, determined to plunder the rich lands there, unaffected by previous conflict and strife and protected over the past century from Saxon, Irish and Pict raids.

Vortigern rode down from London to try and deter him and was staggered by the size of the army he had gathered: thousands if he could judge it.  He hailed Hengest and tried to convince him to turn around.

"You, my friend, have always dealt fairly with me", he said through his interpreter Ceretic.  "But your Senate respects not me.  I have no quarrel with you, but with them.  And if they wish not to pay me, I will go and take payment and teach them lesson."

"But you could expose the whole country to being overrun by other barbarians."

"What barbarians?  My countrymen are with me, my cousin gives the Picts enough to do, and your Irish friends are peaceful.  Where come these barbarians?"

Vortigern could not argue with this.  In fact, to tell the truth, he felt that Hengest was in the right of it.

Eventually, they agreed a deal.  Vortigern would ride west with Hengest and his army.  And they would "negotiate in strength" with the Senators of the west.  Vortigern sent riders ahead to summon the Senate to meet at Durnovaria on the Kalends of June.

---

When Ambrosius first heard that Hengest had left Thanet Island, he sent riders across the south west and told all his followers to get ready for battle and meet at Aquae Sulis as soon as possible.  Once there, they started to move eastwards, and were already past Durnovaria when they received Vortigern's missive; Ambrosius dismissed it.

"This is an illegal invasion and the time for talking is done.  We will proceed and tell them that they must turn around and go back to whence they came.  Only then can we discuss the subject of payments."

The two forces met at Guoloppum.  Ambrosius, like Vortigern, was staggered at the number of troops that Hengest had with him.  He had not realized so many were in Britain.

The three leaders met in the middle of the field with a handful of retainers including the  translator, Ceretic.

"Ambrosius, this is not Durnovaria.  Nor is it yet the Kalends.  But that is where you were summoned."

"Vortigern, these men have left their station without a decree from the Senate.  That is an illegal act.  They must turn around at go home."

"They have not been paid, Ambrosius.  They understandably come to demand payment."

"They _all_ needed to come?  And bring some friends who are not even officially on the list?"

"They were concerned that a smaller delegation might not impress upon the Senate the urgency and the, uh, seriousness of the situation."

"The seriousness of the situation being that any Senator who _didn't_ vote to pay them might find his head detached from his shoulders?  Is this what you have become, Vitalinus?  A bloodthirsty tyrant?"

"Me?  No.  I am with Hengest here because I could not - by myself - dissuade him from continuing and I thought it best to try and maintain order in this march."

"By which you mean, stop your men running amok?"

Vortigern smiled.

"Well, they may not pass."

"You would fight us?"

"I would rather not.  But they may not pass.  I have considered this long and hard, Vitalinus, and I cannot allow this to continue.  My preference has always been for us to defend our lands ourselves.  If we cannot, then I accept the need to pay these Volk to do it for us.  But I cannot accept them ruling us.  If the men return to their base on Thanet Island, we can summon the Senate at Rutupiae, and there we can discuss this in detail and resolve the matter to everyone's satisfaction."

Ceretic had been translating and trying to keep up, but summarizing.  But Hengest had understood the import of Ambrosius' speech.

He interrupted loudly, his gutteral speech lost on the others until Ceretic clarified.

"No.  No, that we will not do.  You trick us, you say you pay us and then you pay us not.  What can you prove?  Huh?  Nothing.  No, we settle this here and now.  You fight, or you get out of road.  Understand?"

Hengest had already turned around and stomped off to rejoin his army before Ceretic had finished his translation.  He scurried after his master.

Vortigern looked at Ambrosius and shrugged.  "In other times, and in other ways, I would consider you brave and a friend.  But here you are being foolish.  Your rabble cannot win against them."

They could not win, and Ambrosius went down to defeat.  But the battle was costly for the Saxons too.  Although the held the field, there was no desire to carry on.  Voritgern and Hengest did a new, private deal:  Vortigern ceded Hengest the control of Kent.  Instead of billeting the men as he had until now, he gave Hengest the powers of a regional governor as he had to his cousin north of the wall and to the governors in the far west of the country.

For a while, Hengest was satisfied and returned to Kent.
