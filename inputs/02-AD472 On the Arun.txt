title=On the Arun

&date AD472

The mist was seeping up from the river.

To the left, they could hear some geese clacking, oblivious to the actions of the men nearby.  Soon the dawn would come, and with it the noise of war.  The geese would fly then.  Hopefully so would the Saxons.

Arthur peered into the mist, trying to divine something from what was little more than reflected moonlight in the dark.  The moon was past full and just setting behind them, as the sun would soon rise in front of them, giving the enemy a precious advantage if they allowed them to take it.  Assuming the enemy were here.

They probably were.  Over the past decade as the British had started to fight back, fords like this were common battle sites.  The river was the "accepted" border although Arthur swore that he would never accept it, never accept that this land was no longer Britain but "SudSexe" as the barbarians called it.  He would rather die than give in to the final destruction of that which he held dearest: the past that had passed before he had been born, but was still, in some sense, preserved and alive in the magnificent books he had read as a child: Cicero, Sulla, Rutilius Rufus, Pompeius, Caesar, Seneca, Tacitus, Severus.  The great men who had understood what it was to be great and yet to be but a small part of something infinitely greater: Rome.

He shook his head slightly to bring himself back to the moment.  A mistake here could cost him his life; more importantly, it could cost the lives of his men.  Men, who, even though many of them were old than him, trusted him implicitly; not because he had been placed over them but because he was the man with the birth, the rank and "the spirit".  A concept he knew that Aelle, somewhere out there but probably not just across the river, would never be able to understand.

From his right came a bird honk that, although fairly natural-sounding, Arthur knew to be out of place.  Quintus had never been able to quite manage the delicate sound of the river heron.  Of course, if you didn't know what to listen for, weren't actively listening to hear it be wrong, be out of place, then you probably wouldn't notice.  Arthur hoped that nobody across the river was listening that closely.  Certainly nobody had moved yet.

Time dripped on; slowly the sky in the east was starting to turn from black to grey; the mist was becoming more substantial, less ethereal.  It would give them some cover but nothing like enough once the horses starting splashing across the ford.  Time to move?  He was waiting for Quintus to call again, confirm that he too saw nothing waiting for them.  Saw nothing at all in this mist, more like.

Arthur tapped Morcant's shoulder next to him.  Morcant made the sound of a crow that alerted the band to be ready and to saddle up.  The horses were a hundred paces back from the ford; even the best-trained horse will occasionally make a noise and that could be fatal at this range.  Arthur and his scouts hurried back and found the other men already saddled and waiting to go in the slowly glistening light.

Arthur considered saying something but everyone knew the plan; everyone had been here before, done this before, survived before.  Saying anything would only betray his own nervousness and fear that maybe they would never do it again.  That maybe on the other side of the river, Aelle had drawn up all his forces in battle array and were waiting for them to cross.  You scouted, you tested, you probed; but until you were in that water you never knew for sure what was going to happen.

Getting back to his horse, Arthur grabbed the saddle with both hands and threw himself up and onto its back.  Some men needed tree stumps - or even help from their friends - to get up onto a horse but Arthur was skilled enough - and young enough - to be able to jump up into the saddle.  He made it a requirement that all the men in his unit could do the same thing.

If it was dark out by the riverbank, here under the trees it was almost pitch black.  With the moon setting behind them, even that faint light was cut off.  By touch Arthur checked his weapons: the long horse-spear that he had heard the Gauls had lengthened again and were now calling a 'lance'; the two javelins that would be needed against defended positions; the cavalry sword, longer than the infantry gladius and with a cutting edge for slashing down at infantry stuck on the ground; and the small, round shield that he rarely used since it was only effective against other cavalry.  Against infantry, the bigger danger was damage to his legs or his horse.

Slowly, they advanced towards the river.  Until they were actually in the water, splashing across the ford, the priority was quiet.  Hopefully, nobody even knew they were coming.  Maybe there wasn't even anybody there.

To the right, he knew that Quintus would be leading his flanking column down to the ford, while to the left, Lucullus would be leading his flankers.  They had practised this enough, and done this enough, that he knew they would be there without looking.

Suddenly, they were at the bank; turning to Morcant, he gave the signal to attack and spurred his horse into a stream.  As if nature itself responded to his signal, suddenly all was confusion in the lightening gloom.  To his left the geese honked and erupted into the sky.  From across the river came the unmistakable sounds of infantrymen reading swords, shields and bows.  Before he was halfway across the ford, the first arrows came threading their way among the charging horses; undeterred, they kept moving.

With a crash they broke through the brush on the far side of the ford.  Behind the brush were two earthworks, one either side of the primitive track that led to the ford.  While ineffective against an organised infantry assault, they were sufficient to deter a horse charge.

With effortless decision making, Arthur led his band straight through the middle, as archers from both sides let fly at his men.  In running the gauntlet, he took a risk, but gained a much superior position.  To start with, he had no actual need to stand here and fight these men; they had been placed here to deter the kind of river crossing he had just attempted and, once he was through, they had failed in their job.  If he simply carried on, they would be forced to choose between holding their current position in safety but settling for just raising the alarm, or chasing after him and putting themselves at a tactical disadvantage.

But plundering and burning lands "occupied" by the Saxons was not Arthur's chief concern.  It was necessary, of course, after a fashion, and certainly easier, but his main drive was to kill Saxon warriors.  It was harder, but it was the only way to make Britain back to the way it should be.

He pulled up, wheeled his horse and signalled to his men to follow suit.  The timing now was critical.  His flanking troopers would, by now, have crossed the river - slower than him due to not having the help of the ford - and be picking their way through the woods to  make it back to the ford.  Pulling out into three files, the horses charged, each of the men pulling his first spear to throw into the defended positions.

It was lighter now and as they charged the sun started to lift above the horizon behind them; hopefully it would shine brightly into the eyes of the men facing them and make it harder to aim their missiles.

As they came back to the defensive positions, the three files split open like a flower.  Arthur led the central column back down the narrow path to the ford, while his men on either side ran across the back of each of the positions, each launching a javelin at a target as they went.

Just as the unit to the south had cleared the area, Quintus crashed through the undergrowth and started a frontal assault from the south side of the enemy position.  Dozens of spears clattered over the earth-and-timber wall, striking defending Saxons and bouncing off defensive timbers.

Arthur's column was trapped between the two embrasures and the river but they had known that was going to happen.  With a precision that could only come from hours of practice from their homes in the West Country, they wheeled and rode back through the narrow channel between the embrasures, drawing and throwing their second spears as they passed.

Now Lucullus arrived from the north, his men dispatching their spears into the northern embrasure before rolling around to join Arthur's men on the track.  As he did so, Quintus disengaged from the southern embrasure and joined Arthur as well.  Reunited, the thirty-strong band rode down the track looking for farms to burn and halls to torch.

Such was life on the border of Saxon England.

---

It always seemed a lot further going back to the West Country.  Of
course, a lot of that was simply because coming, they would make a
stealthy camp close to the border to spend the night, but on the
return journey they had to move away from the border lands as quickly
as possible, and by the time you were far enough from the border to
feel safe and make camp, you might as well carry on back to the
halfway fort.

Arthur was based at the impressive fort of Mons Badonicus, but that
was much too far to ride in a single day; all the more so after the
horses had been in action for much of the day.  So, both outbound and
return, his troop would stop overnight at Clausentum.

Today they were weary and dispirited.  After the initial engagement,
they had spent the morning burning and destroying, but had failed to
find any Saxon soldiers willing to dispute the territory or come and
be killed.  While the halls they had destroyed, and the crops they had
burnt would inconvenience the Saxons come winter, Arthur and his men
all knew that the people that would suffer the brunt of the attacks
would be fellow Britons.

Sometimes they were lucky and the Saxons even chased them across the
river into their own territory.  Without horses, they were unable to
keep up and presented no threat; nevertheless, Arthur had the
opportunity to set an ambush and possibly kill some of them when they
were not behind their fortifications.  Of course, there were other
times when they came and Arthur and his men were not around; then it
was British farms that would be put to the torch.

As they finally arrived back at Badonicus the following evening, the
sun was starting to set and they began the weary climb up through the
fortifications.  As always, Arthur marvelled at these works, built
before the coming of the Romans and left deserted for hundreds of
years before being pressed back into service as the countryside once
again became dangerous and full of bandits.

A voice pulled him out of his reverie.  "Ho, Arthur!" exclaimed
Quintus Ambrosius Aurelianus, officially his general - and possibly
emperor - but to Arthur much more an avuncular figure from his
childhood and "youth" - if you can look back on your youth when you
have just turned 20.

"Good to see you managed not to lose anybody in the forests," joked
Aurelianus.  He usually made some joke such as this when one of the
units came back from a raid.  It was his way of pretending that the
tension they all felt back at base was just about the possibility of a
simple accident; not the all too likely prospect of a man being
fatally wounded by an arrow.

Arthur leapt down from his horse and gave the man a bear hug.
Aurelianus had given Arthur his first sword, taught him to ride and
done a thousand other things for him, all while single-handedly
pulling Britain back from the edge of the abyss.  How he had done it -
how he still did it at that - Arthur could never comprehend.  Even
with the tireless vigour of youth, Arthur was worn out by these
raids.  To have to come back from one to plan another and sort out a
thousand details of the life of a whole community was beyond his
imagination.  Much more to have time for a boy who was more interested
in his books.

"Quintus thinks we may have killed a couple of their sentries at the
border crossing, but other than that the raid was a failure."  Arthur
looked at the ground, unable to meet the eye of this man when relating
the destruction of British, rather than Saxon, property.  "We burnt
out a number of farms, and destroyed two of the halls the Saxons had
built, but nobody came to challenge us.  We came back round to the
south and found that ford wasn't even defended.  It's almost like they
don't care about whether we come and attack anymore."

"Maybe they don't, at that, young Artorius.  You still have much to
learn about the world for all your book-reading and feats of arms.
They know as well as we do that it's stalemate as long as they can
stay in defensible positions and we have the monopoly on horses.
It'll be a sad day when a Saxon finally decides to mount a horse."

"All the more reason for us to beat them sooner," replied Arthur.  "We
can't afford to let them develop cavalry and dominate us on the
battlefield.  We need to get them to come out and fight us."

"That's easily said, young man, easily said.  And don't think we
haven't tried.  But your Saxon knows which side of his bread he's put
that greasy butter on.  He's not going to come out and fight us at a
disadvantage, and the more land they can hold the more safe areas they
have that they can hold on to for supply."

They walked back up the hill together side by side, Arthur leading his
tired horse back to its stall.  As the passed the sentries, Arthur
greeted each of them by name, unconsciously recognizing them and
thanking them for their service.  Without secure bases like these, the
resistance would have frittered away years ago.
