&date AD478

It felt like a distraction, but it needed dealing with.

The Saxons had originally come to Britain to help defend it against
the Picts, but ever since the massacre they had been uninterested in
that task and solely focused on the task of carving out kingdoms for
themselves.

The regional governors that Theodosius had appointed during his
administration there - and their hereditary successors - had done a
good job on the whole holding back the wilder Picts by assembling
confederations of their less-warlike, and more easily influenced,
lowland cousins.

But now - probably because they had heard Ambrosius was dead and
fancied their chances with a new, unproven leader in charge - they had
attacked en masse and Gaius Quintilius Clemens - great-grandson of the
original Clemens placed in charge by Theodosius - had sent emergency
dispatch riders south to Arthur requesting help.

Arthur's immediate reaction had been to gather up all his forces and
set off for the north, but even as he thought it, a vision of Merula's
face came into his head and he knew that first and foremost he must
make a calm and rational decision.

So he did what Merula would have done.  He thought.  Deeply.

---

The council meeting was not going well.

Officially, of course, all policy was decided by the Senate and the
bureaucracy and whatever, but in reality, that system had faded with
the ascendancy of Vortigern fifty years ago.  Arthur was not "the
emperor" and could not decide things by fiat.  But the decisions that
were made here would be respected wherever the title "Dux Bellorum"
held sway.  And anywhere it didn't, they would try to kill Arthur on
sight.

"I cannot believe that you are seriously suggesting this, Arthur,"
huffed Titus Fulvis.  "The men may have granted you the authority, but
that does not mean that you acquired the brains or the experience with
it."

Arthur breathed in, deeply but calmly, trying both to control and
conceal his inner rage.  How could they not understand that what he
was proposing was the _only_ thing that could work.  A risk?
Certainly.  But a risk was better than certain failure.

"Let us ask ourselves, what would Caesar do?"

"Are you now comparing yourself to Caesar?  Is that who you think you
are?  The greatest military genius ever?"

"No, no, I'm not.  But we can at least agree," he paused for emphasis,
"that Caesar _was_ a great military genius and thus," he paused again,
"worthy of emulation.  Is he not?"

Fulvius said nothing, which at the moment felt like progress.

"So I am suggesting that we consider what he would do in this
situation."

"The unexpected."

Almost as unexpected was this intervention by Mardoc.  Arthur was both
surprised and pleased at the comment, since it was exactly the point
he had been trying to make.

"That's what you're trying to say, Arthur.  That Caesar would do
something unexpected, something that would appear rash; and yet, in
hindsight appeared blindingly obvious.  So obvious even the couch
generals in Rome would say afterwards that they would have done the
same thing."

"Yes, that's what I'm trying to say, Mardoc.  Yes, the plan rests upon
its being unexpected, but at the same time, there really is no
choice.  It will work because it's not expected; but it is the right
thing to do because it _will_ work."

"But this plan to strip Linnius of its garrison.  That's all that's
holding back the tide of Saxons from overrunning the middle of the
country and cutting off our communications with York," objected Titus
Fulvius.  "Is that worth it to repel these barbarians?"

"Repel?  Maybe not," admitted Arthur.  "But that's not the plan."

"You're _not_ planning to rebel the invaders," mused Mardoc.  "Yes,
that would be unexpected.  Why not?"

"Because I am going to go north and invade their homelands and put
them to the torch.  And I am going to make them _want_ to run home
with their tails between their legs.  And never come south again.  But
if I'm north of the wall ... who will reinforce the Dux York?"

There was a stunned silence in the council hall for a long while.

---

The main advantage of the good network of Roman roads around Britain
was the system of post riders which enabled rapid communications.
By the following morning both the Dux York and the Comes at Linnius
knew what was expected of them and, although he didn't like his part
in the plan - which everybody acknowledged would mean the probable
loss of Linnius if not the whole of the middle of Britain - recognized
that executing a disliked plan well was better than botching the job.

The plan was a complicated one and, apart from Arthur, nobody knew all
the details.  The first part of the plan called for the Picts to _not_
know that Arthur was riding north with all speed; but required them at
the right moment to find out in a way that did not obviously make it
seem like a trap.

At the same time, the combined British forces had to simultaneously
support the overall plan while being in ignorance of much of it.
Making sure the right people were told the right stories at the right
time was as much a part of the plan as planning "the battle" itself.

Much would come down to what Arthur found once north of the wall, but
he had read everything that there was to read from the copies of
Theodosius' dispatches and reports to an amateur Pliny's attempts at
describing the geography of the north of the island - mixed in with a
few amusing anecdotes about being chased through the glens by the
Picts.

The key, of course, would be to deploy his forces in such a way that
he could win a total victory over the enemy on his home turf - and
then get away.  While he wouldn't claim to be the equal of Julius
Caesar, it was a plan that might well have taxed the old man had he
been here.

Arthur himself took all his mounted soldiers from Badonicus and sent
for reinforcements from Gloucester and made a rendezvous at Chester
where he collected yet more reinforcements.  He had allowed ten days
for the ride to Carlisle and it was going slowly, Roman roads or no.
It wasn't the same if you couldn't change horses every fifteen miles
at the post stations.

A couple of days slippage and the timings would start to fall apart.
Too much of that, and the whole plan could start to unravel.

---

Outside York, the Picts continued to encamp and threaten the city and
fort.  They couldn't get in, and they probably wouldn't be able to
starve the citizens out.  But there were always the two imponderables:
luck and treachery.

Inside, the Dux knew that the next stage of Arthur's plan was about to
go into effect.  Arthur himself was supposed to be coming, but hadn't
arrived yet.  The Comes of Linnius had sent messages to say that _he_
at least was coming and had sent messages to Arthur to find out where
he was.  The message that had come back was discouraging and indicated
that Arthur was distracted fighting Saxons on the road but would be
there when he could and to relieve the city as soon as possible.

Being barbarians, the Picts underrated the value of communications and
"letting one or two people in or out".  So the Dux and the Comes were
able to keep in pretty much constant contact over those long days of
preparations.

So it was that in the early morning, the 22nd of May, AD 478, the
Picts were surprised, to say the least, to find the northern army
issuing out of the city gates in battle array.  Formed up in four
squares, the overstrength legion drove into the centre of a boiling
mass of barbarians: the standard infantry tactics that had won the
Romans an empire for over a thousand years.  An outward-facing metal
box which flipped open from time to time to issue pointed swords that
stabbed, wounded, killed and then pulled back to make a solid wall.

The tactics didn't always work.  If they did, none of Arthur's careful
planning or reinforcements would have been necessary.  This legion
could have gone into action any time in the past month.  But on this
occasion, the barbarians held enough cards that, given time, they
would be able to grind down this too-small force.  Even overstrength,
one legion in such relatively open territory could not deal with this
horde of 50,000 or more barbarians.

But what would always, always work was a sneak attack from behind.
What they had failed to realize was that the Dux's army was just a
lure: a feint for the cavalry charge that seemingly came from
everywhere at once.

And suddenly there they were.  In the midst of a boiling mass of
barbarians, the Comes of Linnius and his eight regiments - twenty-four
centuries - of cavalry charged from all points of the compass and
drove the Picts to scattered flight.

The first phase of the plan had succeeded.  The combined army now
needed to herd the Picts north.

Maybe Arthur hadn't been needed after all.

---

The men came running out of the house screaming.

Arthur had crossed the wall at Carlisle and started heading east.  
Once
across the border he had sent messages to <the other regional 
governor> to
coordinate his actions and find who was friend and who was with the
enemy.  Wherever he had found enemy or enemy sympathizers, he had 
burnt
farmsteads, rounded up livestock and killed indiscriminately.  This
was, in the words of the military tactician Agrippa, a "punishment
expedition".  There was no intent to calm, to control or to hold.  No 
effort to "win hearts and minds".  The
sole intent of this incursion north of the border was to inflame and
enrage the locals into either fighting him at a place of his choosing,
or deciding right now that they would never fight him, ever again.  He
didn't really care which, as long as they feared him and his ability 
to destroy
their livelihoods.

"Kill them," he shouted to his men and quickly Quintus took a squad 
of eight off, using
their lances to skewer the three unarmed men.  Their house continued 
to burn and Arthur and his
men watched until they were content that nobody was left inside.

"Move out," he ordered and they formed up and rode off.

Not every house, barn and farm was destroyed; there simply wasn't the 
time.  But across a five-mile front, the three columns of men burnt 
and destroyed everything that they came across and killed anybody 
they could find who didn't have the sense - or ability - to flee or 
find a secure hiding place.  Each one killed here was one less to be 
killed in battle, although, if he were to be totally honest, Arthur 
would have had to admit that there were almost no warriors here.  
They had all headed south - the only thing that had made this 
punishment raid possible.

"Come on, come on," he called and spurred eastwards.

---

Arthur knew that his four regiments were not much to take on the 
might of the Pict nation,
but he also knew that numbers were not everything.  After all, he was 
facing a mad, enraged enemy
who had recently been defeated and felt betrayed, who were returning 
from an expected rout
to face their homes burning and who just wanted blood.  Men who 
balked at planning even when
they had all winter to do it.   _Divide et Impera_ as the saying 
went.  Confidence might be a two-edged
sword, but confidence in your own competence was a different matter.  
And he and his men _were_ competent; they knew what they were doing 
and had a good plan.

It was a simple plan: break the enemy up into small, ineffective 
units round them up, encircle them and run them down.  In some ways, 
it couldn't fail.  It was just a question of killing and demoralizing 
enough of them that he could retreat back south of the wall without 
running into the same challenge they had right now: being in 
enemy-held territory in full retreat.

The key was finding the right ground to fight this battle.  Too open 
and the numbers could possibly count against him; too wooded and his 
cavalry would be unable to deploy.  The right ground would enable him 
enough room to deploy while keeping the battlefield broken up.  A 
hill would be good.  In a forest.  With gentle slopes broken up by 
defiles.  Where?  Where?

He kept riding and kept looking.

---

"This one speaks British."

He referred of course to the native language of Britain.  Arthur 
spoke it fluently, as did most of the "Romans" although they 
generally spoke Latin to other civilized people.  Many of the British 
peasants only spoke the language, and while they spoke a related 
language, most of the Picts that they had come across were 
unintelligible.  If he'd had the time to properly prepare this 
campaign, he would have tried to find someone who spoke the language 
as an interpreter.  They had several who spoke Saxon back at 
Badonicus, but neither there nor either of his brief stops - in 
Chester and Carlisle - had he managed to track down a native Pict 
speaker.

Arthur rode over, eager to interrogate him.

"You know who we are?"  A nod.  "You know what we are doing here?"  
Another nod.  He might speak some British but was either 
uncomfortable in the language or just not a great communicator.  I 
wonder if he has any friends, Arthur thought absently.

"We are looking for a place.  A clearing in the woods.  We want a 
hill and we want it craggy.  Can you tell us of a place?"

"Why?  Why help I you?  You kill us.  You burn."

True enough, thought Arthur.  But you'll help us because you won't 
have any choice.

"Do you want to die?  Painfully?  You can tell us what we want to 
know and live; or hold it from us and die painfully, right here, 
right now."

The man seemed to consider this.  It really wasn't much of a choice.

"And we will probably find what we need anyway."

The man looked at Arthur, hate burning in his eyes.

"Ride north," he said.  "Two miles, three, the woods end.  You'll 
know.  What you want, I don't know.  But it's there.  Hill in the 
woods.  Grassy.  Cait Celidon."

Arthur and Quintus exchanged glances.  He might be lying.  He might 
not.  But it was enough to save his life.  If he was lying and they 
came back this way, they would kill him.

Arthur waved the men forward, riding north.

---

They burst out of the treeline.

They had been riding uphill for the past hour or so.  Certainly more 
than two or three miles and Arthur had increasingly worried that the 
man had lied.  But the uphill had been good news: ride far enough 
uphill and you will get to the summit.  Only then will you know if it 
is clear or wooded.

But as they came out into the weak afternoon sunshine, Arthur 
marvelled.  The terrain was perfect.  Ahead was the crest, a pointed 
crag.  Between him and the summit was a natural breastwork: oh, yes, 
his men could spend the next two days readying for the battle 
improving it, but it was exactly what he needed.  To the left and 
right, arms splayed out, making the approach look tempting - until 
you found yourself funnelled in with no room to move.

And then the forest, so close and ideal for deploying the rear-attack 
ambush.

Only one challenge remaining: get them to come here.

He signalled to the bugler who sounded "Form up, parade square" over 
and over.  His men, spread over miles, would take an hour or more to 
assemble after hearing the signal to assemble on the bugler, but he 
wasn't going to wait.  His personal command generally had the easiest 
of it, so he would get them to work straight away.

"Quintus - that embrasure: it will be stronger than the walls of Rome 
by sundown day after next.  Take the first half of this regiment and 
get digging.  I'll get you relief tomorrow, but for tonight you're 
it.  Dig all night, we don't have time to waste.  Don't forget to 
pile up any useful-sized rocks you hack up or move for the artillery.

"Cato - supplies.  There has to be food out there.  Go looking for 
it; split up if you can but _do not_ get isolated, trapped or killed. 
 They'll be out there somewhere and my nose says they'll be here in 
two or three days, so take care.  When you've taken everything you 
can find, burn everything but _don't_ kill anyone.  Tell them to go 
south and if they come north we _will_ kill them.  I want them to be 
a signal to all the men coming north.  And make sure you spread out 
in a funnel and get those fires to burn high and bright.

"Cornelius - take two squads and get hurrying south.  Don't bother 
about burning anything close to here; Cato has that covered.  I want 
to know when and where the enemy are and when they're coming.  
Quintus doesn't need his horses for now, so use those to 
set up a basic pony express between here and wherever you get to.  
And the same as for Cato: _don't_ get cut off; _don't_ get killed.  
We're few enough as it is; we need every man we've got when they 
finally come up here.

"Caecilius - we need artillery.  Plenty of wood down here in the 
forest.  Take from there and there," he said, pointing.  "We'll keep 
those other areas dense for hiding the cavalry and we'll get the 
skirmishers to lead the Picts in through the central area.  Clear?  
Four trebuchets and eight scorpions should do the trick.  And lots of 
missiles.  Also, work with Quintus to prepare trebuchet rounds.  
Good."

He looked around.  In every direction the men were moving to their 
assigned tasks.  No haste, no panic; just quiet efficiency.  They 
were Romans; they knew what to do.

---

Three days later, the landscape was transformed.  Many of the trees 
at the edge of the forest had been chopped down; the embrasure had 
been turned into a fortress with a six-foot ditch and a twenty-foot 
high wall.  The wall reached between the two spurs of rock coming 
down to east and west.  Two gates were placed in the wall close to 
either end.  The eastern gate was closed and barred already, while 
the western gate was wide open with a "bridge" of planed pine logs 
laid across it.  Ditches had been dug at what seemed random angles 
across the valley and "enhanced" with timber walls.  Range markers 
had been placed all over the valley, their purpose obscure to anybody 
who had not done the requisite basic Roman army training.

Arthur sat his horse in the middle of the valley and looked around.  
Yes, everything was ready.  The enemy was audible now, coming through 
the forest.  Quintus' skirmishers were leading them here, apparently 
retreating from the enemy's superior force.  Meanwhile the bugler was 
sounding call after call, seemingly to organize the retreat but in 
reality just to make sure that even the laggards in the Pict army 
kept coming the right way.

Cato looked at the fort.  "It's impressive.  We would be able to hold 
that for weeks with enough supplies."

"That's the way it's supposed to look," said Arthur.  "I wonder 
whether they'll bother to dig in themselves, or just assume their 
numbers are enough and just settle down in the valley?"

"Against one hundred men?  I would expect them just to try a frontal 
assault."

Arthur turned and stared at Cato.  "Really?"  He looked at the 
fortification again.  "Really?  Down a six foot trench, lined with 
obstacles and then up a thirty foot sheer cliff?  Are you insane?"

"No," replied Cato, controlled and sober.  "But they might be."

Arthur looked at him and nodded.  He had made a fair point.  Time to 
go.  Arthur rode down and to the west; Cato rode to the east.  All 
would probably be decided in the next few hours.

---

Cornelius led his men at a full gallop across the valley.  If they 
were shocked by its transformation, they gave no hint of it.  Quintus 
took in the range and trail markers at a glance and unerringly led 
his men through one of the prepared cavalry routes to the top.  Once 
there, his men went over the lowered pine bridge through the west 
gate.
When all his twenty-four men were across, the bridge was pulled in, 
the gate closed and secured.

The first Picts had come charging out into the valley, chasing the 
retreating men.  As they burst out of the trees, they stopped, 
involuntarily.  Surely they had just left the fortifications at York? 
 And yet, here was a defensive position almost as strong as the one 
they had left behind - in the middle of their homeland.  This was 
intolerable.

And then, almost as one, they shook off the shock and charged.  
Across the prepared ground.

---

"Wait for it, wait for it, you bloodsuckers.  Wait for them to pass 
the red ones."  Quintus had his eyes on the range markers as did all 
the defenders.  Some were manning the walls, but they had the easy 
job for now, especially since the gates had been closed.  The 
artillery was where the action was.

The Picts were lost in confusion in the battlefield.  If you knew the 
routes, or could read the markers, there were four fairly straight 
routes between the forest and the fortress.  But if you didn't know 
what you were doing, it could almost seem like you were on islands in 
the midst of a volcano.  And the Picts had no clue.

For Quintus, the key to the defence was to allow as many Picts onto 
the battlefield as possible without actually allowing them the 
opportunity to properly deploy, dig in, organize or attack.  
Basically, to keep them on their toes and stop them from being able 
to concentrate on attacking his position, which was strong, but not 
invincible.  A well-disciplined attack force could fill in the 
ditches in the valley, chop down their own wood to build a ramp or 
siege towers, or flank their position up either of the mountain 
spurs.  Quintus didn't think that any of these things were in his 
enemies' minds, but it was better to stop anything occuring to them.

The leading men had made it through to being within range, past the 
red markers.  There was still no clear organization out there - not 
that Quintus was expecting any.  But they were close enough now to 
start charging and possibly hurling missiles.  The time had come.

"Trebuchets, release."  Men cut the ends of the cords and the four 
giant machines launched their missiles.  They arced gracefully over 
the battlefield and crashed down amidst the horde of men on the 
field, just at they'd planned it.  The crews immediately went to work 
winding them back up, reattaching the cables and loading the next 
projectile.

"Scorpions.  As you mark."  The scorpions, more direct and accurate 
than the trebuchets, were really like giant bows.  They had ranged 
off in practice and knew exactly where they would fire.  No point 
shooting if there was nobody standing right there.  The eight guns 
fired as the opportunities presented themselves and rapidly reloaded; 
started a continual rain of missiles across the battlefield.  I hope 
we made enough, thought Quintus.  You really could never have too 
many.

---

The battle was starting to be joined now.

It still wasn't really a fair fight, and from Arthur's vantage point 
in the forest, it wasn't really clear whether it was a fight or just 
a lot of men milling around waiting to be killed.  The leading 
elements were at the ditch now, caught in the classic dilemma: do I 
go down when I want to go up?  or do I stay here, a target but almost 
unable to do anything about it.

This is where the intelligent man presumably thinks to himself, "I 
should have brought something to put in this ditch, or across it," he 
thought.  The prepared man, of course, would in fact have brought 
something.  For himself, Arthur wouldn't have gone anywhere near that 
fortification for a couple of days.  But then, if they weren't 
barbarians, they would be part of Roman Britain and not fighting us.  
And then the Saxons wouldn't be here and we would all be happy.

His biggest concern was that all the men had gathered here now.  No 
big deal if a couple of hundred stragglers were left behind; they 
would probably be the weakest and least brave and would run away 
rather than fight.  But a couple of thousand men in his rear when he 
attacked _could_ be a problem.  He wanted to ask for help - he'd love 
to know what Quintus thought from his much better vantage point - but 
this was his command, his battle - his decision.

"Julius, what would you do?" he murmured, then took his life in his 
hands, literally.

"Charge!"

The men burst out of the forest in three lines; the formation Arthur 
had decided on within minutes of planning this battle and that they 
had practised four times a day since.  No battle ever went exactly 
according to plan, and Arthur had no delusions that this one would be 
any different.  But they were ready; they knew what they were doing; 
and the ground had been prepared.  He looked to his right and saw 
Cato leading the charge from the forest to the east.  Good man! he 
thought.  To have used the bugle would have been to give away 
something happening in the rear, and that he could not afford.  Cato 
would have known the situation just like Arthur did, but he still had 
to wait for Arthur to move before moving himself.

He felt the shock as his lance crashed into the back of the first man 
as he rode up behind him, the full weight of the horse behind the 
thrust.  He stopped and pulled the lance back out and charged again.  
All around him his men were doing the same, Picts dying without 
knowing why or how.  They were turning now, starting to ready 
themselves, full of blood lust but not organized.  Never organized.  
Arthur's men knew what they were about, knew the ground, knew their 
tactics.  Some died under lance and spear, some were pushed into the 
gullies and ditches, and others died under the relentless hail of 
missiles from the wall.

---

Obviously, the battle could not be won by cavalry alone.

But that had never been the plan.  The plan was to use the cavalry to 
bring chaos to the battlefield and to _turn_ the enemy around.  The 
bravest men, the strongest, the most daring, the most talented, all 
moved to the front.  They were the men facing Quintus; the men at the 
back were making up the numbers: too young, too old, too weak, too 
cowardly to be real soldiers.  Arthur cut them down like the sheep 
they were and in six lines cut his way through the prepared routes.  
He was vulnerable to an attack from behind - if the Picts still had 
enough brave enough men in the woods - but he was also vulnerable to 
his own success: sooner or later he would cut through enough of the 
fodder to get to the real warriors.  Time to retreat.

The bugler sounded "fall back; reform fifty yards" and the men fell 
back in good order.  No rush, no chaos.  The pressure relieved, the 
Picts started to re-form themselves and to form a good battle line.  
Given time, they would learn, Arthur knew, and resolved to not give 
them time.  Another trebuchet strike came down in the midst of the 
enemy, adding to the chaos.

Now the leading men were pushing through the horde, trying to form up 
and get to grips with an enemy not behind twenty-foot ramparts.  But 
their way was hampered by the men in between, the ditches and the 
continual artillery strikes.  But they were successful in clearing 
away the area immediately in front of the walls.

Quintus gave the signal and the two gates were opened in unison and 
the bridges lowered across them.  All the men not working the 
artillery ran down the bridges on the double and formed a shield wall 
across the neck of the battlefield.

And the classic Roman infantry meat-grinding machine went to work.

---

In the end, Arthur and Cato were forced to sound "run away" - that 
is, full retreat to prepared positions.  The pressure from the 
retreating Picts had become so intense that it was about to turn to 
rout and that would have cost Arthur too many men.  But "run away" 
did not mean they were giving in; rather, that they were retreating 
to prepared positions to the sides of the ravine.  From here, they 
would watch any Picts who were truly running away head south; but if 
any tried to form up and resist, they would charge in skirmish order, 
stopping effective resistance and driving the men south.  Not that 
Arthur really wanted them to go back south - the way his men would 
need to go themselves - but it was the only way out of this trap.

And flee the men did.  As the pressure from in front was released, 
and the meat-grinder kept grinding from behind, men threw away 
swords, shields, packs, armour - anything that would slow them down 
as they ran away.

As the last of the Picts fled, Quintus and Arthur greeted each other 
on the bloody battlefield.  "I would say we should clean this mess 
up," said Arthur.  "But I feel the need to get back to Linnius before 
it becomes Saxon."
