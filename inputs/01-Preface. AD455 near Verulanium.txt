title=Preface

&date AD455

Artorius greeted Ambrosius warmly.

The atrium of the villa was massive, its domed roof and giant floor 
mosaic dwarfing the guests.  Like all villas built in the prior 
century in Britain, it was a testament to its owner's wealth and 
power.  After the chill autumn morning outside, the villa's hypocaust 
heating offered a warm sanctuary without visible means of heating.

Around the hall, the men and women stood in small groups, seeming 
lost in the giant atrium, greeting friends, comrades and 
acquaintances.  Most of the aristocracy of Britain was here tonight, 
and all of the Council members.  And, regardless of which sides they 
had taken in the various disputes of the past half-century, the 
recognized each other as the powers in this land.

At least, the powers that they officially recognized.  Also in the 
room, and looking out of place in the midst of such grandeur and 
witchcraft were the Saxon barbarians who had come here to "protect" 
them and now were possibly the biggest existential threat to Britain 
since the Emperor Hadrianus had built his wall 300 years ago.

Ambrosius gently pulled Artorius to one side and started to whisper 
conspiratorially.  "There's something in the air," he confided and 
looked at his bosom companion meaningfully.  Artorius looked around 
the villa but saw nothing.  "What?" he asked, but starting to feel 
that prick of uncertainty even as he tried to shake off the feeling 
that his friend had an instinct about such things.  "Treachery," 
Ambrosius said, firmly.  "I can smell it."

"Vortigern?" Artorius asked, naming their nominal leader, but who, on 
any given day, seemed more likely to side with the Saxons that with 
his own people.

"Maybe," admitted Ambrosius, "and maybe not.  No, I feel my 'old 
friend' is on our side today."

"Not that we should have 'sides' on a day like today," interjected 
Artorius with a wry smile.

"That naive you are not, young Artorius," replied the other.  
"Hengest knows he won't be able to take Kent back by force of arms, 
so he has to beat us at the negotiating table.  He will achieve here 
what he did 20 years ago."

Artorius looked over at the aging warrior and would-be King, with his 
flowing locks washed and arranged for the formal meeting.  "Does he 
have another daughter to marry off, then?  Which tribe does he want 
to depose for this one?"

Ambrosius remained unmoved. "Your wit will get you into a lot of 
trouble one day, old friend.  I just hope it isn't today."

---

Vortigern called the meeting to order.  Older now, he was visibly 
tired and worn from thirty years of struggling to hold this nation 
together.  The meeting was to be held in Latin, the first language of 
many of the rich British nobles present in the room, and easily 
accessible to the other British.  But Hengest, even after all this 
time, spoke only a little of the language and many of his underlings 
even less.  Ceretic was there to translate and duly translated 
Vortigern's brief opening remarks into a flowery address for the 
benefit of his leaders.

"Let's get down to business," Ambrosius whispered to Artorius.  "Then 
we can get on with the drinking."

"This war must stop," Vortigern declared.  "You are here to defend 
this Republic from its enemies, and yet you have declared war on us."

Ceretic translated, waited for his master to consider this, respond, 
and then translated for the Britons waiting in the hall.

"My master says when you invited him here, you agree to pay him to 
defend you.  But you do not pay.  When you say you cannot pay, you 
agree to give Kentiworg to him as his possession and you marry his 
daughter make you brothers.  How now say you that he has no rights in 
Kentiworg?"

"We are not saying he has no rights, in Kent or anywhere else.  But 
when, as a Patron, he abuses the trust of his _free_ citizens, he has 
to expect that those citizens will rise up in rebellion against him.  
He has the rights to the produce and wealth of Kent, not the right of 
Paterfamilias over them."

Again a pause as Ceretic and Hengest translated, and then the 
riposte: "But how my lord collects the rents of people in Kentiworg 
when he is once more locked in Ruoihm by actions of your men?"

"They are not _my men_; they are the free citizens of Kent, acting 
independently," retorted Vortigern.  "Do you not know the law?"

Here and there in the hall, there was an intake of breath.  Vortigern 
seemed to be disowning the actions of the Cives of Kent; disowning 
them in front of their own representatives in this hall.  Disowning 
them, indeed, when his son had died this past winter past at the battle 
of Rutupiae.  Could he, really?

Ambrosius turned to Artorius, significantly.  Artorius turned to meet 
his gaze and whispered, "I thought you said it was not to be 
Vortigern selling us out this time."

"Indeed I did, although anybody can be wrong.  But in this case, I 
think he is just telling it how he feels it.  He wants peace, peace 
at any price and to go back to the way things used to be.  If he 
could get Hengest to leave, he would, but there are prices he will 
not pay.  But no, the man I'm watching is Osric.  I don't trust him."

---

Osric was the Lord of the Keels of Norfolk, based at Venta Icenorum 
at the end of the Icknield Way.  He was responsible for guarding all 
of the East Coast of Britain from his bases between the Thames 
Estuary and the river of Linnius.  His responsibility was mainly 
seaborne, but since the crushing of the Picts twenty years before, 
and the fact that he welcomed in Saxons with open arms, he had little 
to do.  Stuck in the flat boglands of the East, it was possible to do 
some farming, but where was the glory in that?  He would rather be 
marching in full battle order down the Icknield Way and laying waste 
to Verulanium that coming down here to hold a peace conference that 
would do him no good.

Yes, Artorius thought, he would love to be Lord of Verulanium, or 
Linnius, or even Emperor of Britain.  Ambrosius was right to be wary 
of him.

Artorius, of course, was not _Lord_ of anywhere.  A true Roman did 
not aspire to anything as crass as _ruling_ over other people.  Some 
things (why was the Army always the first thing that came into 
anybody's head) had to be provided by the people in concert, and 
obviously those things had to be paid for by "the people" and that 
meant that _somebody_ had to be in charge of collecting the money in 
taxation and dealing with all the thousands of petty, bureaucratic 
details.  Which is why we had magistrates.  Artorius had, himself, 
been _duumvir_ of Linnius a few years back and had been approached by 
Vortigern about standing for one of the praetor roles when his year 
came around; not long now.

No, the barbarians may have been here for a while now, but they were 
still barbarians.  Much like the army in the north, they basically 
kept themselves to themselves and did their job without involving 
themselves in the political situation that surrounded them.  Even 
when they "took over" a part of the country - as Hengest had in Kent 
- they still treated the people that lived there - free, Roman 
citizens! - as if they were chattels or slaves.  What was the world 
coming to?  Well, we all know that.  The end days are nigh.

Artorius shut out the depressing thought.  Enough time later for 
thoughts like that.  This was a time to try and bring this ludicrous 
internecine warfare to an end.  Osric and Hengest were here to defend 
Britain and the British way of life: why would they try to tear it 
apart?

---

"No, no, no!  One thousand times no!  My master says No!  He been 
cheated, he been betrayed, he been defrauded", Ceretic yelled, 
stumbling over some of the trickier Latin words that he still 
struggled to learn.  "My master is right King of Kentiworg and you 
must agree this.  Or else."

The words came out like a threat; there could be no denying it.  
Slowly it dawned on all those present that they were a threat.  Some 
atavistic premonition made Artorius turn from the men in the 
triblinum to face back towards the door.  Where there had been an 
empty space, now fifty or more Saxons stood blocking the entrance, 
all armed with swords and axes in spite of the complete ban on all 
weapons at the conference.

"Treachery! We are betrayed!" he shouted, reaching instinctively for 
the gladius which should have hung at his left hip had he not been 
disarmed for the conference.  His mind whirled, trying to think of 
how he could obtain some weapon; get some leverage to defend himself 
against these barbarians.

Others were turning now, facing the revealed enemy in shock and 
horror.  Some like Artorius sought weapons to defend themselves; 
others looked to flee.  Ambrosius was the first to take action, 
sweeping most of the detritus off a nearby table, grabbing up the 
heavy lead candlestick in his right hand and lifting the table by one 
of its legs in the other.  Artorius saw him move and jumped to his 
side, lifting the other half of the heavy oak table and snatching up 
another of the candlesticks as a makeshift club.  Together they 
charged the Saxons, hoping to break the still-forming line as they 
had so many times before.

Behind them, Hengest was yelling now, incoherent in his native 
barbarian tongue.  Ceretic had ceased translating; obviously whatever 
he was saying was intended only for his own men; those invited into 
the hall or those who had entered surreptiously.  Everywhere action 
was erupting as those on both sides separated, struggling for 
makeshift weapons and clamoring imprecations.

The table crashed into the shields of the first Saxons.  Although the 
move was probably surprising, there was no doubt that these Saxons 
knew why they were here and were expecting trouble.  Only the fact 
that they were still coming into the hall, and had not had the 
opportunity to properly deploy and form their battle line gave 
Artorius and Ambrosius any opening at all.  And yet, it was a slim 
one.

"To me, to me," yelled Ambrosius, naturally taking the lead as he had 
so many times before as Legate and General.

"For the glory of the Second", should Artorius, slipping back into 
the battle cry of the unit where he had done his military service on 
the border against the Picts.

The Saxon line held but swayed; the first Saxon opposite them heaved 
his axe, spewing thousands of splinters from the once-elegant table.  
Ambrosius and Artorius swung in unison with their clubs and forced 
the opposing Saxon to retreat a step: all they needed to start the 
line moving backwards until it would eventually break.

Others were coming to join them now; veterans of military service 
either in the North or in the recent wars in Kent.  Artorius felt, 
rather than saw, Gwyrangon, the erstwhile Consul of Kent come to his 
left hand side, a serving tray acting as a shield and a meat skewer 
for a weapon.  Cornelius Albus, ever a good man in a crisis, had 
hauled down a tapestry and was using it as a Retiarius might have in 
the arena in Camolodunum in years gone by.

"To the left," shouted Ambrosius and without conscious thought, 
Artorius obeyed the order of his superior.  Together they turned the 
Saxon line, opening a hole through which some of their compatriots 
might escape to the door.  From behind, men came running, some to 
further open the gap, others to flee through it.  For a few moments, 
the Britons held the field, then the numbers and weapons told and 
Ambrosius and Artorius went down, stabbed from behind by the 
encircling Saxons.

Some fled; many more were executed by the rampaging Saxons.  
Vortigern was captured and held alive, forced to cede all of Kent, 
along with the lands west of there as far as the Arun to Hengest and 
his compatriots.

Those that fled passed down the story that we all know.  When it 
mattered most, Vortigern crumbled but Ambrosius and Artorius stood 
firm in their bravery, even when the cause was hopeless.  But to 
Artorius' now-orphaned son, that would be cold comfort on the nights 
to come in the years ahead.
