title=Europe

&date AD350-AD410

Of course, the troubles didn't start in 472, or even 452.  Opinions
vary, but I think everybody would agree that it goes back a hundred
years or more.

The history books tell us that the Roman Republic died in a plethora
of civil wars the like of which could not be imagined in this age.  If
Pollio tells the truth - and why should we doubt him? - then in their
day enough Roman soldiers died fighting each other that if they were
here with us today we could not only see of these pesky Saxons but
push the Goths and the Huns back across the Rhine and the Danube.

But for four hundred years, after Octavianus took sole control of the
government and the Republic died its slow death of strangulation when
it seemed nobody was looking, there was peace throughout the whole of
the Roman domain.  To be sure, there were occasional skirmishes with
the barbarians across the border rivers, and every century or so a
particularly bad emperor would have to be deposed violently, but for
the majority of us - including those of us in Britain who missed the
first century of peace because Augustus (as Octavianus came to be
known) decreed that his father Julius had been wrong to come here at
all and they should just let the Britons keep to themselves - it was a
very placid time.

But then, in AD350, when the boy Constans was titular Emperor of the
Western half of the Empire, holding it in the name of his brother
Constantius, a Rhine general called Magnentius decided that he would
be a better emperor than Constantius.  Enough time has gone by that
it's hard to say now what he was really thinking, but given that
Constans was a boy at the time let's give him the benefit of the
doubt: a man would have been a better emperor than a boy.

If he had actually been the emperor.

The problem was, that while he thought he might make a better emperor,
and had the backing of an influential group of Senators in Rome and
the west, there wasn't an actual vacancy, and he didn't have the
backing of the armies on the Danube who remained stedfastly loyal to
the Emperor of the East, the deposed emperor's brother Constantius.
So the only way for Magnentius to get the job was to take his army,
based in Gaul, tasked with holding the Rhine bridges in the face of
the Goths, all the way to Eastern Europe to fight a battle with
Constantinus.

The fact that he lost the Battle of Mursa a couple of years later is
really a footnote to history.  The two important consequences were
that while he was away, the Goths flooded into Gaul, devastating
swathes of the countryside and wreaking havoc; and, like Sulla
marching on Rome, it gave other people the idea that it was fine to
decide that if you didn't like the current emperor, you could find
some troops holding back the dam against the barbarians and whisk them
off to your coronation ceremony in Rome without worrying about the
consequences for the rest of us too much.

It took Constantinus another half a decade to try and put the genie
back in the bottle and force, bribe or blackmail the barbarians back
across the Rhine where they belonged.  But in a lot of ways, the genie
never was put back in the bottle.  Civil wars are much deadlier than
foreign wars for a number of reasons.  First off, all the casualties
are "yours", so that's going to be double what it would otherwise be.
Then, the troops you're facing are generally a lot better trained than
barbarians, so the death count is much higher.  And, finally, when
you're facing barbarians, they often decide that discretion is the
better part of valour, and go home when the going gets tough.  That's
not an option when you have two well-trained Roman generals in
charge.  If you take all that into account, you can see that both on
the Rhine and on the Danube, we were under-strength for a long time.

Moreover, while the Goths were in Gaul, there was a rearrangement of
barbarian forces in Central Europe.  We will probably never know
everything that went on, but new tribes appeared from nowhere - east
of Parthia, they say, although where that might be, I have no idea.
The Huns were fearsome horse warriors.  Excellent, as Arthur always
said, that none of them decided to come here.  The Saxons were always
ship people, but they used to ply their violent trade somewhere in the
far east where we never saw them.  Between the Goths raiding into
Gaul, and the Huns pushing up into Central Europe, the Saxons were
displaced and found their way towards Western Europe, but still on the
other side of the Rhine.  When Constantinus pushed the Goths back
across the Rhine, the Saxons wanted out - and sailing to Britain was
an obvious trick to try.  Time and again over the next decade or two,
Britain came under attack from every direction - Saxons from the east;
Irish from the west; Picts from the north.  Britain had always had
four legions since the conquest under Claudius (the II, IX, XIV and
XX), although under Hadrianus the VI had come and the IX had
eventually left.

Most of the time, these four legions were enough to deal with these
attacks, but in AD367 the forces of darkness united against us and
launched a coordinated raid, destroying much of our defences down the
East Coast of Britain and overrunning our northern stations.  The new
Emperor, Valentinian, sent his most dependable troubleshooter,
Theodosius, Count of Gaul, to Britain to reinforce the legions and to
eject the invaders.  In a series of lightning campaigns he defeated
and destroyed the Saxons in the South and East of Britain, redeployed
the legions in such a way as to free up the II and XX to humiliate the
Irish in the Demetia and near Anglesey, and then reorganized the whole
of the defence of the northern marches, appointing regional governors
with plenipotentiary powers as a buffer before the regular legions
based at Hadrian's wall.

The next disaster to befall the empire happened far off in the east.
Some of the Gothic tribes were allowed to cross the Danube and settle
in Roman lands.  But they were unhappy in their new homes, and soon
rebelled, defeating the Imperial forces and killing the Emperor in a
battle that was fought at Adrianople in AD 378.  In his place, the new
emperor was the son of Theodosius who had rescued Britain, also called
Theodosius.  But he - and his colleague in the west, Gratian - were
unpopular in much of the west, and once again Britain provided the
leader of a new rebellion.  Gnaeus Magnus Maximus left Britain in 383,
taking the II and the XIV with him, leaving us woefully undermanned.
He picked up more units on the Rhine and went east to do battle with
Theodosius - and once again lost.  Some of the troops returned to Gaul
five years later, but none of them made it back to Britain.

Again, Gaul was overrun by barbarians, and it took yet more years of
hard work by Theodosius, only to die himself in 395.  On his death,
Stilicho, the senior general of the west, controlled the new emperor
Honorius, and in his name restored order to Gaul and Britain.

But events in the east dominated time and again.  When Goths crossed
the Danube in force and threatened Italy, Stilicho had to strip the
Rhine of many of its units and recalled the XX from Chester in a bid
to protect Italy.  Not surprisingly, the barbarians hordes held up on
the other side of the Rhine exploded into Gaul and threatened Britain.
Constantine, the general of the one remaining British legion - the IX
based at Hadrian's Wall - was quickly proclaimed "Emperor" and sent to
Gaul to round up the remaining forces, put some backbone into them and
do battle with the marauding Goths, Saxons, Alans, Suebi, Vandals and
all the rest.

Eventually Constantine prevailed and drove the barbarians from Gaul,
but was unable to destroy them or drive them back across the Rhine.
Instead, they took flight to the south, considering invading Italy but
ultimately crossing the Pyrenees into the Spains.  For once,
Constantine was able to return the units to whence they had come, but
remained himself in Gaul.  For mercy, he did not try to make himself
Emperor of all the West, but just settled for "Emperor of the Gauls
and Britain".

On the other hand, Honorius could not let him be even that, and sent
his generals to attack Constantine rather than working together to
destroy the barbarians, or at least hold some of them out of Italy.
Yes, he succeeded in destroying Constantine, and getting the British
and Gauls to recant, but it cost him the Empire.

Because next came the big blow.  Through treachery and betrayal, Rome
was sold out to the barbarians and was sacked.  A thousand years of
civilization and history was destroyed overnight.  And the 24th day of
the month of August in the year of our Lord 410 went down in the
history books as a day of infamy that none will ever forget.
