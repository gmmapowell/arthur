@Chapter
title=Demetia

&date AD503

The army had settled into a tight unit.

It had drilled consistently for over five years now, and the officers
had bedded in and weeded out the troublemakers.

At length, Arthur had persuaded the Senate to authorize this mission
and given authority to Agricola to lead it as "General".  Arthur, of
course, was along as an advisor and general expert on everything
war-related; the resident genius if you will.

The first battle had been fought at Carleon, where once the IX Legion
had been based.  It was an obvious base for the army to start from,
but it had been held by the Irish for over fifty years.

When they had arrived, Arthur had had no bright suggestions for
evicting the Irish through trickery or deceit.

"We have the men and the machines," he said and Agricola ordered a
frontal assault.

It wasn't pretty, and it wasn't quick, but within six months the fort
was theirs - just in time to winter in.

The rest of the winter was spent in re-fortifying the fort, repairing
the damage they had done themselves in storming it, and in ensuring
their supply lines all the way back to the territory they held solidly
on the far side of the Severn.

---

"This was never going to be a quick campaign," Arthur said to Agricola
as the rode out one day to observe the next fort up the valley.  On
top of a high hill, it looked almost impossible to reach it, let alone
to march an army up there to take it.

"Which is to our advantage," countered Arthur when Agricola raised
these objections.  "How do they manage to supply it?  There can only
be two or three routes up there."

"Circumvallate?  That hill?  You'd have to go two hundred miles
around," Agricola gasped in awe at the prospect of such a task.

"Not so," said Arthur.  "As I said, there can only be a few routes up
or down.  Seal each of them off at the base with a fort and a wall.
Connect any together where it makes sense and then just wait them
out.  We may starve them out or they may prefer to come down and do
battle.  We win either way."

"But it will take ages," complained Agricola.

"I never said it was going to be a quick campaign," repeated Arthur
with a grin.

---

Two months later, when Arthur had gone back to London to continue his
"duties" - mainly trying to bash heads together to pass his agenda and
get the economy back on its feet - the Irish came down from their hill
and attacked in the night.

Agricola's forces were taken by surprise, but nevertheless they
managed to rally and fight a defensive action by torchlight.  The
Irish had chosen to attack under cover of darkness in the hope that in
the confusion some of them at least might escape.  Probably some did,
but as the battle raged many were killed and many more finally
surrended.  In the morning, the sun rose on a field of dead and
wounded.

Agricola marched up the hill, demolished the fort and moved on through
Demetia.

---

Arthur rode over the hill with a Gaul in tow.

Agricola was notified by one of his staff and rode out to meet him.
The two saluted and Agricola eyed up the Gaul.

"Are you going to introduce us?" asked Agricola.

"This is Theodoric, lately from Armorica.  He has come across the sea
due to the sad loss of his home port there has come north, along with
all his vessels.  He arrived down in Cornwall, and offered his
services in exchange for being granted land and safe harbour.  Since
this is where the action is right now, I thought it might be good for
him to come and help you - a fleet would probably come in handy along
these coasts - and then the pair of you could probably make him Dux of
Cornwall."

"By evicting the current rulers, you mean."

"That's exactly what I mean.  You can be in charge on this side of the
estuary, and he can rule the other side.  Between you, I expect you to
keep the estuary safe from any marauders - Saxon, Irish, Frank or any
others brave enough to chart these waters.

"He brings ships, you know."

"Those could be useful.  We haven't had to fight many naval
engagements, but supplies along the coast can be hard here.  Supply by
ship might be the most practical choice."

---

Over the next three years, Agricola, Theoderic and Marcellus worked
together to evict the Irish from all of Demetia and to build strong
garrison forts and monasteries along the coast road.  By 508, a
network of forts, monasteries and roads had made the territory a
secure part of the British Republic.

True to his word, Agricola took the majority of the army south and
together Agricola and Theoderic established firm grip over the whole
of Cornwall.  In a series of lightning campaigns, they took the hill
forts using siege techniques honed in Demetia.  By 512, Agricola was
back in Demetia with a small force based at Carleon; Theoderic
operated a naval base at Falmouth - an ideal base for his naval forces
to protect the British coastline; and Marcellus had taken charge of
central Cornwall with another small part of the armed forces.

That left Arthur with the majority of the army to discharge.

---

Foolishly, he had assumed that discharging the army would be popular.

Technically, it wasn't particularly his responsibility; but because he
had been largely responsible for keeping the army running - making
sure it was supplied, recruiting new troops, discharging those who
were too old - somehow the overall responsibility had devolved onto
him.

And now there were six thousand or so men to discharge all at once,
with little memory of anything than being under the colours.

"We cannot just send them to be subsistence peasants," exclaimed
Arthur, aghast at the Senate's reaction.  "These were men of property
who have fought in our armies for years, if not decades.  They have
kept us safe from the Saxons when they were our enemies; they have
reunited this island; they have fought up inhospitable mountains in
the west.  We owe them land and property."

"We simply cannot afford to keep giving away the republic," countered
<some senator>.  "Again and again you come here, demanding that we
give away this or that part of the country to this or that group with
special needs and impoverishing us over and over.  You cannot continue
to do this."

"And I will not continue to do this.  This is the last thing.  I have
settled Britain now.  It would be better if you were to do more to
help make the farms productive and to create incentives for people to
start new businesses and operations in the towns - the towns are
dying!  But there is no more that I will ask after this.  After this,
I will retire."

"But you ask so much ..."

"Is it really so much?  That each of these men who has given so much
should be given either enough land to farm to produce for himself, his
family and to sell to others; or else to be given the capital to start
a business in a town?  These are things that _need doing_; these are
people that _need something to do_.  These are two problems that
cancel each other out."

"But where does the land come from?  Where does the money come from?
How do you propose to pay for this?"

"There is still plenty of land lying fallow all around the country.
We know where it is, because we have the census from a decade ago.
You've done so little to bring that land back into cultivation that I
don't imagine there is any shortage of it.  If we went through the
census today, I have no doubt that we could find three thousand
suitable farms for the men who would choose this path."

"And for the men who choose to start businesses?"

"We would need to provide money from taxation."

"But we are already broke."

"We are broke because there is not enough activity in the economy.  We
are slowly starting to recover from a half century of fear,
uncertainty and destruction.  We need to act to solve that; the best
way of doing that is to invest in activities that have been allowed to
degrade over the past half century or more.  Invest.  Not throw away."

"But you would take our money and give it to others.  We have no money
to give."

"Of course you do; you have been paying for the army for all these
years, and now that expense will be going away.  In five years, all
the money that I'm asking for now will have been recouped in lower
taxes because the army has been discharged."

"Until there's another war!"

"But where?  We have settled all the borders.  We are at peace with
the Saxons to the one hand and the Irish to the other.  Anyone else
would have to come over the sea, and the Saxons defend our eastern and
southern shores, while Theoderic does the same to the south and west.
In the north, the Picts may be fighting with the Scots, but it is very
unlikely that either of them will trouble Dyfnwal, let alone come
through to the midlands.  As long as you are sensible and govern well,
there need be no more wars in this island."

Yet they remained unconvinced.  For day after day, and week after
week, the Senate met and argued about what could and could not be
afforded.  Ultimately, they made it the responsibility of the
individual section commanders to find somewhere for their troops to
go; something for them to do.

A worse solution Arthur could not think of.  They would go nowhere in
particular and they would prey on their fellow citizens.  At times, he
couldn't help feeling that they were just determined to undo all of
his hard work.
