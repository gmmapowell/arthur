&date AD487

The Saxons crashed out of the forest and forded the river.

They had come in strength.  Messages had rapidly reached them of
Arthur's siege and they had been amazed.  What was the strategic
significance of this site?  What made it worth the effort of a long
siege?

They came and looked, presuming themselves unseen from their cover.
Certainly, the siegeworks were impressive, as were the number of men.
Where had they come from?  Linnius across the river in British
territory could be the only reasonable answer.  A couple of
reconnaissance missions confirmed that the garrison there was
undermanned.  They also observed the work that had been done
fortifying the ford and improving the road.  While such things meant
less to them than they did to Romans, they were nevertheless impressed
by his industry and his preparation should he need to retreat.

Of the two options they saw, trying to lift the siege directly seemed
much less likely to succeed than counter-attacking Linnius.  That
approach held the best chance of relieving the siege - surely news
that Linnius was under attack would reach Arthur and he would lift the
siege to come and counter-attack them, having to cross the river to do
so.  And beyond that, it held the upside potential that while Arthur
was coming they could possibly manage to storm Linnius.  And even if
not that, they could potentially ambush Arthur as he had to retreat
across the Dubglas.

That Arthur was there was certain.  Although less of a legend in the
town of his birth than he was in the south of Britain, his leadership,
his victory over the Picts, and his year of battling here before were
all well known.  But his genius was widely regarded as exaggerated and
the local Saxon leader was convinced that he could win this
engagement.

So while Arthur spent two fruitless weeks sieging their fort,
bombarding it will missiles and surrounding it with
ever-more-impressive earthworks, but never actually assaulting it,
they gathered over five hundred warriors from the region and gathered
close to two fords upstream from the one that Arthur had secured.

And attacked.

---

The gates were closed.

Arthur's instructions to the men inside Linnius had been clear and
explicit.  They were to appear undermanned at all times and to avoid
giving away their true strength even to the town residents.  The gates
of the city were to be kept closed at all times, except when
specifically required for the movement of troops, and civilians were
not to be permitted in or out of the city.

These were draconian restrictions.

To Arthur, this one battle was more important than the transient
lifeblood of the region.  He had calculated that it would not be for
more than a month and, while his inner circle knew this, the town
residents did not, and chafed under the restrictions.

But once they saw a horde of Saxons come and camp under their walls,
they were grateful for the closed gates and the handful of soldiers in
the city, but wished for more.

Unlike Arthur's tidy, exquisitely prepared siegeworks, complete with
artillery, the Saxon camp was a mess.  They had built a rough fence
surrounding the camp, it was true, but there were no proper
fortifications, no earthworks to control the territory, no proper
facilities and no apparent plan for defence should they be attacked.

The reason was simple.  The camp was just a base from which the Saxons
were planning to attack an undefended city and overrun it within a
day.  Arriving in the evening, the camp was intended only for a single
night: tomorrow night they were would be plundering and raping inside
the walls of the city and ready to face Arthur's retreating forces
from behind the city walls the following day.

The one recognizably defensive move they made was to detach fifty men
to dig a ditch across the road at "Arthur's ford" and build a
defensive work there to prevent Arthur's retreat across the bridge he
had prepared.  This, they reasoned, would delay Arthur's retreat even
if he heard about their assault before they had taken the city.

The only siege equipment they had brought with them were scaling
ladders.  They assumed that it was all they would need.

---

The ladders crashed against the outer walls.

It was early in the morning and the sun was glaring over the tops of
the trees to the east.  The Saxons had deployed at four points around
the walls and had started their assault.

On the walls, the fifty or so defenders had hurried to the apparent
attack points and had prepared their defences.  Against so many, they
were an inadequate force and would probably soon be overwhelmed.

Walls naturally favoured the defenders.  Both the height and the
protection afforded by the walls gave the defenders a concrete
advantage throughout the battle that would follow.  The Romans had
demonstrated, time and again, that it was possible to negate these
advantages by the use of the right equipment: mantlets and siege
towers, among others.  Arthur had read Vespasian's description of how
he had even built a mile-long ramp in front of Masada in Palestine in
order to reduce that mountaintop fortress.  It had taken him a couple
of years, of course.  But the Saxons had read none of that, and didn't
care.  Climb up the walls, hack down the defenders and get inside as
quickly as possible.  Attack from multiple angles and you'll split the
defenders.

On the walls, the defenders were armed with lances and other pole
arms, designed for their long reach.  They were able to stab down at
the attackers long before the attackers - trying to hold on to the
ladder with one hand, protect themselves with a shield with the other,
and at some point reach back for axe or spear with one hand or the
other - could retaliate.  In places, they tried to shove the ladders
away from the walls, but the combined weight of the Saxons and the
angle of the ladders kept them firmly in place.

At the north east corner, a couple of young lads tried hacking away at
one of the ladders with axes, but the ladders were wrapped in bark and
while that could be chipped away, the meat of the ladder was not
affected before the Saxons were at the top.

A big, hairy Saxon came flying up a ladder, his shield held out in
front of him like a battering ram.  He charged off the ladder and
landed like a cat on top of the narrow outer wall.  From here he was
even higher than the defenders on the fighting pediment and held them
off with his shield as he reached back for his axe.  In a few moments
he was down on the fighting step, swinging his axe at retreating
Britons as the others came up behind him.

Then one of the Britons stepped forward and engaged him in combat,
heavy Roman shield against that scything axe, _gladius_ held ready to
stab when the opportunity presented itself.

Around them, more Saxons were crashing up and over the wall.  Some
were quickly dispatched, either in the process of jumping from the
ladder to to the wall, or while they stood atop the outer wall: either
thrust backwards to the outside, or helped on in, down and into the
killing grounds of the courtyard below.

Soon two or three more Saxons had joined their first comrade and
opened up a breach on the wall, fighting back the Britons in both
directions.  More Saxons were now able to breast the wall from the
ladders, unopposed by British spears.  The Saxons exulted, for surely
now the castle was theirs.  More Saxons started climbing the ladders.

Around Linnius, the British on the walls were now in full retreat and
hurrying down the stairs to the city below.  As they left the walls,
the artillery in the town square started firing, spearing the Saxons
on the walls and sending them tumbling outside the city walls.

Half the Saxons were now up on the walls, and more were coming up the
ladders, unopposed.  They held the top of the staircases and were
coming down, protected by shields.  Now they needed to deploy across
the killing grounds at the base of the stairs, but they found their
way blocked by a shield wall of infantry.  From above, their comrades
cast spears and other missiles on the heads on the infantry, but they
responded by raising their shields over their heads in the classic
_testudo_ formation.

The first two Saxons to emerge from the northeast stair found their
way blocked by a solid defense line, multiple ranks deep.  Were there
still this many men left alive?  But they went to with gusto, laying
about with their axes.

The defensive wall gave slightly, but then the shields opened and the
_gladii_ came stabbing out.  The wall closed and pressed again as the
Saxons whirled their axes.  The _gladii_ struck again and this time
one of the Saxons died.  Another took his place.

In the background, the noise of the _ballistae_ continued unabated:
clack, whirr, rachet.  More often than not, the bolts would hit one of
the men on the ever-more-crowded walls.  On the ground, the need to
open the gates and let in the rest of the army was ever more urgent,
but it was impossible to break through the dense ranks of defending
Britons.

Suddenly a major commotion eruped outside, and then on the wall.  Down
on the floor of the city in the heat of battle, anything could be
happening elsewhere, and the objective was clear: get to the gates.
The axes continued to swing, but the resistance was solid.

---

Outside, the remaining Saxons were under attack.  Arthur himself - and
his two hundred men split between the ford and the fort they were
besieging - were not there, but the other units he had carefully
prepared and deployed in the woodlands against this moment had
attacked at the prescribed moment - once the walls had been fully
taken by the Saxons and the men outside were staging to enter through
the gates.

In one swift cavalry charge they were in and amongst the Saxons.
Hurriedly the Saxons attempted to set up a defensive wall but they
were turned about and out of formation.  Without a solid initial
defence, the cavalry were able to run over them and break them up into
small groups and hunt them down.  Within five minutes, the group still
outside the walls had been killed or had managed to flee to the
relative safety of the woods or river.  Outside the city, the British
held sway.

They followed up this initial victory by pulling down the scaling
ladders, even as some of the attackers on the walls considered the
relative merits of coming back down to face this new enemy.  The
Saxons were now trapped either inside or on the walls of the city.

On the walls, death was raining up from the continually firing
_ballistae_ in the centre of the town.  The only real option was to
fight their way down the staircases.  They held the staircases
themselves, but beyond that the courtyard was stoutly defended by more
soldiers than had been on the walls.  Without recourse, men were
dying.

Some of the Saxons survived that day, and went back to tell their
Lords that they had been led into a trap.  They escaped across the
river or through the woods.  Others surrendered and became slaves,
disarmed and taken far to the west where they would never meet others
of their own kind who might try and rescue them.

Arthur continued his siege until late Autumn.  No point in revealing
that it had been a ruse.  And anyway, they might still be foolish
enough to try and raise the siege.

In late October, the garrison surrendered.  They too, were taken
captive and sent to the west of Britain.  And Arthur returned to spend
the winter at his own base, in Badonicus and to plan his next step in
drawing out the Saxons to fight on his terms.

Linnius had been a complete success.
