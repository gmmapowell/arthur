&date AD477

Arthur looked up as he heard the shouts.

Instinctively, he knew something was up.  Something in the tenor of
the shouting was off; this was not the normal sound of a returning
unit.

He dropped the hammer he had been using to hammer out the new shoes he
was preparing for his horse and walked out of the forge.  Down in the
valley he could see the column of men coming in, ragged and torn.
Aside from anything else, they were late, but now he recognized the
tonality of the sound for what it was: the grief of men who had lost
their leader.  Suddenly it dawned on Arthur: Ambrosius!  He had been
leading yesterday's raid and it would be he that was being mourned.
Without thought, he ran for the gates and down the hill.

"I don't know how they knew we was coming, Young Arthur," Mardoc said
when Arthur ran up to him and asked for news.  "But they must've.
They was ready for us, all right and we walked right into their
ambush."

"How?" asked Arthur, struggling to handle this news on top of the
tragedy of Ambrosius' death.  With the massive advantage in
maneouverability and speed, it was almost impossible for the Saxons to
ambush the British: their only real weapon was defence, either through
fortifications or through a solid shield wall.

"I don't know how they knew we was coming, but they knew all right.
Just on the ford, before we crossed the river.  They was there.  Must
have been there before we arrived the night before the raid.  As we
was getting ready to cross the river in the morning, there they was.
All around us.  We was mounting up and they was just appearing out of
the trees, swinging them axes of theirs and just hacking everyone in
sight.  Until we got mounted it was slaughter, it was.

"And then?"

"They got old Ambrosius before he could even make it back to his
horse.  We tried to get over there and rescue him, but he was dead by
the time we got there.  You know what he was like for quiet: no
weapons with him when scouting and we couldn't cover for him when we
was under attack ourselves."

Arthur screamed inside his head in frustration and rage.  The way they
did things made sense - as long as everything went to plan.  And until
today, everything had gone to plan.  How _had_ the Saxons known that
Ambrosius would be there?  Good luck?  Did they guess every time and
just guess wrong most of the time?  Were they tracking somewhere back
in British territory?  Did somebody give us away?  Maybe they had
raided and held somebody hostage against this day.  His thoughts
spiralled through all the ramifications.

Stay calm.

The first lesson that Merula had taught him all those years ago.  When
he was tempted to give into his rage at the unfairness of it all,
Merula would calmly - oh so calmly - remind him that losing his temper
was only the first step towards losing his head.  But a calm man could
do anything.  So said Merula.  We'll see.

The cortege wound its way up the hillside and through the gates of the
fort.  After the men had mounted their horses, it had been relatively
easy to disperse the Saxons, who had, in any case, wanted to flee at
that point.  By the time the men had regrouped and recovered the
bodies of their leader and four others who had been killed, there
seemed little option but retreat.  Again Arthur fumed: if Saxons were
out of their forts and on our side of the river, there was the
opportunity for slaughter.  An opportunity which was not taken.  If he
had been there ...

If he had been there, he, like Ambrosius, would now be tied on the
back of his horse being taken home for burial.

---

At last the funeral was over.

It had only been a couple of days, but despite his best efforts to
remain calm, Arthur had struggled to deal with the loss of the man who
had given him so much.  Only Merula had done more for him.  And - may
the Lord be praised - Merula had arrived this morning in answer to his
summons for the funeral.  Merula would probably not have left his home
in Glestinium in the west of Britain had it not been for the death of
his erstwhile friend and colleague.

Merula - Gnaeus Laelius Merula to give him his full name - had had a
long and illustrious history in these islands and had now reached a
very respectable old age.  How old, he would not say when Arthur
questioned him, but old enough to have been older that Arthur and
Ambrosius, but a contemporary - if not older still - than their
fathers.

When Arthur's father had died outside Verulanium twenty years before,
Arthur had been left an orphan.  Merula - who then lived in Linnius
with the Artorii - had adopted him under the terms of Artorius' will.
In many ways, Merula was the father Arthur would have chosen; or maybe
Arthur was the child Merula would have chosen.  Or maybe Arthur grew
up to be the child Merula formed.

They walked together back to Arthur's "hut".  When the rebellion had
first started, they had quickly realized the need for safe and secure
bases long from the reach of the Saxons.  They had quickly felled logs
and dragged them up here, building temporary structures that they
sarcastically called "huts".  As time had gone on, the houses, the
stables and the whole community had grown into something approaching a
real Roman village, but everything still retained the casual names
they had had when first occupied.  So Arthur still lived in a "hut",
albeit one connected to a central village hypocaust and with covered
access to the stables and the forge.

Merula walked inside and sat down.  Arthur poured him a cup of wine
and mixed it with water.  He passed it over and reached for another
cup for himself, poured and sat down facing Merula.

Merula sipped and thought, leaning his head on one side in a way
Arthur remembered from his youth.  "Good wine, this.  Where do you get
it?"

Arthur laughed; of all his concerns right now, the wine supply was a
long way down the list.  "Ambrosius has - had - connections deep in
Gaul somewhere.  There's still money to be had for the finer things in
life - just not the essentials."

Merula looked at him sharply.  "Don't get all bitter, now, Arthur.  It
won't do you any good, you know.  You need to keep things in
perspective."

"I'm trying; I really am.  But you know, I think we're losing."

Merula's eyes seemed to bore into him, seeking deep answers somewhere
in the depth of his soul.

"For one so young, you seem to have become very cynical, my boy.
Where did you acquire this age-weary pose?  Somebody been giving you
bad tutoring?"

"No, no, nothing like that.  It's just very simple: numbers.  We can
go raid them and kill a dozen men, but then the following week another
boat arrives and they have more men there than they did before.  But
every time we lose a man, who replaces him?  They are warriors born;
we prefer ... scholars."

"And what's wrong with that?"

"Nothing, nothing at all.  Until you are sitting in your library with
your scrolls one day, composing witty epigrams about Julius Caesar's
love life when a Saxon strides in with his battle-axe and dismembers
you."

"Colorfully put.  Remind me not to play at epigrams with you,
particularly not to those rules.  But no, I was more thinking
... well, about thinking."

"Thinking?"

"Yes, have you really _thought_ about the position we are in?"

"I think about almost nothing else."

"Yes, but have you really, truly _thought_ about it.  Like Caesar,
say, rather than Pompey.  Thought deeply about and around the issues
and seen if you can't think of another way out."

"Such as?"

"So that would be a no, then, I presume.  Too busy hacking people's
heads off to spend any quality time being a general."

"I'm not the general.  Ambrosius is."

"Was."

"Whatever.  I'm not."

"Yet."

Arthur looked up in surprise.  At 25, he was not even officially old
enough to be quaestor or to be admitted to the Senate.  He was way too
young to officially command an army and, moreover, there were any
number of men on this hill who were old enough, had the rank and the
birth.  Were they all going to die in a storm to make way for him?

"No," replied Merula, apparently to his thoughts alone.  "They will
appoint the best man for the job.  Special circumstances and all
that.  Always a camp lawyer somewhere who can find a loophole when you
need one.  But no matter about that ..."  He trailed off and waved his
hand absently, lost in thought.

"No, what you need to concern yourself with is what you are going to
do with that power.  In short, how you are going to wipe all the
Saxons from this land.  And, if you think about it for a moment, you
will realize there is only one answer."

Arthur continued to stare.  If there was one answer, it was one more
than he had managed to think of.  What was Merula going to suggest?
Wave a magic wand and send them back into the sea?  Or summon up the
old British and Roman gods to wreak vengeance?  Arthur didn't see it
working.

"You know, there are times when I think you may have been my densest
pupil, Arthur.  It's sitting in front of you and yet you still don't
see it ... not even today of all days."

Arthur said nothing; simply sat in silence.  His mind was more than
blank; it had simply ceased to function.  Finally he opened his mouth.

"No, go on.  Tell me.  Or at least give me a hint."

"All right then.  In what situation _could_ you destroy them?"

Arthur thought for a moment.  Merula had played this logic games with
him since he was a child.  Usually the answer was buried somewhere in
Plato, or Aristotle or one of the "greats" of philosophy.  Merula
could be as annoying as Socrates as well at times, thought Arthur.
Now might be one of those times.

"Well, if they were foolish enough to try and climb this hill and
attack us in broad daylight when we had men stationed to ambush them
on their retreat ..."

"Yes?" Merula's eyebrows flickered up a little.  This was an age-old
clue to Arthur that he was on the right lines.  Arthur briefly
wondered if Socrates had done the same thing when one of his
interlocutors had given the "correct" answer to one of his questions
about justice, or the soul, or the meaning of life.

Yes, we could beat them - even destroy them - on an open battlefield
or a battlefield of our choosing.  Like Marius defeating the Germans
at Aquae Sextiae, or Sulla rolling up the natives of Pontus.  That
would be the easy part - almost too easy given the cavalry advantage
on top of everything else.  But what would tempt them to do something
so foolish ...

"Greed," supplied Merula, once again uncannily knowing the drift of
Arthur's thoughts.  "Yes, indeed, greed is a powerful motive that
makes men do stupid things.  Greed or lust.  And a belief that you
will win and the other side doesn't have a chance.  And don't forget,
it doesn't have to be tomorrow.  Give it ten or twenty years to mature
into a plan.  Wear them down until they're ready to be foolish.  Ready
to give in to anything that might make you go away."

"Greed.  Yes.  And anger.  I can see that.  But how?"

"Details, details.  The first of which is to get you elected as
Imperator here.  Not Emperor, no.  That would never happen.  Let's see
... a good title?  Comes, perhaps?  Dux?  I've always thought Dux
sounded better."

"But the Duke is bound to stay in one place and guard that territory.
We are fighting a mobile war; it is the age of the Count."

"True, true.  How you do test me, young Arthur.  One place eh?  How
about 'the war'?  You're not going to drift far from that, are you?
And it will stop too much jealousy of your ranke and role.  Yes, Dux
Bellorum, I think that's the way to go."

"Dux Bellorum?  I've never heard of such a thing.  What does it even
mean?"

"Special circumstances, my boy.  Special circumstances.  It means you
will be in charge of this war by sundown."

---

Merula called the meeting to order.

The men gathered around the tribunal as the sun started to set in the
west and formed into ranks, unconsciously letting their training come
to the fore.  On the platform stood four men, one of whom would be
their leader by the evening.  Officially, the decision was in the
hands of the Council, but that had been so dominated, first by
Vortigern and then by Ambrosius, that it would ratify anything the men
here decided.

Merula would not be the leader, even though he was one of the four men
on the tribunal.  He was here to run proceedings and to make sure that
nothing went awry.  Roman discipline being what it was, trouble was
unlikely, but it always helped to have a cool head on hand to make
things feel more certain.

While Arthur was popular, and certainly a good leader of his unit, he
was generally considered too young to make the grade, which meant that
the choice was really between the other two men on the stage:
Ambrosius' deputy Mardoc, or the leader of the third troop, the
insufferably pompous Titus Fulvius Crispus.  The smart money was on
Mardoc, but there was hardly a man there that didn't think that Arthur
was "the one".

"It falls to us to elect a new leader of the Roman Resistance.  The
tablets and buckets are prepared and you all, being citizens, know the
methods of voting.  Normally, we would allow a few weeks for
campaigning and the proper procedure for you to get to know the
candidates, but really, with the war and all ... you know, special
circumstances ..." he drifted off dreamily.

"Anyway, I thought it would be good to allow each of the candidates to
say something about why they thought you should choose them and, uh,
well ... here we are.  Mardoc?  Get on with it, if you would."

Although expecting to have to say something, Mardoc was taken aback by
this somewhat brief introduction.  Rallying himself, he gamely began.

"Friends, Romans, Countrymen ... We have all lost today a great man, a
man I am pleased to call by the name of friend.

"Just a few short years ago, this man roused us from the slumbers of
the Massacre and the Raid and showed us that we could fight back
against the unwelcome foreigner, against those that would take our
lands, take our women and destroy our way of life.  I had given up
hope, I tell you, given up hope.  I was considering leaving, going to
Armorica with the others ...

"But Ambrosius gave me back hope.  He showed me ... showed us ... that
it was possible for small bands of dedicated men to take the struggle
to the Saxon and, through striving, to overcome.  And we will ...

"He showed us how and we will.  I will carry on the struggle.  Until
we win."

Merula stepped forward again.  "Thank you, Mardoc.  Now ... uh, Titus
Fulvius, if you would."

"Clearly, there is a process here and a right order for things and I
can only see one proper and correct way forward which is to give the
leadership to the senior man.  I am that man.

"It is incumbent upon us to do the right thing and to appoint the
right man.  The man with the birth, the experience and the knowledge.
I am that man."

Merula nodded: "Thank you, Titus Fulvius.

"It may be said that the other man here, Gaius Artorius, is officially
old enough to contest this election.  That may be true for different
men in different situations, but we are here to elect, how shall we
put it?  The _Dux Bellorum Brittanicae_ and as such, there is no
specific requirement on the law books as to age.  If Honorius could be
emperor at age 10, presumably Arthur could be Dux at 25.  Hmmm.
Anyway.  I suppose if you don't agree with that, then you should vote
for one of the other eminently ... qualified ... candidates."

He paused for a moment, looked around.

"Arthur?"

Arthur stepped forward, hesitantly.

He knew that Merula had given him all the clues he needed, and now it
was up to him to make that speech that would make the dream a
reality.  The dream that had been his since that awful night when his
parents had been murdered and he swore vengeance on the Saxons by all
gods, British, Roman or Christian.

He looked at the two other men on the tribunal with him.  They had
said what he had expected them to say:  one that he was the friend of
Ambrosius, a safe pair of hands; the other that he was the man with
the right.  What should his message be?

He looked around him, looked at the faces out there, expecting
_something_ of him.  Did they know what?  Did he?  Did Merula?  They
were expecting him to ... that was it! They demanded hope.

"Comrades, I too mourn my dear friend Ambrosius.  And I say with
Mardoc that we must continue without him.  But why, my friends?  Why
this struggle?"  He looked out at the bemused faces.  "No, I do not
suggest lamely giving in now our leader is dead.  Oh, no.

"But I do ask this: are you satisfied with raiding the Saxons, killing
a few, and then being killed in turn?  Are you satisfied with ceding
the lands east of the Arun?  Are you content that the lands of the
Iceni are held by foreigners?  That none can live in Linnius because
it is too close to the lands of the North Folk?

"If you are, I am not the right leader.  Because I am not satisfied.
I want revenge for the brutal murder of my parents twenty years ago.
I want the interlopers ousted.  I want our lands back.  I demand a
return to the proper forms of government.  The Council of Britain must
be supreme.  Until that day, I struggle for that day!"

"Well, I suppose that wraps up the speeches.  If you'll kindly come
and choose a tablet and put it in a bucket we can get on with the
acclamation and then maybe there's some food somewhere ..."

The election officers stepped forward and handed tablets to the men as
they passed.  Each one marked an appropriate glyph in the soft clay
for the man he wanted to be the new leader and deposited it in the
appropriate bucket for his unit.  Within a few minutes, the voting was
over and the count began.

"The first unit votes for Arthur," declared the official when the
first bucket had been counted.  Not really a surprise, since it was
his unit.

"The second unit votes for Arthur," declared the official, counting
out the second bucket.

"And the third unit votes for ... Arthur."

It was complete ... he was now officially _Dux Bellorium Brittaniae_,
whatever that meant.

And as the sun finally set, the cheering started.  They may not have
thought it possible, but the men were pleased with what they had done.
