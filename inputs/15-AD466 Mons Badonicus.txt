@Chapter
title=Mons Badonicus
toc=

&date AD466

The wind swept over the ancient hill fort.

For four centuries, it had been deserted.  The Romans had had no use
for such places: their armies defended the borders of their
territories, and within them they had civilian means to preserve the
peace.  These forts were here to protect tribe against tribe and
primitive peoples against other primitive peoples: those without the
siege technology to capture these forts.  Vespasian hadn't even felt
the need to apply himself when he had come through here in the AD60s:
he had turned to his _primus pilus_ centurion of the II Legion
(Augusta) and said, "Take this place.  Usual methods.  Nothing
special.  Report to me at sundown."  Salutes were exchanged and the
army got on with its business, while Vespasian got on with his
paperwork.  Within a decade he would be emperor.

The Saxons had none of the technology, organization or techniques that
would enable them to take this place.  At best, they could hope to
starve it out.  Even so, Aurelianus' first suggestion was that they
improve the defences, make it a proper fortress that might even
present an obstacle to a latter-day Vespasian.

"We will need to fight a largely mobile war from a long way behind our
lines.  We cannot at this point organize the kinds of forces that can
either defend our territory or claim back that which is lost.  But we
can hit and run: burn and kill Saxons where we find them.  Our
resistance may be futile but it is all we have."

The men gathered here on this desolate hill were thrilled by his
words.  Almost a decade had passed since the massacre of their leaders
and in that time none in Britain had known peace, but neither had any
truly stood up to the invaders.

Here and there, individuals had attempted to resist: fought the
invading Saxons but, without the support of a network of neighbours or
a true army, they had been unable to stop the rampaging Saxons.

Strangely, to the north, north of the Humber, where the Saxons had
neither gone nor been sent, the "official" Roman army kept going
through its paces, but its job remained to protect the island from the
Picts.  Although Octha offered a buffer zone to the north, there was
no question that should the army move south against the Saxons, both
Picts and Saxon guardians in the north would come south and overrun
their deserted positions.

And so, cut off from the main supply routes to the south, the army
struggled to feed itself, became more dependent on marauding into
Pictish territory, thus incurring more hostility from the Picts -
although they were unable to gather their strength to retaliate.

"No," said Ambrosius Aurelianus.  "If we are going to be saved, we
need to do it ourselves.  And we cannot do it piecemeal, for if we
try, we shall surely be gobbled up piecemeal.  No, we must form
ourselves into a mutual assistance force, capable of defending our own
lands from the enemy and attacking them in theirs."

"To do that, we need to have secure places where we can run, hide and
re-equip ourselves.  This is one such place.  It is just what we need
to corral horses, establish weapons' manufacturies and keep
blacksmiths.  There will be others - Cadbury for one - and we must
recruit both more people and defend more locations.  We must find
secure locations like this in the heartland and others, less secure,
near the border from where we can respond quickly - or flee if
necessary.

"We must ring the Saxons with a network of forts that do not allow
them to escape, or to raid.  We cannot allow them a moment's peace to
gather their forces and attack us again in strength, for surely then
we will go down.

"Our chief weapon must be the horse.  We have horses; they do not.
They have ships, which we do not; and so we must avoid trying to fight
them on the water.  Our biggest vulnerability will be our coastline,
and again, forts such as this offer us bases from where we can repel
their coastal assaults.  Vortimer and my father showed us that it was
possible in the days of the war before the massacre.  We must do it
again."

The men discussed and planned on top of that hill for two days, the
bleak location reminding them of the dire situation that faced them in
the country.

And then they took action

---

The resistance spread like wildfire.

Until this point, most of the men in the country had not held back
from doing something out of fear, but from lack of leadership.  None
had shown the way to resist, and in each man's heart was a desire to
protect his own.  As that had failed, this new resistance offered a
hope heretofore undreamt of.

Over the summer of AD462, forts across the south and midlands of
Britain sprouted new defenses: trenches, walls, palisades.  They were
equipped with artillery: trebuchets, _ballistae_, mortars and more.
Pits were dug and stores laid in.  Huts, houses, factories, stables -
all were constructed and defended.

Across the countryside, recruiting and requisitioning went on.  With
the structures of government in a shambles, there was no central
authority capable of "enforcing" the requisition orders, and many -
who were least affected by the depradations of the Saxons - remained
aloof.  But many gave what they could - of their time, their crops or
their horses - to support a mission that might mean security for all
over the long haul.

---

After the Massacre had come the Great Raid, where Saxons had poured
over the countryside.  But like a high tide at the beach, the flow was
followed by the ebb.  With insufficient numbers to hold the whole
island, they retreated to the areas where they had traditionally been
quartered.  Kent became Saxon, as did the lands to the west occupied
by Aelle and his crews, who became known as the "South Saxons".  In
the east, the Angles took over the lands of the Iceni and used their
fens to defend themselves and the Icknield Way to go out raiding.

Further north, the Jutes occupied the lands north of the Wash and
south of the Humber, reaching as far as Linnius.

To the rest of the country, they were still raiders.  Warriors born
and bred it was easier to group together in a band and hike across
country pillaging and stealing what the wanted: livestock, slaves and
produce.  The Britons had tried to resist, but it had been in vain
against such overwhelming, organized force.

Aurelianus' solution was to create impregnable - at least to the
Saxons - bases across the British-held territory, and to station
highly mobile forces at each of them.  Resurrecting the post service
that had collapsed during the Great Raid, he enabled his various units
around the country to communicate, and instructed villagers to be
ready to signal for help using fires - something that admittedly the
Saxons often did for them anyway.

When the Saxons raided, the mobile forces could issue from their bases
and meet strength with strength.  Over the next couple of years, the
number of raids diminished, and they became less invasive.
Eventually, a rudimentary border was established between "Saxon" lands
and "free Britain".  And then Aurelianus started to turn the tables.

Initially planned for defence against Saxon raiders, the bases were
often ideally placed for incursions against the Saxons.  Even those
that were further west - such as Badonicus in the south and Chester in
the north - were ideal for preparing for a raid, keeping units up to
fighting trim and with very short supply lines, and then staging
closer to the border.

Within five years, Aurelianus had completely transformed the situation
in Britain.  The threat of the Saxons overrunning the entire island
had retreated, but the border zone was in a state of continual warfare
that it seemed neither side would be able to bring to an end.
