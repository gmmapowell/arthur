@Chapter
title=Camolodunum

&date AD497

Looking back, it was his high point as Consul, just fifteen days in.

The Senate opposed just about everything he tried to do; for the very simple reason that just about everything he wanted to do had short-term costs and benefits only in the long - or very long - term.  All of his plans were going to cost a great deal of money, and in the short term at least, there was basically none to be had.

The economy was collapsing.  Although none of them really understood what was happening, they were aware of the consequences.  From the time of the massacre, the great rebellion and the great flight of citizens and lords to Armorica, the sophisticated infrastructure of British and European life had collapsed.  For the previous centuries, life in Britain had been ordered and organized, with a division of labour that would not be seen again until the industrial revolution once again ushered in an organized society.  Many men had worked in the fields, it was true.  This was a basic staple of life throughout the millenia.  But these men had worked for pay, and had produced goods from the land that were sold and distributed across the island and often sent to Europe in exchange for other goods.

Each man worked within the fabric of a society in which other men bought and sold their goods and services.  And the scale of Roman society - and even British society if the whole island hung together - was enough to allow specialists to grow up in towns such as Gloucester, Wroxeter and London.  Specialists in metalworking, publishing, arms manufacture - or even such simple necessities as making ploughs and harnesses.

For the past fifty years of the troubles, that had broken down.  Now, most men simply farmed whatever land was sufficient to feed their families and maybe their neighbours.  Some trades and professions had dwindled or even died out due to lack of demand.  Across the south coast, the oyster beds that had been farmed for export to Rome - and other rich patrons across the western empire - had been abandoned in favour of the more immediately marketable, and edible, craft of near-shore fishing.  Deep-sea fishing - like ocean trade - had almost vanished because it was just too dangerous in waters infested with pirates, raiders and generic "enemies".

Arthur sought to turn back the clock on all of these trends, but he was continually swimming upstream.  In order to achieve any of his goals, he needed to achieve one of the others first.  This vicious circle - again unseen by his contemporaries - just generated resistance from some quarter to whatever he tried to do on any given day.

In order to boost the economy, Arthur had to try and re-integrate society.  In order to do that, he needed to offer people security and enforce a structure on society that had been absent for fifty years - beyond the memory of most of those alive today, who resented his interference in the only life they had ever known.

In order to offer security or enforce the laws, he needed the force to oppose generals and others who set themselves up as "kings" or local, independent governors.  That meant he needed to muster, arm, equip and provide for a force big and powerful enough to overawe any of the lesser generals in the land.

In order to overawe other generals - not go to war with each one individually - he needed an army so large as to intimidate them into not even fighting him.  One that was loyal to the state, not to the individual generals, since that would lead back to the same problem he was trying to avoid.  And the only way to make sure that the army was truly loyal to the state was to make sure the state paid it - and that they would stop paying it the moment it did the wrong thing.

In order to pay this army, he needed funds of some description.  Money had largely broken down as a form of exchange in the past few years - the trust in it had gone along with the markets where it could be freely spent, and you can't eat precious metals - but funds in the form of food and equipment would have been sufficient if he could manage to create a surplus and use that to feed and equip his (necessarily large) army.

In order to obtain those goods, he needed two separate and contrary things: he first needed an efficient method of controlling that - involving yet more useless, non-food-producing mouths to organize, collate and control a commissary and taxation system; and he needed to boost the economy to greater production.  If money were still effective, it would be possible to "prime the pump" by borrowing money, offering it in the markets for whatever goods were available, and then hope that production would gradually increase as more and more lords and men realized the profits that could be made by growing crops to sell to the state.

In order to make a real success of it, Arthur really needed a century or more of peace and stability in which to make his investments and see the results grow.  But obviously he was never going to have anything like that, and he just needed to act quickly and hope for the best.  He knew everything that needed to be done; it was just a question of taking a stab at whatever parts of it he could find to do.

---

While he left Dyfnwal and Quintus in charge of trying to round up, subdue and organize all the rebel British military forces into a single army, he took a party of senators together with a century of administrators and rode east.  He had arranged to meet with the Saxon leaders at the beginning of February at Camoludunum, and while he was not wildly optimistic about the short-term benefits he could achieve, settling peace terms with the Saxons was at least a policy agenda item that didn't involve any fighting in the short term.

Much of Camolodunum was still standing; it had been sacked by the Saxons at the time of the Great Rebellion, and parts of it - including the walls - had been burnt.  For Arthur's present purposes, the wall was not important, since the only people he felt the need to defend against were the very people he was inviting inside.  As with many towns and cities of the later Roman Empire, defence was not the issue - commerce and law were.

The two parties met in the central forum.  Here the Stoa of Claudius still stood, somewhat damaged and, in the odd way that often seemed to affect buildings, with much of its roof missing.  Fortunately, the day was clear and bright; a blessing in early February.  The Saxon party consisted of twenty leaders, all from the area they now called _Anglia_.

One of the problems that Arthur felt urgently needed addressing faced him immediately: the Britons spoke and wrote Latin; they also spoke the traditional Celtic language of the islands.  The foreigners spoke their dialects of German and almost wrote not at all.  Since his youth, Arthur himself had learnt to speak the Saxon dialect, and planned to converse directly with the Saxons in that language, his interpreter translating for his own side, most of whom had not the opportunity - or taken the time - to learn the barbarian tongue.  But in the long run, peace and stability would only be possible with a common language supporting a common culture - Latin being the basis of a Roman system of laws.

"I greet you in the name of the Roman Republic of Britain, for while I am Artorius, and you know me as a warlord, since in our lands we do not have warlords, I am not, in fact, such.

"I am merely one servant of many in service of that which we know as Rome.  Sadly, Rome is no more and has been sacked and destroyed by those of your brethren who chose a path of destruction.  It is clear, however, that even if Rome is not always stronger than barbarism, it is better.

"You are here because you are strong.  In the innocence of youth and unfettered by civilization, your ancestors were the perfect tool to fight our enemies and protect our culture and civilization.  But two mistakes were made; mistakes that, even now, sixty or more years after the event, I would put right and try to salvage what I can of our common bond of living in these islands.

"The first mistake - and most obvious - was to take your service for granted and treat you as interlopers rather than allies.  To accept your protection, but not to be prepared to meet the cost.  Today, I promise that this will not happen again.  I say that we are stronger together than we are apart, and that divisions between us serve neither of our parties.

"Just a few months ago, I was in western Britain - maybe some of you were too.  Thousands of men died; some British, many more Saxon, or Angle, or Jute.  We could continue this vicious cycle of killing, of destruction and violence, or we could try living alongside each other.

"The second mistake - and in my opinion, more serious - was to invite you here, and to accept you alongside us, but to keep you at arm's length.  To tell you that you were welcome to settle here, but not to incorporate you into our society, with all the rights and privileges that come from being _Roman_.

"There were many - indeed, there still are some - who refuse to accept that _mere Saxons_ could be worthy of admission to the councils of the _good_ and the _great_.  They look to their own privileges and perquisites and care nothing about others.  These men I represent in name - since they are Romans - but not in spirit, for theirs is a mean spirit and I have a great heart.

"I have fought you almost my entire life.  For many years, I hated you.  After all, through what my people view as treachery, you killed my parents.  But as I grew older, I saw that what I did myself caused pain: to Britons, to Saxons, to Angles.  And I saw that you responded in kind, causing yet more pain and suffering to those I was sworn to protect.

"In other words, eventually, I realized that my hate was killing my own people."

Arthur paused and reflected, then continued.

"I come here today not as the victorious conqueror of Badonicus - although that I am - but as a friend and a representative of the legal government of all of Britain, to invite you to participate in that government.

"Ours is a government of laws, and a government of representatives.  We do not respect force, or arms, or violence, even though those may sometimes be necessary, but we believe in important men, men of standing, with property, meeting and discussing the common weal of the republic and acting in accordance with its prosperity.

"This is what I propose.  We are here met in the traditional seat of government here north of London.  I propose to reinstate this as the government of this area, and to have you appoint a committee of one hundred leading local men to meet here and decide what happens in all of this area.  If you accept, we can decide the exact territory and that territory will be Saxon.  But not Saxon in a barbaric sense; Saxon under Roman law, and Roman discipline.

"This council will choose ten men - the ten men it finds to be most outstanding, and preferably all able to speak Latin or British - and I will invest them as Senators.  As you may have heard, our Senate will be returning to meet in London, and I welcome them to join us there.

"We will provide all the assistance you need - lawyers, men versed with procedure, administrators, census-takers and the rest.  Over time, you will train and appoint other men.  More of your men will learn Latin, and you will become Romans, as we British are Romans: obeying the laws of Rome and living in the Roman way."

Arthur paused again to gauge their reaction and saw what he had expected, rather than what he had hoped for.  These men knew that after their defeat the previous year they could not realistically expect to resist him, or anything he asked them to do.  They would do what he asked but, initially at least, it would be due to the lack of ability to resist; not from any true enthusiasm.

"As long as we live like this, discussing our differences and resolving our disputes in the proper fashion, I undertake neither to do battle with you nor to cease from paying you.  We will ensure that a certain portion of all the goods of the country come to you and that your forces are equipped to defend your coastline against any enemies - from the east or from the north.

"There are those who see what I am doing as 'arming the enemy'.  If we all act in good faith, that need not be the case.  We have no need to be at odds.  Your gain is our gain - we all wish to live together, in one accord, in one island.  I give you my word that I will work for this."

The Saxon leaders conferred, discussed and let out a great shout.  Then the leader of their delegation came to Arthur, grabbed him by the arm and them in a giant bear hug.

"You are indeed a man among men, Arthur.  I have fought you many a time, and been beaten by you many a time.  If we fight again, I mean to beat you, but I would rather fight as your friend and drink beer in your tents.

"As long as you live, and as long as you deal honestly with us, we will deal honestly with you."
