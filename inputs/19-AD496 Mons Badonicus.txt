@Chapter
title=Mons Badonicus

&date AD496

Arthur was no longer at Cadbury.

The forty miles he had to cover from Cadbury to Badonicus was almost
nothing for a horseman; still less for the post riders who covered the
distance in both directions each day.  But for his two legions of
infantry - III Legion (Artoria) from Gloucester had now joined them as
well - marching across countryside rather than down the main road, it
was a two-day slog.  That he managed to get near the top of the rise
just to the north of Badonicus was due to the tireless efforts of his
harrying cavalry preventing the Saxon infantry from coming too far
north without combat.  While the combat was often ineffective and more
tiring and time-consuming than deadly, it served its purpose when all
Arthur really needed was to keep the Saxons away from the forest where
he was staging.

All was now in readiness.  He just needed to make sure that everything
went according to plan and that he could deploy all three legions, and
force the Saxons to commit.

He wandered up to the top of the rise and looked out through the
trees.  In the distance he could see Mons Badonicus rising up, but he
could not see what was going on up there.

Quintus came and stood beside him.  "Do you want to go and have a
closer look?"

Arthur considered.  "I'd like to, but I don't think it's worth the
risk.  We've come this far, we don't really want to give it away."

"The delegation has come down the side of the hill and they're
treating right now.  Only time will tell what deal is struck."

"It doesn't matter.  We aren't going to be bound by whatever they
agree to, no matter what.  I just want them looking in, not out, and
the key moment."

One of Quintus' men came riding up.

"Sire, the delegation have started to retreat up the hill.  The
formation they are in says that they have reached an agreement to
leave.  They will start leaving the settlement soon.  Presumably they
have agreed to give up their armour and weapons."

He rode off.

Shortly, another man rode up.

"The first men have started to form up at the edges of the encampment
and are coming out.  They appear to be disarmed, Sire.  I think it is
time."

"That's my decision, of course," corrected Arthur.  "But I agree with
you: it is time."

He moved back into the woods, not really needing to hurry.  Everything
had been prepared for this moment.

---

"I don't like this.  There's something wrong."

Norvel turned to face Lucullus as the translator completed the
thought.

"What do you mean?"

"They shouldn't give up this easily.  I would expect them to hold on
to this position; buy Arthur some time to try and mobilize his other
units."

The leaders had promised that all the men would surrender and come out
without armour or weapons.  The cavalry would leave their horses as
well, and all the food would be left undisturbed.  The Saxons would
have possession of the camp by nightfall.

In exchange, the Saxons had agreed to let the Britons leave alive.

"But you tell me they are peasants who do not want or know how to
fight.  Why should they sacrifice their lives for a lost cause?"

Lucullus heard the words but could not shake the feeling that Norvel
was missing something.  Something big.  He wanted to shout that it was
all a trap - but how could it be?  Just something about his instincts
about Arthur told him that something here was not as it should be.

Something about the way the men were moving.  They were coming through
the barricades, moving slowly down the hillside.  They were
unarmoured, it was true, but ...

"Do they look half-dead to you?"

Norvel asked the translator for clarification, but none was
immediately forthcoming.  Then Lucullus elaborated.

"These men claim to have given up due to lack of water.  I would
expect them to be stumbling, falling down, looking weak and pathetic.
But these men ... are in formation!  It's a trick!"

Norvel immediately saw what Lucullus was saying.  But he did not know
what to do about it.  What threat could unarmed men be, even in
formation?  Ormod provided the answer.

"Look!  Behind them!  The fourth rank!"

Norvel stared.  And there, sure enough, darting behind the first three
ranks were glints of metal.  Metal shields, metal spears.  The first
three ranks were a decoy and behind them were coming the soldiers,
fully armed.

"Prepare to repel the assault!  Man the barricade!"

---

Agrippa had never expected to get all the way to the wall without the
deception being uncovered.  Truth be told, he had never believed they
would go for it in the first place.  But, as one of Arthur's few
confidantes in this plan from the very beginning, he knew that the
deception was just one twist within another.

Once the first spears were launched from the Saxon lines, he called
the order and the first three ranks of unarmed men turned and fled
through the formation.  The rest of the men then formed up as the
others ran back to the camp for their equipment.  The legion formed up
tightly and moved at parade-ground speed across the uneven hill.  On
his command, Agrippa's men cast their javelins in four volleys and
then charged on to the barricades.

The defences gave the Saxons an edge, no doubt about it.  So did their
numbers.  Time would tell how organization and preparation would play
out.

---

Across the valley, less than a mile away, the other two legions were
coming at the double.  Even so, the II Legion would have to hold for
over a quarter of an hour before relief arrived.

Arthur ached to commit his cavalry - they could have bounded up and
attacked in much less time - but although he had doubts about his
ability to launch a surprise attack from the rear, to have used his
cavalry at this point would have alerted them to that very real
danger.

The two legions were formed up in four divisions of five cohorts
each.  His plan was to attack from the north and try and suck in as
many of the Saxons as he could along a roughly half-mile front.  His
cavalry would be deployed to protect his wings and stop the Saxons
using their still-superior numbers to roll him up.

But first he had to make it to the battlefield.

---

On the hill, the walls of shields were kept apart by the rough
palisade of the Saxon defensive line.  As the first rank fought and
stabbed, others from behind tried to dismantle, remove, disrupt that
palisade.  In cramped and tight conditions, it was almost impossible
to move, let alone remove the tighly bedded stakes.

Over two yards apart, both sides were resorting to siege spears; the
Saxons more effective because their defensive wall more than made up
for being further down the hill.  On Agrippa's signal, the Britons
retreated five steps and took a breath.

Agrippa hoped - rather than believed - that the Saxons might be
foolish enough to abandon their defences and follow their retreat.
But it didn't happen.  Before they could have the opportunity to look
around and consider what else might be going on, Agrippa ordered his
men back to the attack.

Suddenly, just to his left, a number of stakes came flying loose and
they were quickly tumbled into the ditch.  Men charged into the
breach, forming a mini-wall inside the palisade and facing the
Saxons.  The breach was there for a moment, and then Saxons came
flying into breach, literally pushing the Britons off the wall over
the ditch by sheer force of numbers behind their shields.  Stabbing
down with their siege spears, one of the Britons' was stabbed through
the eye and fell to the ground, dying.

Suddenly a commotion from behind the Saxon lines and a giant roar went
up.  Agrippa knew what that meant!  From behind the Saxons came the
belling sound of shields crashing into each other and the sharp
cacophony of swords rattling into spears.  From the corner of his eye,
Agrippa saw a flight of javelins crash into the backs of the Saxons
opposite him.

Now more logs were coming out of the rough palisade and the trench was
filling with logs, bodies and discarded equipment.  Agrippa grabbed a
siege spear and leapt up to the Saxon side of the ditch, thrusting it
before him.  He impaled a Saxon and thrust his shield into his face to
force him out of the way.  At his back he felt others climb up beside
him and more press up to hold the breach.

As the weight of the attack grew from outside, the Saxons simply did
not have the numbers to resist every thrust from the inside wall, and
here and there up and down the line, Britons established breaches in
the Saxon defences and drove on through to do battle on equal terms.

---

The battle had descended into chaos.

In the centre, the British forces attacking both inwards and outwards
had met and split the Saxon forces in two.  But the confined space
between the palisades had allowed the Saxons to form a new defensive
wall and their numbers were superior there.

Further away, the Saxons had voluntarily spilled through their own
defences, moving around and trying to encircle the British forces
coming down the hillside.  They were slowly succeeding and the British
were inexorably being killed.

But the long front on which Arthur had chosen to attack from outside
had paid dividends: he had managed to break through at multiple points
and sucked in considerable numbers of the enemy on disadvantageous
terms.  And now he was through the palisades, the time-tested Roman
tactics of advance-open-thrust-close were reaping their heaps of
dead.  And as the battlefield started to open up, the cavalry came
into their own, charging down and mopping up loose units who had
become separated from the main body of the Saxon army.

The battle still rested on a knife-edge, but Arthur felt it was
swinging his way.

---

The bugler signalled "retreat and reform".

Arthur's troops pulled back from the defensive works, taking yet more
of it with them.  As they did so, the Saxons followed.  And when they
did, they lost the battle.

Out on the plain, with two legions in front of them and another
behind, and surrounded by cavalry, the Saxons went down to a mighty
slaughter.

As the sun set, two thousand Saxons asked for quarter and surrendered.
