&date AD410

With all the changing of emperors and sacking of Rome that was going
on, the details of how Britain was governed understandably mattered
little to those close to the Emperor Honorius.

After the demise of Constantine had caused the deposition of the governor
and prefects he had appointed and sent men scurrying to write begging
and apologetic letters to Honorius, asking for forgiveness and new
governors, word came of the sack of Rome.  Unthinkable!
Inconceivable!  How could such a thing happen?  Even London was more
secure against barbarian attack than that.

Honorius himself, of course, was not based at Rome but at the Adriatic
port city of Ravenna, in the midst of an almost impenetrable marsh.
There are those who accused him of cowardice, but a good defensive
position is a good defence, as they say.

Eventually, a response came from the Emperor to the effect that he
would not be sending any officials and that while Britain was still
nominally part of "his Empire", the lines of communication were too
long for him to effectively administer it - after all, northern Italy
and southern Gaul were swarming with barbarians right now - and that
the cities and provinces of Britain should look to their own defence
and appoint such officers and recruit such units as seemed expedient
to preserve their homes.

In other words, Britain was no longer part of the empire as far as
Honorius was concerned and could do what it liked.

Of course, the first thing it did was to call together the Council of
Britain for a lot of talking.  The promptly took all the resolutions
necessary for an effective transition of power.  Firstly, the renamed
themselves the "British Senate"; then they ordained that there would
be annual elections for two officials called Consuls; then they held
the elections with all due ceremony; also elected four Praetors to
run the main districts other than London (which was to be directly
administered by the Consuls) - based at Linnius, Gloucester and
York and the so-called "Saxon Shore", the south-east coast of the
island where the Comes - previously an imperial appointment - was in
charge of Britain's mobile forces, able to take them to any likely
point of attack at a moment's notice.

Sadly, very little of this addressed the most pressing needs, which,
as was pointed out the following spring, really were very pressing.

As had happened before, the Saxons came and attacked in numbers on the
south coast, landing in a multitude of places and driving inland,
burning and looting.  At the same time, the Picts attacked, driving
south through the border regions established by Theodosius in his time
in Britain.  And on the west coast, the Irish attacked Ordovica, coming
through Anglesey and driving down through the mountains.

Fortunately, when making all their preparations, the Council of
Britain, I mean the British Senate, had in fact done one very wise
thing.  They had elected Ambrosius Aurelianus (the father of the
later leader of the rebellion) to be Senior Consul and given him the
responsibility for the defence of the island.  He had been working
tirelessly - over significant opposition, it has to be said - to try
and raise funds, an army and prepare the defences for such an attack.
That he failed to do so says more about the difficulties of the task
than it truly does about the man himself.

The Saxons needed dealing with first.  They were in amongst us and had
easy access down good roads to the heart of the country: the rich
fields of the western lowlands.  What troops Aurelianus had raised and
half-trained he took with him and joined forces with the Comes Saxonae
and fought a dozen engagements across the south coast.  As raiders,
the Saxons were everywhere prepared to retreat rather than suffer
losses, and in those days retreat meant retreat to sea.

Aurelianus in those days developed the tactics that his son was to
deploy so successfully: the used of mounted units to outpace and
ambush the retreating Saxons.  Many of the Saxon companies were
defeated and utterly annihilated without being able to return to the
ships.  Others retreated to find that we had got in behind them and
burnt their keels to the waterline.  Many fierce battles were fought
around those ships as their rearguards desperately tried to hold our
men off.

With the Saxons defeated, Aurelianus took his units and some of the
Comes' mobile cavalry and went to reinforce the Dux York in his
defence against the Picts.  They had come in strength, driving across
the wall in the border regions to the east of the Pennines and drove
down the Via Suetonia towards York.  The fortifications of the city
were impressive, and Aurelianus had ensured that the Dux York would
hold that line until he could be reinforced and the combined forces
could roll up the Picts.  By forced marches he made it to York on the
fifteenth day of the siege and lifted it.  A bloody battle was fought
and the remnants of the Picts retreated back north of the wall.  Two
more engagements were fought before the Picts finally fled and stayed
where they belonged for two or three years.

The Irish remained the biggest menace.  Unlike the Saxons, who had
simply come to raid, or the Picts, who fought across a border that
provided a safe haven and retreat back to the deserted highlands, the
Irish came to stay and to stake out kingdoms in our land.  They had
established bases high in the craggy hills of the Ordovices, and it
would not be a simple matter to evict them.  The full might of the
Roman empire could, of course, have waited them out in siege and
stormed many of the fastnesses.  With most of the legions having left
at some point in the previous twenty years, and limited time and
financial resources, the Senate found it more expedient to apply a
policy of containment.  Aurelianus raised local levies there,
appointed towns for defence and generally tried to motivate a society
used to security and being defended by professionals to take
responsibility for their own defence.  The problem, of course, is that
it is much cheaper to pay professionals to defend you that it is to
try and make every man do two jobs: farmer by day and soldier by
night.  But in those days, there didn't seem to be the money to pay
for that "much cheaper" professional army.

There was, of course.  Like a candle burning brightly as it uses up
the last of its wax, the Roman civilization in Britain was in some
ways at its richest.  Although the markets of Europe were slowly
seizing up, at this point London was still filling ships full of
oysters from the Thames valley for transportation to Rome.  The tin
and lead being mined in the southwestern regions of Dumnonia was in
demand across Europe as far as Constantinople.  With the wars and
disruption throughout Gaul and the Spains, there was a ready market
for food - often at famine prices - wherever you could manage to ship
it.  For all their complaints, the magnates of Britain were sitting
pretty in comparison to their continental compatriots.  They were,
apart from the occasional raids, secure behind their sea walls; they
were rich and getting richer; their commercial activities were
largely undisturbed; and their government was still largely stable and
Roman.

Aurelianus' term as Consul was over, and others took their turns; as
ever through the history of the Republic, and to a lesser extent the
empire, some were more talented and some less.  Most managed to defend
the borders and repel the occasional Saxon raid.  The Picts attacked
from time to time and were generally repelled; and the Irish remained.

Britain carried on through the twilight years of the Roman Empire,
waiting for normality to return and slowly losing the capacity to
defend itself when a big shock came.
