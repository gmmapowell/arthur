&date AD455

Vortigern travelled to see Hengest on Thanet Island.

In the same way that he could not control Hengest - and could not
explain to the Senate why he could not - neither could he control his
son - or explain by messenger to Hengest why he could not.

Hengest had been livid with Vortigern about the treachery and betrayal
he felt at his hands because of what his son had been allowed to do,
and that Vortigern had seen fit to give him a burial as a hero for
doing it.

Messengers had passed backwards and forwards between London and Thanet
throughout the winter of AD454, and by June Vortigern had recognized
that the only way to resolve this argument was to go to see Hengest in
person, either to apologize or to accept that he would be held hostage
for, or killed in lieu of, blood price - the barbaric practice of valuing
life in money.

He had travelled down through the Kent roads, and then taken the track
that led to the dock where the ferry sailed across to Thanet.  He had
climbed on the boat with a heavy heart, knowing that this was the end
of Britain as he knew it, but really not quite sure why.

By the time he arrived at Hengest's headquarters, almost a year had
gone by since the great battle at Rutupiae when so many Saxons had
died - and Voritgern's son.  If nothing else, the two men had that
loss in common.

Hengest's rage had cooled in the intervening months.

"My friend, how you?" asked Ceretic, the ever-present interpreter.
"It is long time we no see."

"I am weary, my friend.  These battles between you and us, they are
pointless.  I have lost my son.  You have lost your brother.  Many
others have died.  All so unnecessary."

Ceretic translated, and then there was a pause while Hengest thought,
and then spoke.  A further pause, and chatter between Ceretic and
Hengest.  Then Ceretic translated for Vortigern.

"Ever it seems that you are unable.  You not make Senate pay us.  They
not support us.  They not give us land.  Now they kill us and you
nothing do.  How?"

Vortigern let out a deep breath.  Yes, he thought, to them with their
Lords and Kings it must seem strange, this system of ours.  And truly
it is strange that I cannot do as I wish.

"Because I have no real power.  I cannot make anybody do anything,
because I do not have an army.  The Army of the North does what it's
Dux in York tells it to do, and that is what the Senate tells him to
do.

"I can tell the Senate what needs to happen, but if they do not listen
to me, there is nothing I can do.

"I could not oppose my son, because he raised troops to fight you; I
could not fight him without troops of my own, and no Britons will
support me fighting for you against him.  Thus you must fight him
yourself.

"I buried my son with full honours because, even though I disagreed
with what he did, he _was_ my son, and he was brave and acted like a
hero.  If I had not done this, the people would have reviled me and
done it anyway.  Then yet more people would have risen up and joined
the forces which even now oppose you."

"So what can we do?" asked Ceretic for Hengest.

"I do not know," replied Vortigern.  "I cannot control my people, and
I cannot get them to agree to what is needed.  They say that I cannot
control you - which I cannot - and that they neither want you here not
are prepared to pay for you to be here to defend them against threats
that have been defanged."

"But they are wrong," pointed out Ceretic.

"I encourage you to try and tell them that yourselves, for they
certainly will not listen to me.  I have told them, I have asked them,
I have cajoled them, but they will not tolerate your presence here,
let alone as masters of Kent."

"How do you suggest we 'tell' them?  Have we not tried?"

"I have done your talking for you, until now.  Perhaps it would be
best if you sailed up to London to discuss it with them?"

"That nest of vipers?  If I sail to London, I will bring fire and burn
it.  Burn it all.  You tell them!"

"If not London, maybe elsewhere?  Camolodunum?  Verulanium?"

"In Kent, there many villas.  How about villa in Kent?"

"I could see how that would work.  Who should be at the peace
conference?"

"Peace conference?  What are these words you use?"  This directly from
Ceretic without consulting Hengest.

"Do we not need to all talk?  And sort things out?  And settle once
and for all how we will live together?  We call this peace."

"Ah, I see."  Ceretic conferred with Hengest for a long while.
Presumably they discussed the nature of this "peace" and what benefits
it could bring.

"If we are going to discuss everything, all our brethren should be
there.  And thus somewhere in the middle of the island would be good.
Let us say somewhere on the Icknield Way, near Verulanium.  We will
cross to near Camulodunum and work through to there.  Osric must be
there.  When should we meet?"

"The Kalends of October.  That should be enough time for every one to
gather together.  Until that time, we will not attack you and you will
not attack us."

"Agreed."

---

And so it was that they all gathered at Vortigern's villa outside
Verulanium on the Kalends of October.

Gaius Artorius Senior had left his son at home in Linnius with the
family of the Princeps Senatus, Merula.  Merula had said that he did
not approve of this gathering, but privately he felt Merula knew more
than he should.  It led to an air of apprehension even before he met
Ambrosius.  He and his wife had travelled the hundred and fifty miles
to Vortigern's villa.  Although she had no legal standing at the
conference, many Britons followed in the Roman tradition of deeply
respecting their wives' insights - Livia being one of the most
powerful individuals in early Imperial Rome, for example - and often
included them in informal conferences.  Many of the wives were coming
and were going to have separate "back room" meetings over the next few
days.

As the first day of the peace conference had progressed, the two sides
had felt themselves ever farther apart, and many - Ambrosius and
Artorius in particular - had felt that no resolution would be found.

They were, of course, spectacularly proved right when the treachery
erupted and the villa was attacked by hidden Saxons and Osric and
Hengest killed the majority of those in the villa - including the
women and slaves - and took Vortigern hostage.

The Saxons had never really taken the time to understand the country
in which they lived and how it operated.  They had a very primitive
view of wealth, that was not continually earned, year on year, by
planting and harvesting crops, and by the operation of industry.  When
the rebellion erupted in AD455, they thought that there was simply
wealth for the taking.

Within days of the massacre at Vortigern's villa, the whole of the
east of the island was aflame with violence.  Many towns and cities
were destroyed, and others captured, ransacked and goods and captives
carried off.  Among other cities, Camulodunum was taken by surprise
and sacked; its treasures carried off to Osric's base in Iceni.

The high water mark saw parties of Saxons raid as far west as
Gloucester and Chester before retreating to their own lands.  In
general, the walled towns - including London - held out as the news of
the rebellion outran the fact.  But in the countryside, the damage to
people, property and infrastructure was almost total.

---

There were two main long-term consequences of that night's massacre
for the Saxons.  In the first place, the hostage Vortigern agreed to
give the Saxons yet more land beyond Kent, up to the River Arun.  And
in the second, with no more ability of the British to resist, Hengest
invited all his countrymen - Angles, Jutes or Saxons - to come with
their boats and families and occupy this island, giving him support
and a power base and his people a new home.

For the British, their society crumbled and collapsed.  Although
London held, and the west was attacked, the fact remained that the
west had always been the rich part of the country and the source of
its wealth, while the areas around London were now either in enemy
hands or close to enemy territory and at continual risk.  Since most
of the surviving Senators hailed from the west, they moved their
capital there, first to Aquae Sulis and then to Glastonbury.

For some, even the west country was not far enough.  For those who
still viewed themselves as Roman first and British second, emigrating
to Gaul seemed an attractive option.  While no more secure than
Britain - after all, the Goths and Franks were there - the north-west
of Gaul, called Armorica, had managed to maintain its status as an
independent kingdom and was looking for support from solid Romans.
Many, particularly those who had lost lands in the east of Britain or
had their holdings destroyed in the Great Raids, found it easier to
emigrate to be with friends or relatives in Armorica than to tediously
build up from scratch again.

A dark night was spreading over Britain.  The break with Rome was now
complete.  Would it end in a new morning or in total destruction?
Were these the end days predicted by the Christians?
