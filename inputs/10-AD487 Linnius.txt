&date AD487

Arthur was born in Linnius and always had a special affinity for the
place.

His stepfather, Merula, took him away after his parents were killed
and the Saxons revolted, burning and looting much of the town.  There
can be little doubt that the events of those days left a scar on his
soul.

By AD487, Arthur had been leading the British resistance for over a
decade.  After the early years where he fought pitched battles in
Celidon Forest and at Portchester, the war had returned to its
previous status quo with raiding across the various borders.

In the south, most of the fighting was along the Arun, the border
between the South Saxons and the British.  London was still strongly
held.  North of London, the Angles held much of the territory of the
Iceni, and Camoldonum had been deserted by both sides as a frontier
town for many years now.  North of the Angles lay Linnius, a British
salient into the Saxon-held territories which, combined with the
estuary of the Humber just to the north kept the Angles separated from
the Jutes in the north-east of Britain.  Strategically, keeping these
three groups separated - and thus holding both London and Linnius -
was vital to the survival of the independent Republic.

More than that, Arthur was still looking for ways to concentrate and
draw out the Saxons for an open battle where he could deploy both
infantry and cavalry together, rather than depending on the mobility
of cavalry to take him across the border and back again.

Arthur was still chronically short of resources, however.  No matter
how hard he tried, the wealthy landowners of the west saw a stable
situation which was both "under control" and "far away".  Since the
shock of the rebellion in 455 - over thirty years ago now - they had
not been directly affected.

Coming up with a viable strategy under these circumstances was more
than a little taxing and Arthur was showing the strain.  As the months
and years went by, he was becoming both more irritable and more
haggard.  Although he still had in his mind "the grand plan", he felt
no closer to it happening than when he had first thought of it.  It
was all very well to know how to destroy the enemy if they were to
come out and fight you, but another to figure out how to get them to
do it.

The front between Linnius and the Angles - which had been stable for
almost a decade - seemed to Arthur to offer the best prospects for
disrupting the current stalemate.

As the spring campaigning season had approached, he had summoned
various leaders and counsellors from around the country.  The Comes of
Linnius was there, as was the Dux from the main northern army, holding
back both the Picts and contaning the northern Jutes.  Merula was
there, aging gracefully - how old was he now, thought Arthur - as well
as loyal retainers such as Quintus.

"If we are going to beat the Saxons - truly beat them, not just
contain them within the lands they already have, but drive them
backwards and into or across the sea - we need to shake things up.
These raids of ours barely touch them; they retaliate and we turn the
border into a ten-mile wide strip of wasteland.  We need to fight the
Saxons, not burn out British peasants who have the misfortune to live
under or near the Saxons."

"Do you have a plan?"

"No, not as such.  But I feel they will only come out - and in numbers
- if they feel there is something in it for them.  We have to offer
them a prize that would be worth taking - and thus worth taking a
risk.  But then we have to make sure that we do not actually lose that
prize."

"Did you have something in mind?" from the Comes of Linnius.

"As it happens, I did," replied Arthur.  "Although it may not be much
to your liking.  I propose that we put our continued holding of
Linnius into play."

Aside from some audible intakes of breath, a stony silence greeted
this.

"You do, understand, Arthur, that Linnius is the cornerstone of our
defence.  Lose that, and the country must inevitably fall."

"Yes.  But unless we do something daring, something dramatic - and
something soon - I think the country must inevitably fall anyway.  Or
be ruined.  Or destroyed.  I do not feel we can keep this up forever."

"What do you have in mind?"

"Removing the forces from Linnius and taking them somewhere else.
Allowing Linnius to thus be exposed to attack.  And then somehow
rescuing it with the destruction of all the encircling Saxons.  I
think the key would be misdirection as to where all our forces were.
Specifically, we would either need to move our forces in the north
southwards to attack Linnius, or bring my forces up from Badonicus
when the Saxons believed that we were still on the Arun."

"Either plan is fraught with danger.  What if they were to learn where
our real weakness lies?"

"Our real weakness is that they can outwait us.  The more time passes,
the more barbarians come to our shores and are given refuge.  The more
Saxons, the more warriors we face.  True, they have no cavalry - yet -
but sooner or later they will have force enough to come west and to
take our western cities and, once encircled, we lose Linnius and all
the rest.  We could possibly hold the western highlands - possibly
even the central highlands - but we would have lost.

"It is possible that they could outfox us with whatever plan we
devise.  But - if you will forgive the presumption - I honestly
believe they have no leaders to compare to me.  We have the knowledge
and experience of a thousand years of generals passed down to us.
Merula will happily discourse over dinner on the relative merits of
Alexander in the East or Caesar in the West, but we know of both and
can read their accounts.  We read also the Peloponesian War, of
Hannibal and Carthage; we have Rutilius' manual of war derived from
the Great Man, Marius, himself.  We all learnt to recite the summary
of Sulla's campaigns.  What is there to generalling that we do not
know?  And, on the other hand, what _do_ they know?  They know how to
fight and how to sail.  I say to you that if we stay off the water, we
_will_ beat them in any fair battle.  It is just a question of
_bringing_ them to battle."

The council was quiet for a while.  Then Merula spoke.

"What have we not tried?"

A silence fell again.  Most of the councillors waited for him to say
more, assuming the question to be rhetorical.  Arthur, who knew
better, knew that Merula would not supply the answer, but knew that
there was only one answer - or at least, only one answer that fit the
context of the question.  He thought hard: what _had_ they not tried?

What was there to try?  A shipborne attack?  They certainly hadn't
tried that, but since that was tantamount to suicide, he had to assume
that that was not what Merula had in mind.

An infantry assault?  They hadn't tried it, although Arthur would
dearly have loved to, for the simple reason that they didn't have the
kind of infantry numbers that such an assault would take.  Their
forces were much more of a mobile strike force.

Taking one of their strongholds?  Something about that resonated for
Arthur.  They hadn't tried it for a couple of reasons: they didn't
really have the technology these days, and they didn't have the
manpower to make it happen and to defend themselves while in enemy
territory.  And there it was: both of those were reasons not to do
that _if your goal was to succeed_.  But if your goal was to trick the
enemy into believing that that was your goal when your actual goal was
something else ...  As if by magic, the plan unfurled in his mind,
reaching out over the coming weeks and months until ... ah, _that_ is
how they would defeat the Saxons.

He nearly cried out loud for joy.  In a moment, all of the years of
frustration felt wiped away.  In gratuitous detail, he saw what was
going to come and how he would manage it all.  Now it was just a
question of getting everybody to play their part - including the
Saxons.  And that depended on him giving out the right, careful,
drip-drip-drip of information over the next few years as his plan
reached fruition.

And he owed it all to a simple question from Merula.

He looked around, saw the confusion on the others' faces.  How _could_
they be so ignorant?  They had the same resources as he did, the same
experiences, the same knowledge of the world situation.  How was it
possible for them to fail to see that which was so obvious to him?

Patiently, he approached opening up the conversation, trying to work
as Merula would, asking rather than answering.  But to fit the
contract, he needed to first answer Merula's question, but making it
sound tentative, not absolute.

"We have not tried a siege.  We could invade their territory and try
and take one of their forts."

"Do they have such things?" asked Quintus.  "I'm familiar with the
border fortifications, but actual forts?  I don't think I've seen
one."

"Indeed they do," replied the Comes.  We have raided through to one
not far from Linnius.  They defend it with some strength and use it as
a base for bringing in their tribute.  They hold grain there against
our raids."

"Which would make it particularly difficult to take by siege," mused
Arthur aloud, but internally thinking, _a few miles from Linnius
... perfect_.

"I thought the siege was just meant to be a distraction," croaked
Merula.

Arthur looked at him intensely.  A wry smile was on Merula's lips and
as their eyes met a thought appeared directly in Arthur's brain: "Well
done, Arthur.  I knew you could figure this out.  Don't mind me; I'm
just playing along with you."

Arthur glanced away and looked at Quintus.

"It would be a good location, Arthur," he began.  "If we were still
relatively near the border with Linnius, then we would have a good
supply route from there."

"One that the Saxons would dearly like to interrupt," growled
Fulvius.  "They could do that by attacking Linnius."

"And they would be successful if we had removed all the defensive
forces from Linnius," added the Comes.

"And they would fail if they merely _thought_ that," said Arthur, "but
in reality we had enough men there to act as the vice when the rest of
our forces attacked as the hammer."

Yes, thought Arthur, we have a plan.

---

Arthur moved himself and two units of his forces to Linnius in early
May and began the detailed planning for the operation.  Three times
they deliberately planned raids across the border that went near the
fort, and the third time dropped Tiberius Gallus off in a wood about a
mile from the fort.  A brave and loyal member of Arthur's personal
forces, he had offered to stay in the area for a few nights and do
reconnaissance of who came and went; how many men were in the area,
and then slip back to Linnius when the opportunity presented itself.
For this purpose, he had "disguised" himself as a British peasant.  He
would not, obviously, be able to pretend that he lived here if anybody
investigated closely - none of the locals would know him - but to any
Saxons in the neighbourhood he would just be another faceless peasant
in the fields.

It could not have escaped the notice of the Saxons that there was
increased activity in this sector, but given that both sides were just
awakening from the slumbers of a hard winter in eastern Britain, the
difference from last year was probably not as significant as the fact
that the raids had started up again.

Gallus returned to Linnius almost a week later, but with a story to
tell.  Arthur called together the inner war council and asked him for
the details.

"Well, I hid out in the woods for that first day and into the night.
I was trying to see if I could tell what the lie of the land was
before risking myself to casual observation.

"But the following morning, I set out and before long I'd come across
a group of Britons ploughing a field.  I said I was a wanderer looking
to make it out of Saxon territory, and gave them the impression that
maybe I was an outlaw of some kind.  They let me into their party and
for that day I helped them with their farming and at the end of the
day the head man took me back to his house and fed me.

"By that time I had - foolishly or not, I cannot tell - decided he was
worthy of trust and told him a little more about what I was doing
there.  Not the whole plan; fear not.  But I told him that I knew some
people on the British side of the border and was trying to help them
to more effectively raid the Saxons and liberate the British.  Our
raids, are, of course, not wildly popular among the men that suffer
from them, but there are still some of the older men there who
remember what liberty was and I was fortunate to have found such a
one.  He agreed to help me.

"I worked a couple more days in the fields, and then they said it was
market day.  They didn't have much, but they gathered together a few
chickens and some eggs, and the head man and I went together to the
Saxon fort.  The market was held inside in the central square.  You
can be sure that I had a good look around while I was there.

"It looks on the inside much the way that it would seem to from the
outside.  The basic fortification is the ten-foot-high wall you can
see from the outside, and on the inside there's the five-foot-high
fighting platform that you'd expect.  Around the inside, there are
huts for the defenders - I'd say the fort has about forty men that live
there permanently - and others for storage, hard to tell how much.
And in the centre of the fort is the square where they were holding
the market, but it could also be used for assembling troops, drill (if
they do that) or other practice, or for getting ready to sally out of
the gates.

"The gates are solid and thick and well reinforced.  There's a second
gate inside the fighting platform that's reinforced with metal.  You
would be very lucky indeed to be able to get through that.  If you did
want to storm it, I would suggest going over the top.  It wouldn't be
easy, and they've built an artificial hill so that it's about five or
six feet up to the front gate.  Not too hard to get to if you're on
the path, but you wouldn't want to be doing it with missiles coming
down on your head."

"Very good, Gallus.  Questions anybody?"

"Just one," said Quintus.  "When do we begin?"

---

To a casual observer, it might have been just another raid.  But they
came in strength and numbers, and with some unusual equipment.  First,
they took and held the ford over the Dubglas and the Saxon
fortifications on the far side.  This in itself was unusual, along
with the cost in lives on both sides: an attacking force of over 200
had lost ten men but had taken the defensive position and killed its
twelve defenders.  Fifty men stayed there the rest of the day
fortifying the positions on both sides of the river and connecting
them with a primitive log bridge for quicker transit.

Once the ford was secure, a heavy baggage train, complete with
artillery and siege engines, crossed the ford and wound through the
woods on the Saxon side.  None knew exactly where the alert would be
raised; they had been careful to make sure none of the Saxons at the
river could escape, but the noises of battle were unmistakable and
travelled a long way on warm spring days; possibly even as far as the
fort.

As ever, Arthur regretted his lack of manpower.  He would have liked
to copy Caesar's exploits in northern Gaul and hack down all the trees
on a ten-man front, build a road along it  and use the logs to build
barriers to protect the sides from attack.  Aside from the practical
aspects of protection and speed of communications, such a strategy
made a statement about the sheer _power_ of a Roman army, even over
the forces of nature.  To an enemy as superstitious as these Saxons,
that would seem to chip away at the very power of the gods themselves.

Between Gallus' detailed reconnaissance and his own impressions of the
area from horseback, Arthur knew just where he wanted to go and how to
get there.  He led the column and brought it down into the valley in
front of the fort.  Any potential element of surprise was now gone.
Ahead, he could see the defenders on the walls of the fort, with the
outer door securely closed.  Arthur had no doubts that everybody in
the fort was on a war footing and ready to repel any assault.  None of
this, of course, came as any surprise.  Arthur would have stormed the
fort had they given him the opportunity, but he never expected it.

Instead, they settled down in encampments around the fort.  Given a
couple of legions, he would have circumvallated this place in a day
and gone into proper camps.  Of course, given two legions he would
probably just have formed a _testudo_ with ten cohorts, supported by
artillery fire, marched up to the front gate and battered it in while
the defenders did their best.  With 150 men, that would be suicide.

Still, he did what he could with the available men.  Each camp of
fifty was responsible for building a partial wall enclosing the fort
and for installing its artillery securely.  _This needs to look good_
Arthur thought, even if it stands no chance of success.

A general can only control part of any battle.  And Arthur knew well
that the Saxons had a number of different strategies they could choose
to retaliate.  He knew what he wanted them to do: assume that he had
brought all his forces here and counter-attack behind him, using more
of their own forces to try and take Linnius.  But they could reinforce
and attack him here, where he would be at something of a disadvantage,
but was why he had made his men dig in so well.  Or, they could just
ignore him altogether and hope that the fort could hold out, and just
simply give up on it altogether if he could outlast them.  That, he
reflected, would be the _right_ thing to do unless they had unlimited
manpower.  Realistically, this location had no strategic significance
whatsoever, since it was on the wrong side of the river.  If they
couldn't hold it from a position of strength over the summer, he
wouldn't be able to hold it with almost no preparation over the
winter, and surely that would be obvious to them, too.

But somehow, he knew deep inside that they didn't see that.
